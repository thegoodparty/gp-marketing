# Maryland: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Maryland so you do not have to research the same
ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which offices
are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race data, which
always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean. Every claim below has a verbatim quote
in [`research/maryland.json`](./research/maryland.json).

**The one thing to know first:** in Maryland, counties are the main local
government, and nearly all county offices are elected together once every four
years, in the governor's election year, after a closed June party primary.
School board races are the exception: they are nonpartisan, and every voter
can vote in the school board primary. Cities and towns run their own elections
under their own charters, outside state election law, except Baltimore City,
which is a county-level city with partisan elections in presidential years.

## Legislative calendar

Next regular session: convenes 13 January 2027 (the second Wednesday of
January, every year, Md. Const. art. III, § 14). The session lasts up to 90
consecutive days, so it ends by 12 April 2027 unless three-fifths of each
house extend it by up to 30 days (art. III, § 15). A special session called by
the governor is limited to 30 days.

During the session the governor has six days, Sundays excepted, to act on a
bill. A bill presented in the last six days or after adjournment becomes law
unless the governor vetoes it within 30 days of being presented (art. II,
§ 17(b)-(c)), so late bills are settled by about late May. No law takes effect
before 1 June after the session unless it is an emergency law passed by
three-fifths (art. XVI, § 2). Most acts set a later date, often 1 October
(`usual case`).

## How elections work in Maryland

Safe on any Maryland page, at the tier named.

### Election days

**County offices** are elected at the November general election (the Tuesday
after the first Monday) every four years, in the governor's election year
(counting from 1926), and serve four-year terms. That covers county councils,
county executives, county commissioners, sheriffs, state's attorneys, circuit
court clerks, registers of wills and orphans' court judges.

*Md. Const. art. XVII, §§ 2, 3 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** this four-year rule does not apply to elected school boards or
to Cecil County's commissioners, and Baltimore City's own offices are on the
presidential cycle (below).

*Md. Const. art. XVII, § 7; Md. Code, Elec. Law § 8-201(b) · statutory · read in statute*

**School boards** are elected in 20 counties, and are part elected, part
appointed in Baltimore City, Baltimore County, Caroline and Harford. School
board elections are held at the state primary and general elections.

*Md. Code, Educ. § 3-114 · statutory · read in statute*

**Cities and towns** hold their elections on their own schedules, under their
own charters. State election law does not cover them, except in Baltimore City.
Never give a Maryland municipality's election date unless race data shows it.

*Md. Code, Elec. Law § 1-101; Local Gov't § 4-101(c) · varies by place · read in statute*

### Primary type

The state primary is the fourth Tuesday in June in the governor's election
year, and the fourth Tuesday in April in presidential years. Principal parties
must nominate by primary. Primaries are **closed**: only voters registered with
a party vote in its primary, unless that party has chosen to let unaffiliated
voters in and told the State Board in advance.

*Md. Code, Elec. Law §§ 8-201(a), 8-202(a), (c) · statutory · read in statute*

**School board primaries are open to everyone.** Any registered voter in the
county may vote in a school board primary, whatever their party, and the top
twice-as-many-as-seats candidates go on to November.

*Md. Code, Elec. Law §§ 8-802(a), 8-804(c)(1) · statutory · read in statute*

### Partisanship

**County races are partisan.** Party candidates are nominated in the closed
primary.

*Md. Code, Elec. Law § 8-202 · statutory · read in statute*

**School board races are nonpartisan.** Candidates file, appear on the ballot
and are elected without party labels.

*Md. Code, Elec. Law § 8-802(a) · statutory · read in statute*

**City and town races** follow each charter. Most are run by the municipality
itself. Avoid calling a Maryland city or town race partisan or nonpartisan
unless race data says which.

*Md. Const. art. XI-E, § 3 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** "local races are nonpartisan" is wrong on every Maryland county
page. "Local races are partisan" is wrong for every school board.

### Winning an election

**Plurality**: the candidate with the most votes wins. A tie for the most votes
means a new election ordered by the governor. No runoff was found in the law.

*Md. Code, Elec. Law § 11-503(a)(2); Md. Const. art. IV, § 25, art. XVII, § 8 · statutory · read in statute*

### Running as an independent

A candidate **not affiliated with any party** may be nominated by petition,
signed by registered voters eligible to vote for that office. A voter
affiliated with a party cannot use this route. It does not apply to
nonpartisan school board races, where every candidate files directly.

*Md. Code, Elec. Law § 5-703(a)-(b) · statutory · read in statute*

## County government

Our data has a page for each of Maryland's 23 counties and one for Baltimore
City, which acts as a county. Maryland law sorts counties into three types,
which decides who governs:

- **Charter counties** (home rule under Article XI-A): an elected **county
  council**, and an elected **county executive** if the charter provides one.
- **Code counties** (home rule under Article XI-F): **county commissioners**,
  who can pass the county's own local laws.
- **Commission counties** (neither): **county commissioners**, elected by
  district, at large or both as the General Assembly sets for that county.

*Md. Code, Local Gov't § 1-101(b)-(f); Md. Const. art. XI-A, § 3, art. VII, § 1, art. XI-F, § 3 · statutory · read in statute*

Which type each county is changes when voters adopt a charter, so take the
governing body from race data. Ten of our county-level pages carry a county
executive or mayor race (including Baltimore County, Montgomery, Prince
George's, Anne Arundel, Howard and Frederick). Avoid describing county
commissioners on those pages.

Every county and Baltimore City also elects a **sheriff**, a **state's
attorney**, a **clerk of the circuit court** and a **register of wills**, each
for four years. Every county except Howard, Montgomery and Harford elects three
**orphans' court judges**, who handle wills and estates.

*Md. Const. art. IV, §§ 25, 40(a), 41, 44, art. V, § 7 · statutory · read in statute*

Our labels call the state's attorney "district attorney" and the orphans' court
judge "probate judge". In copy, say state's attorney and orphans' court judge.

### Home rule and charters

A county moves to charter home rule when its voters elect a charter board, which
drafts a charter for a public vote. **Does home rule reach elections?** The
timing does not: county offices stay on the four-year state cycle, and state
election law runs county elections. A charter decides the county council's size,
its districts and whether there is an elected executive.

*Md. Const. art. XI-A, §§ 1, 3, art. XVII, § 2 · statutory · read in statute*

### No townships

No Maryland law read for this doc creates townships, and our data has no
Maryland township pages. Below the county, the general local governments are
municipalities.

## City and town government

Maryland has **one class of municipality**, whatever it is called (city, town
or village). Every municipality has charter home rule: it adopts and amends its
own charter, by resolution of its council or a petition of 5% of its voters,
and the General Assembly may act on municipalities only by general laws.

*Md. Code, Local Gov't § 4-102; Md. Const. art. XI-E, §§ 1, 3, 4 · statutory · read in statute*

**Does home rule reach elections?** Yes. The charter sets who may vote, when
elections are held, the offices, the terms and whether races carry party
labels. A county council cannot legislate for a municipality on matters its
charter covers. Take every city and town's offices, dates and terms from race
data.

*Md. Code, Local Gov't § 4-101(c); Md. Const. art. XI-A, § 3 · varies by place · read in statute*

## Writing rules

These are the ways Maryland copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never infer a county from a place name.** Somerset, the town, is in
   Montgomery County (`md/montgomery-county/somerset`), not Somerset County.
   The full list is in
   [`research/places-named-after-another-county.json`](./research/places-named-after-another-county.json).
   Always take the county from the page data.
2. **Use the right calendar for the tier.** County offices: November of the
   governor's election year, every four years. School boards: the state primary
   and general election. Cities and towns: their own charters. Baltimore City:
   presidential years.
3. **Do not call local races nonpartisan or partisan without naming the tier.**
   County races are partisan, school board races are nonpartisan, and city
   races depend on the charter.
4. Call Maryland primaries closed, but do not say unaffiliated voters can never
   vote in them: a party may open its primary, and everyone votes in the school
   board primary.
5. Do not mention runoffs. Plurality wins.
6. Do not describe county commissioners in a county with an elected executive.
   Say state's attorney and orphans' court judge.
7. Do not describe an orphans' court election in Howard, Montgomery or Harford
   County.
8. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
9. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
   contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.
