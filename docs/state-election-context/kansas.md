# Kansas: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Kansas so you do not have to research the same ground
on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which offices are
actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race data, which
always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean. Every claim below has a verbatim quote
in [`research/kansas.json`](./research/kansas.json).

**The one thing to know first:** Kansas runs two election calendars. County and
township offices are elected in November of even-numbered years, with party
primaries in August. City, school board and most special district offices are
elected in November of odd-numbered years. And partisanship splits by type of
government: county and township races are partisan, school board races are
always nonpartisan, and each city's governing body decides for its own races.
Two-thirds of our Kansas place pages are townships, so the even-year partisan
calendar is the right one for most of them.

## Legislative calendar

Next regular session: convenes 11 January 2027 (the second Monday in January,
every year, Kan. Const. art. 2, § 8). Sessions in even-numbered years are limited
to 90 calendar days unless two-thirds of each house vote to extend. Odd-year
sessions have no fixed limit. Bills reach the governor within ten days of
passage, and a bill the governor neither signs nor vetoes within ten calendar
days becomes law (Kan. Const. art. 2, § 14(a)), so the last bills are settled
about three weeks after the session's final votes. The governor can also call a
special session (Kan. Const. art. 1, § 5).

New laws usually take effect when the session laws are published, which is
1 July when the volume is ready by then, unless the act sets another date
(K.S.A. 45-310, 45-311).

## How elections work in Kansas

Safe on any Kansas page, at the tier named.

### Election days

**County and township** offices are on the even-year cycle: party primaries on
the first Tuesday of August in even-numbered years, and the general election on
the Tuesday after the first Monday in November.

*K.S.A. 25-203, 25-2102(a) · statutory · read in statute*

**Townships** elect in November of even-numbered years. The trustee and
treasurer are elected together for four-year terms, and the clerk for four years
on the alternate cycle, so every township has an election every two years.
"City townships" are the exception and hold no township election.

*K.S.A. 25-1601 · statutory · read in statute*

**Cities, school districts and most special districts** are on the odd-year
cycle. Since 2017 their primaries are the first Tuesday in August of
odd-numbered years and their general elections the Tuesday after the first
Monday in November. This covers cities, consolidated city-counties, school
districts, community colleges, and drainage, extension, irrigation, improvement,
water and hospital districts. Special districts that elect their board at a
meeting of the district are not covered.

*K.S.A. 25-21a01(a), (g) · statutory · read in statute*

Cities and hospital districts may also hold elections in even-numbered years to
stagger terms or use three-year terms, and each one sets its own terms by
ordinance or resolution. So some Kansas cities have races on the November
ballot in even years too.

*K.S.A. 25-21a01(c), (h); 25-2107(a) · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** before 2015 Kansas cities and school boards voted in April. Some
older city statutes still name April (K.S.A. 14-201 does), but the 2015 law
moved every city and school election to November and voided old local
ordinances on election timing. Never say a Kansas city or school board votes in
April or in the spring.

*K.S.A. 14-201, 12-184a(b), 25-21a01(a) · statutory · read in statute*

**School boards** are elected in November of odd-numbered years. A unified school
district board usually has seven members serving four-year terms, elected at
large, by member district, or by a combination the district has chosen.

*K.S.A. 25-2010, 72-1072 · usual case · read in statute*

**City and school primaries happen only when a race is crowded.** For a district
seat, there is a primary only if more than three candidates file. For at-large
seats, only if candidates outnumber seats by more than three to one. Otherwise
every candidate goes straight to the November ballot.

*K.S.A. 25-2108a(b)-(c), 25-2021(a) · statutory · read in statute*

### Primary type

Kansas has party registration, and its partisan primaries are closed by
default. A voter registered with a party votes in that party's primary. Each
party decides by 15 January of an election year whether unaffiliated voters may
vote in its primary. An unaffiliated voter can declare a party at the polls and
receive that party's ballot, and that declaration becomes their registered
affiliation. Voters cannot change party between the candidate filing deadline
and certification of the primary results.

*K.S.A. 25-3301(a), (c), 25-3304(b) · statutory · read in statute*

Parties whose candidate for governor polled under 5% at the last election
nominate by convention instead of primary.

*K.S.A. 25-202(b) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not call Kansas primaries open. Do not say unaffiliated voters
can never vote in them either: a party may allow it, and any unaffiliated voter
may join a party at the polls.

### Partisanship

**County and township races are partisan.** Candidates are nominated by party
primary or by independent petition, and run with a party label in November.

*K.S.A. 25-202(a) · statutory · read in statute*

**School board races are always nonpartisan.** No local choice changes this.

*K.S.A. 25-2009(a) · statutory · read in statute*

**City races are partisan or nonpartisan as each city's governing body
decides.** The state sets no default. Never call a Kansas city race partisan or
nonpartisan unless race data says which.

*K.S.A. 25-2113 · varies by place · read in statute*

In Wyandotte County, the consolidation plan for the Unified Government decides
whether its governing body is elected on a partisan or nonpartisan basis.

*K.S.A. 12-344(c)(2) · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** "local races are nonpartisan" is wrong on most Kansas pages. It is
wrong for every county and every township, and not reliable for any one city.

### Winning an election

Plurality: the candidate with the most votes wins, unless a law expressly says
otherwise. None of the local election laws read for this doc provides a runoff.
The August primaries above narrow the field instead.

*K.S.A. 25-702(a) · statutory · read in statute*

### Running as an independent

For **county and township** office, a candidate runs either in a party primary
or by independent nomination petition, not both in the same cycle. A voter who
is registered with a party cannot accept an independent nomination. Petition
signers must live in the county and election district of the office, and may not
sign for more than one candidate for the same office.

*K.S.A. 25-202(c), 25-303(b), (e), (f), (h) · statutory · read in statute*

For **city and school** office, the independent-nomination law does not apply.
A candidate files a declaration of
intent with the county election officer and pays a fee, or files a nomination
petition. Each city sets its own petition requirement by ordinance.

*K.S.A. 25-303(a), 25-21a01(f), 25-2110(b) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** the county election officer, not the city, runs every city
election. For a city in more than one county, it is the officer of the county
holding most of the city's people.

*K.S.A. 25-2110(e) · statutory · read in statute*

## County government

Kansas has 105 counties (our data has a page for each). Each is governed by a
**board of county commissioners** of three, five or seven members. Each
commissioner lives in and represents a commissioner district, and terms are four
years, staggered so that no more than a bare majority is elected at once.
Changing the number of commissioners takes a vote of the county's residents.

*K.S.A. 19-202(a)-(d), 19-204(c) · usual case · read in statute*

Sherman County may elect its commissioners at large.

*K.S.A. 19-204b · varies by place · read in statute*

Counties also elect a **county clerk, county treasurer, register of deeds,
county attorney and sheriff**, each for four years, all partisan.

*K.S.A. 19-301, 19-501, 19-1201, 19-701, 19-801a · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** not every county elects a sheriff. The constitution requires one
except in a county that abolished the office before 2022 under a consolidated
law enforcement act. Our Riley County page has no sheriff race, which fits
this. Never write that every Kansas county elects a sheriff.

*Kan. Const. art. 9, § 2(a)-(b); K.S.A. 19-801a · statutory · read in statute*

### Home rule and charters

Every Kansas county has statutory home rule, exercised by resolution of the
county commission. But counties stay subject to every state law on elections,
election officers and the election of county officers. So timing, partisanship
and nominations are flat-assertable for every county, home rule or not.

*K.S.A. 19-101a(a)(6) · statutory · read in statute*

Only counties the state has designated urban areas may adopt a **charter**, and
those are Johnson and Sedgwick. A charter may change the number, terms and
election method of the commissioners, and may make elected county offices
appointive or abolish them. Our Johnson County page has no clerk, treasurer or
register of deeds race, which fits this. On Johnson and Sedgwick pages, take the
county's offices from race data and do not describe the general structure above.

*K.S.A. 19-2654, 19-2680(a), (e) · varies by place · read in statute*

### Townships

Kansas townships are a real, elected layer of government below the county.
Every township except a city township elects a **trustee, treasurer and clerk**,
each for four years, on the partisan even-year ballot.

*K.S.A. 25-1601, 25-202(a) · statutory · read in statute*

A city of the second or first class is not part of any township: when a city
becomes second class, its land leaves the township. So a township and a city
with the same name are two separate governments with separate elections.

*K.S.A. 14-101(d) · statutory · read in statute*

## City government

Kansas has three classes of city, set by population. "City" is the only name:
Kansas law has no towns or villages as a separate type.

*Kan. Const. art. 12, § 5(b); K.S.A. 13-101, 14-101, 15-101 · statutory · read in statute*

- **First class:** over 15,000 people. A city between 15,000 and 25,000 may
  choose to stay second class.
- **Second class:** over 2,000 and under 15,000. A city between 2,000 and 5,000
  may choose to stay third class.
- **Third class:** 2,000 or fewer, plus county seats that were unincorporated
  towns, whatever their size.

*K.S.A. 13-101, 14-101(a), 15-101 · statutory · read in statute*

**Class does not follow population automatically.** A city moves up only when it
certifies its population and the governor proclaims the change. A city that
shrinks keeps its class, unless a second-class city falls to 1,000 or fewer and
its residents vote to go back to third class. Never infer a Kansas city's class
from its population.

*K.S.A. 13-101, 14-901 · statutory · read in statute*

### What cities elect

The default structures in state law:

- **Third class:** a mayor and five council members, two-year terms.
- **Second class:** a mayor, council members and a city treasurer, two-year terms.

*K.S.A. 15-201, 14-201 · usual case · read in statute*

These defaults rarely hold as written. A city may adopt or abandon a form of
government (mayor-council, commission, manager, or the modified mayor-council
form with a mayor, three at-large and four district council members) by a vote
of its residents, set its own terms by ordinance, and change much else by
charter ordinance. Take each city's offices and terms from race data.

*K.S.A. 12-184(b), 12-10a02, 25-21a01(c) · varies by place · read in statute*

### Home rule

Every Kansas city has constitutional home rule over its local affairs, whatever
its class, and can exempt itself by **charter ordinance** from state laws that
do not apply uniformly to all cities. There is no population threshold.

*Kan. Const. art. 12, § 5(b)-(c) · statutory · read in statute*

**Does home rule reach elections?** For timing, no. A charter ordinance cannot
exempt a city from laws that apply uniformly to every city, and the 2015 timing
law applies to every city. For partisanship, the city decides anyway: state law
hands that choice to each city's governing body.

*Kan. Const. art. 12, § 5(c)(1); K.S.A. 25-21a01, 25-2113 · statutory · read in statute*

### Wichita

`ks/sedgwick-county/wichita`

In Sedgwick County, not in Sedgwick, the city, which is in
Harvey County. Sedgwick County is one of the two counties allowed a charter, so
check its county offices against race data.

*K.S.A. 19-2654(b) · statutory · read in statute*

### Kansas City and Wyandotte County

`ks/wyandotte-county`, `ks/wyandotte-county/kansas-city`

Kansas City, Kansas, and Wyandotte County are consolidated as the Unified
Government, headed by a chief executive who is also mayor. Its governing body
serves the whole county, but Bonner Springs, Edwardsville and Lake Quivira
remain separate cities. The consolidation plan, not the general county rules,
sets the governing body, its districts and whether its races are partisan.

*K.S.A. 12-340, 12-344(c), 12-345(h) · statutory · read in statute*

Do not describe a three-to-seven-member county commission or a separate Kansas
City mayor and council on these pages.

## Writing rules

These are the ways Kansas copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never infer a county from a place name.** 15 of our Kansas place pages share
   a name with a different Kansas county. Wichita is in Sedgwick County, while
   the city of Sedgwick is in Harvey County. Ottawa is in Franklin County,
   Shawnee is in Johnson County, Cherokee is in Crawford County. The full list
   is in
   [`research/places-named-after-another-county.json`](./research/places-named-after-another-county.json).
   Always take the county from the page data.
2. **Never decide from the slug alone whether a page is a city or a township.**
   197 of our Kansas place names appear twice in one county, almost always a
   city and its township ("Kingman" and "Kingman Township"). They are separate
   governments with different offices and different election years. Decide from
   the offices on the page: trustee, treasurer and clerk mean a township.
3. **Use the right calendar for the tier.** Townships and counties: November of
   even-numbered years, partisan. Cities and school boards: November of
   odd-numbered years. Never say April or spring.
4. **Do not call local races nonpartisan without naming the tier.** County and
   township races are partisan, school board races never are, and city races
   depend on the city.
5. Do not call Kansas primaries open.
6. Do not mention runoffs. Plurality wins, and no local runoff was found in
   the law.
7. Do not infer a city's class from its population, or call any Kansas place a
   town or village as a legal type.
8. Do not say every county elects a sheriff, or describe a standard county
   commission for Wyandotte, Johnson or Sedgwick County.
9. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
10. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
    contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.
