# South Dakota: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across South Dakota so you do not have to research the same
ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which
offices are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race
data, which always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean.

**The one thing to know first:** most South Dakota location pages are not cities.
Of our 985 municipal pages, 909 are **civil townships**, which elect their
officers at an annual township meeting in March, not at a polling-place election.
County races are partisan. City, town, school board and township races carry no
party label. And the city and school election calendar changed on 1 January 2026:
each city and school board now picks June or November, so older guides that give
a different month are out of date.

## Legislative calendar

The Legislature meets every year. It next convenes on 12 January 2027 (the second
Tuesday of January, S.D. Const. art. III, § 7), and a regular session lasts at
most forty legislative days (S.D. Const. art. III, § 6), so it usually ends in
March. Once the Legislature adjourns or recesses for more than five days, the
governor has fifteen days to act on bills presented in the last five days
(S.D. Const. art. IV, § 4), so the session's bills are settled by about the end
of March. A law passed at a regular session usually takes effect on 1 July
(SDCL 2-14-16), and never sooner than ninety days after adjournment unless it is
passed as an emergency (S.D. Const. art. III, § 22).

Re-check this doc: late April 2027. Last researched: October 2026.

## How elections work in South Dakota

Read this by tier. Timing and partisanship differ for counties, cities and towns,
school districts and townships.

### Election days

**County: even years.** The primary is the first Tuesday after the first Monday in
**June** of even-numbered years, and the general election is the first Tuesday
after the first Monday in **November** of even-numbered years. County officers are
elected at the general election.

*SDCL 12-2-1, 12-2-2 · statutory · read in statute*

**Cities and towns: every year, in June or November.** Every municipality holds a
regular election each year on the first Tuesday after the first Monday in either
**June** or **November**. Its governing body picks which by 14 January of the
election year. In even-numbered years the city election must run alongside the
June primary or the November general election. A city may also choose, by
ordinance, to hold its regular election only in even-numbered years.

*SDCL 9-13-1, 9-13-1.3, 9-13-37 · statutory · read in statute*

**School districts: every year, in June or November.** Each school district holds
an annual election on the first Tuesday after the first Monday in June or
November, chosen by the school board early in the year. In even-numbered years
the school board election runs alongside the June primary or the November general
election. In odd-numbered years a city and its school district may hold a joint
election.

*SDCL 13-7-10, 13-7-10.1, 13-7-10.3, 9-13-1.1 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never write "South Dakota city elections are in June" or "in
November". Each city and school board picks, and it can differ between a city and
its own school district. Take the month from our race data. These sections were
rewritten in the 2025 session, effective 1 January 2026, and amended again in 2026.

**Uncontested races may not be on a ballot at all.** If no city race is contested
and no question is on the ballot, the city holds no election and the unopposed
candidates are simply certified. School districts work the same way.

*SDCL 9-13-5, 13-7-9 · statutory · read in statute*

**Civil townships: the annual township meeting.** Township voters assemble every
year on the **first Tuesday of March** for the annual township meeting, where
they elect their officers by ballot.

*SDCL 8-3-1, 8-3-15 · statutory · read in statute*

**Special districts.** Conservation district supervisors are elected at the
November general election on the nonpolitical ballot, after being nominated by
petition.

*SDCL 38-8-39 · statutory · read in statute*

### Primary type

South Dakota runs **closed primaries**. Only voters registered with a party may
vote that party's primary ballot, and a party candidate must be registered with
that party. A party may choose, in its own rules, to let voters registered with no
party affiliation vote in its primary.

*SDCL 12-6-26, 12-6-3.2 · statutory · read in statute*

For our audience: an independent voter cannot take a party ballot unless that
party has opened its primary to them, and that can change from year to year, so
never say whether a given party's primary is open. Primaries only matter on county
pages here: city, town, school and township races have no party primary.

### Partisanship

South Dakota splits cleanly by type of local government.

**County: partisan.** County commissioners and the elected county officers
(sheriff, auditor, register of deeds, treasurer, state's attorney, coroner) are
nominated in party primaries and elected on the political ballot.

*SDCL 12-1-1.1, 7-8-2, 12-6-7 · statutory · read in statute*

**Cities and towns: no party label.** Municipal candidates are nominated by
petition, and their names appear on the ballot with no designation other than
the office.

*SDCL 9-13-7, 9-13-21 · statutory · read in statute*

**School boards: no party label.** State law says no party affiliation may appear
on a school ballot.

*SDCL 13-7-13 · statutory · read in statute*

**Civil townships: no party process.** Township officers are elected by ballot at
the annual meeting. There is no primary, and a township may require nominating
petitions.

*SDCL 8-3-1.1, 8-3-15 · usual case · read in statute*

**Conservation districts: nonpolitical ballot.**

*SDCL 38-8-39 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never write "South Dakota local elections are nonpartisan" or
"partisan". County races are partisan, everything else local is not.

### Winning

**Party primaries** are won by the most votes. Primary runoffs exist only for
U.S. Senate, U.S. House and Governor, so they never apply to a local page.

*SDCL 12-6-50, 12-6-51.1 · statutory · read in statute*

**City and town races** are won by the most votes, unless the city has passed an
ordinance, at least six months before the election, requiring a runoff. Where it
has, a race of three or more candidates with no majority winner goes to a runoff
three weeks later between the top two.

*SDCL 9-13-25, 9-13-26.1 · usual case · read in statute*

**Ranked choice voting is banned** for every office, and no local charter or
ordinance may adopt it.

*SDCL 12-1-9.1 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not write "no runoff" on a city page. Whether a city uses
runoffs is its own choice, and we do not hold which ones do.

### Getting on the ballot as an independent

**County offices.** A candidate who is not nominated in a primary can run as an
independent by filing a certificate of nomination signed by registered voters of
the district. Any registered voter can run as an independent, whatever their own
registration. Someone who ran in the primary for an office cannot then file as an
independent for the same office that year.

*SDCL 12-7-1, 12-1-3(25), 12-7-5 · statutory · read in statute*

Voters registered with a party may sign an independent's petition, as long as
they have not already signed a petition for that office. Voters may not sign a
party candidate's petition for a party they do not belong to.

*SDCL 12-6-8 · statutory · read in statute*

**Cities, towns, school boards and townships.** Every candidate gets on the ballot
by petition (or, in many townships, at the meeting itself), and none runs under a
party. "Independent" is not a separate path at these tiers.

*SDCL 9-13-7, 13-7-6, 8-3-1.1 · statutory · read in statute*

## County government

Our data has **66** county pages for South Dakota.

*Our page data (pages-by-state.csv, 27 October 2026)*

### Board of county commissioners

Each organized county has a **board of county commissioners** of three to seven
members, elected **by district** to **four-year** terms at the November general
election. The terms are staggered: odd-numbered districts are elected in
governor's-race years, even-numbered districts in presidential years. County
voters can change the board to three, five or seven by petition and election.

*SDCL 7-8-1, 7-8-2, 7-8-3 · statutory · read in statute*

Counties also elect a **sheriff, county auditor and register of deeds** in one
cycle, and a **treasurer, state's attorney and coroner** two years later, each for
four years, unless a county charter provides otherwise.

*SDCL 7-7-1.1 · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** a county commission may **combine** two or more county offices
into one elected office. Some counties have done this, so never list all six
officers as separate races. Let race data supply which county offices exist.

*SDCL 7-7-1.2 · varies by place · read in statute*

Counties may also adopt an appointed **county manager**. The commission is
still elected the same way.

*SDCL 7-8A-1, 7-8A-2 · varies by place · read in statute*

### Charter counties

Any county may adopt a home rule charter. A charter may set any structure of
government, which then outranks statute, as long as the legislative body is
chosen by popular election. Elections are **not** reserved to state law at the
county tier, though a charter cannot set standards lower than state law, and the
Election Code bans ranked choice voting in any charter.

*S.D. Const. art. IX, § 2; SDCL 6-12-5, 12-1-9.1 · statutory · read in statute*

This doc did not check which counties, if any, have charters. If race data shows
offices that differ from the commission and six officers above, follow race data.

### Civil townships

Civil townships are the main sub-county layer and make up most of our South
Dakota pages. Their slugs usually end in `-township`
(`sd/brookings-county/brookings-township`).

At each annual township meeting voters elect **one supervisor** for a three-year
term, so the board of supervisors turns over one seat a year, plus a **clerk** and
a **treasurer**. They may also elect a constable.

*SDCL 8-3-12, 8-3-13 · statutory · read in statute*

Not all of the state is organized into townships, and a township's voters can
vote to abolish it. A township that already exists cannot be abolished without
such a vote.

*S.D. Const. art. IX, § 1; SDCL 8-1-5, 8-1-23 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** a town and a township often share a name and a county
(`sd/brookings-county/brookings` and `sd/brookings-county/brookings-township`).
They are different governments, with different offices and different election
days. Never drop the word "Township".

## City and town government

South Dakota law calls its municipalities **cities** and **towns**, and sorts
them into three classes by population.

*SDCL 9-1-1, 9-2-1 · statutory · read in statute*

| Class | Population | Form of government |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First class | 5,000 and over | mayor and council, or commission, either with or without a city manager |
| Second class | 500 to 4,999 | same choices as first class |
| Third class | under 500 | board of trustees, with or without a manager |

*SDCL 9-2-1, 9-11-11 · usual case · read in statute*

Class follows the federal census, but a city does not change class on its own. A
city that crosses a line must apply to circuit court for the change, and existing
classifications and forms of government carry on until changed.

*SDCL 9-2-2.1, 9-2-4, 9-11-11 · statutory · read in statute*

### Mayor and council (aldermanic)

A **mayor** elected at large and a **common council** of two aldermen per ward.
The mayor's term is two to five years, set by ordinance. Aldermen serve two
years unless an ordinance sets three, four or five. Voters of a second or third
class municipality may choose to elect aldermen at large.

*SDCL 9-8-1, 9-8-4, 9-8-4.1 · usual case · read in statute*

### Commission

A **mayor** and **two or four commissioners**, all elected at large, with terms
of two to five years set by ordinance. A commission city that employs a city
manager instead has **nine at-large commissioners**.

*SDCL 9-9-1, 9-9-3, 9-10-5 · usual case · read in statute*

### Board of trustees

Third class municipalities usually have a **board of three or five trustees**,
elected at large to three-year terms.

*SDCL 9-7-1, 9-7-3 · usual case · read in statute*

### Changing the form

Voters can change the form of government, or the number of wards,
commissioners or trustees, by petition and election. Councils and boards can set
several term lengths by ordinance within the ranges above.

*SDCL 9-11-6, 9-8-1, 9-9-3 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** seat counts and terms vary by place. Describe the offices race
data shows, not a template.

### Home rule cities

Any city may adopt a home rule charter, and its charter's structure outranks
statute as long as the council is popularly elected. Home rule does not reserve
elections to state law at the municipal tier, but a charter cannot set standards
lower than state law or adopt ranked choice voting. The Election Code itself
grants charter cities no other election exceptions.

*S.D. Const. art. IX, § 2; SDCL 6-12-5, 12-1-9.1 · statutory · read in statute*

This doc did not read any city charter. On the largest cities, assume a charter
may change offices and terms, and follow race data.

## Writing rules

These are the ways South Dakota copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never say South Dakota local elections are nonpartisan, or that they are
   partisan.** County races are partisan. City, town, school board, township and
   conservation district races carry no party label.
2. **Never state a fixed month for city or school elections.** Each city and
   school board picks June or November, and the rules changed on 1 January 2026.
   Take the month from race data.
3. **Township officers are elected at the annual township meeting in March.**
   Never describe a township race as being on the June or November ballot.
4. **Keep "Township" in township names.** 23 South Dakota pages share a name with
   another place in the same county, almost always a town and its township.
   Decide the government type from the offices on the page, not the slug (see
   the data issues below).
5. **Call the county prosecutor the "state's attorney"** and the county board the
   "board of county commissioners". Our position labels say "district attorney"
   and "county legislature".
6. **Never list all six county officers as separate races.** Counties can combine
   offices. Let race data supply them.
7. Do not state seat counts or term lengths as fixed for a city or town. Forms
   and ordinances vary them.
8. Never infer a county from a place name. No South Dakota municipal page is named
   after a county it is not in, but the rule still holds.
9. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
10. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
    contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures, and whether a party has
    opened its primary to independents.

### What search results commonly get wrong

- South Dakota is often called an "open primary" state. It is closed by default,
  and each party decides whether to let unaffiliated voters in.
- Older guides and city websites give city and school election dates that no
  longer apply. Since 1 January 2026, each city and school board picks June or
  November.
- Township officers are sometimes described as elected in November. They are
  elected at the March township meeting.

**Bulk-access recipe.** The site itself is a JavaScript app, but its API serves
text. A whole chapter is `https://sdlegislature.gov/api/Statutes/<chapter>.html?all=true`
(e.g. `12-6`, `9-13`), returned as **UTF-16**, so decode it as UTF-16LE before
searching. Without `?all=true` you get only the table of contents. Cite single
sections as `https://sdlegislature.gov/api/Statutes/<section>.html` (UTF-8, works
with `verify-ledger.ts`). The constitution is title `0N`: whole articles are
`/api/Statutes/0N-<article>.html?all=true`, and single sections cite as
`/api/Statutes/Constitution/<article>-<section>` (JSON). Two quirks for quotes:
the JSON keeps line breaks as a literal `\r\n`, so keep constitution quotes within
one printed line, and the HTML often splits hyphenated words across tags, so
avoid quoting across a hyphen ("odd-numbered"). Each section ends with its source
notes, which list every session law that changed it, the quickest amendment
check. No rate limit was hit across about 40 chapter downloads.

Aggregator sites were not used and must not appear in published copy. Statutes
may be cited by name and number.
