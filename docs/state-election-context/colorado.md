# Colorado: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Colorado so you do not have to research the same
ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which
offices are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race
data, which always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean. Every claim below has a verbatim quote
in [`research/colorado.json`](./research/colorado.json).

**The one thing to know first:** Colorado runs several election calendars, and
partisanship splits by type of government. County races are partisan, on the
November ballot of even-numbered years, with party primaries in June that
unaffiliated voters may vote in. School board races are nonpartisan, in November
of odd-numbered years. Special districts are nonpartisan and vote in May of
odd-numbered years. Statutory cities and towns are nonpartisan, and by default
towns vote in April of even years and cities in November of odd years, but each
can move its date. Home rule cities and towns set their own election rules by
charter. About half of our Colorado location pages are school districts.

## Legislative calendar

Next regular session: convenes 13 January 2027 (the General Assembly meets every
year no later than the second Wednesday of January, Colo. Const. art. V, § 7).
Regular sessions are limited to 120 calendar days, so the session ends by
12 May 2027. The governor has ten days to act on a bill during the session. For
bills still on the desk when the session ends, the governor has thirty days
after adjournment to veto, or the bill becomes law (Colo. Const. art. IV, § 11),
so the session's bills are settled by about 11 June 2027.

New laws take effect on the date the act states, or on passage if it states none
(Colo. Const. art. V, § 19). A referendum petition against an act may be filed
up to ninety days after the session ends (Colo. Const. art. V, § 1(3)).

## How elections work in Colorado

Safe on any Colorado page, at the tier named.

### Election days

**County** offices are on the even-year cycle. The general election is the
Tuesday after the first Monday of November in even-numbered years, and party
primaries are the last Tuesday in June of even-numbered years. County
commissioners and the other elected county officers serve four-year terms, with
commissioner seats staggered so the board does not turn over all at once.

*C.R.S. 1-1-104(17), 1-4-101(1); Colo. Const. art. XIV, §§ 6, 8 · statutory · read in statute*

**School boards** are elected on the first Tuesday in November of odd-numbered
years. A board has five, six or seven directors serving four-year terms. Every
director is usually voted on by the whole district, even where directors must
live in a director district. A few districts that had six-year terms in 1999
may still use them.

*C.R.S. 22-31-104(1), 22-31-105(1)(a), (2) · usual case · read in statute*

**Special districts** (fire protection, water, metropolitan and other districts
under Title 32) hold their regular elections on the Tuesday after the first
Monday of May in odd-numbered years. Boards have five or seven directors serving
four-year terms.

*C.R.S. 32-1-103, 1-13.5-111(1), 32-1-305.5(2), (3)(a) · statutory · read in statute*

**Cities and towns** depend on what kind of municipality they are (see City and
town government):

- A **statutory town** holds its regular election on the first Tuesday of April
  in even-numbered years.
- A **statutory city** holds its regular election on the first Tuesday of
  November in odd-numbered years.
- Either may move, by a vote of its residents, to November of odd or even
  years, and may later move back.
- A **home rule** city or town sets its own election date by charter.

*C.R.S. 31-1-101(10), 31-10-109(1); Colo. Const. art. XX, § 6(d) · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never say a Colorado city or town votes in April, or in November,
without race data. The April default applies only to statutory towns that have
not moved their date, and home rule places pick their own. Our Denver and
Colorado Springs pages show city races in the spring of odd years, Cañon City in
November. Take the date from race data.

When several governments with overlapping boundaries hold elections on the same
November day, the county clerk and recorder runs them together as one
**coordinated election**, by mail ballot. That is why a November ballot in an
odd year often carries school board, city and ballot measure races together.
Sharing a ballot with partisan races does not make a nonpartisan race partisan.

*C.R.S. 1-7-116(1)(a), 1-1-104(23.6) · statutory · read in statute*

**Uncontested local races may never reach the ballot.** In a nonpartisan
election, if there are no more candidates than seats when filing closes, the
governing body may cancel the election and declare the candidates elected. The
same applies to special districts. A party primary may likewise be canceled when
no party has a contested race.

*C.R.S. 1-5-208(1.5), 1-13.5-513(1), 1-4-104.5(1) · varies by place · read in statute*

### Primary type

Colorado's party primaries are open to unaffiliated voters. A voter registered
with a party votes that party's ballot. An unaffiliated voter may vote in the
primary of one major party without joining it. Unaffiliated voters are mailed
the ballots of every major party and may return only one. If they return more
than one, none is counted.

*C.R.S. 1-7-201(2), (2.3), 1-4-101(2)(b) · statutory · read in statute*

Only major parties nominate by primary. County officers of major parties are
nominated that way, unless the party switches to nominating by assembly or
convention, which takes a vote of three-fourths of the party's state central
committee. Primary nominees win by plurality.

*C.R.S. 1-4-101(1), (3), 1-4-702(1), 1-4-104 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not call Colorado primaries fully open, or closed. Party
members cannot cross over, and unaffiliated voters choose one party's ballot.

### Partisanship

Partisanship splits by type of government.

**County races are partisan.** Major party candidates are nominated in the
primary and run with their party label in November.

*C.R.S. 1-4-101(3), 1-1-104(23.6) · statutory · read in statute*

**School board races are nonpartisan.** Candidates are nominated by petition and
appear without a party label.

*C.R.S. 22-31-107(2), 1-4-503, 1-5-406 · statutory · read in statute*

**Special district races are nonpartisan.** Candidates nominate themselves by
filing a form.

*C.R.S. 1-13.5-303(1), 1-4-503, 1-5-406 · statutory · read in statute*

**Statutory city and town races are nonpartisan.** Candidates are nominated by
petition without regard to party, and the ballot shows no party label.

*C.R.S. 31-10-302(1), 31-10-902(2) · statutory · read in statute*

**Home rule city and town races follow the charter.** The constitution gives a
home rule charter control over all matters of municipal elections, including
making them nonpartisan. Nonpartisan is the usual case, but avoid calling a
home rule city's races nonpartisan as a matter of law. Defer to race data.

*Colo. Const. art. XX, § 6(d) · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** "local races are nonpartisan" is wrong on every Colorado county
page. Name the tier.

### Winning an election

The candidate with the most votes wins, and ties are settled by lot. Statutory
cities and towns declare elected those with the greatest number of votes. No
runoff appears in the state election laws read for this doc.

*C.R.S. 1-4-104, 1-11-102, 31-10-1205(1) · statutory · read in statute*

Local governments may use **instant runoff voting** (ranked choice) for
single-seat races, and a statutory or home rule municipality may run such an
election as part of the November coordinated ballot. A home rule charter may
also set its own runoff. Avoid saying a Colorado city has no runoff or ranked
voting. Defer to race data.

*C.R.S. 1-7-1003(3)(a), 1-7-116(1)(d); Colo. Const. art. XX, § 6(d) · varies by place · read in statute*

### Running as an independent

For **county** office, a candidate who does not want a major party label is
nominated by petition, signed by voters who live in the county. The candidate
must have been registered as unaffiliated (or with a minor party) by the first
business day of January of the election year, and may not use a party's name to
describe themselves. Any eligible voter may sign, but not for two candidates for
the same office.

*C.R.S. 1-4-802(1), (1)(a), (1)(c), (1)(g)(II), 1-4-904(2)(b) · statutory · read in statute*

For **school board, special district and statutory city or town** office, every
candidate is in effect an independent: there is no party nomination. School and
municipal candidates file a petition. Special district candidates file a
self-nomination form. A voter may not sign more than one municipal nomination
petition for the same office.

*C.R.S. 1-4-503, 22-31-107(2), 1-13.5-303(1), 31-10-302(1), (5) · statutory · read in statute*

## County government

Our data has 64 Colorado county pages, including the City and County of Denver
and the City and County of Broomfield. The Election Code treats a city and
county as a county.

*C.R.S. 1-1-104(7) · statutory · read in statute*

### Board of county commissioners

Each county is governed by a **board of county commissioners**. A county of under
70,000 people has three commissioners. A county of 70,000 or more may expand to
five by a vote of its residents. Commissioners serve four-year staggered terms.

*Colo. Const. art. XIV, § 6; C.R.S. 30-10-306.5(1) · usual case · read in statute*

With three commissioners, the county is divided into three districts. Each
commissioner must live in a district but is elected by the voters of the whole
county. Five-member counties may instead have some commissioners elected only by
their district's voters.

*C.R.S. 30-10-306(1), 30-10-306.7(2)(a) · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not write that a Colorado county commissioner is elected only
by voters in their own district. In a three-member county the whole county
votes for every seat.

### Other elected county offices

Each county elects a **clerk and recorder, sheriff, coroner, treasurer, surveyor
and assessor**, each for four years, at the same election as the legislature.
The county attorney is hired by the commissioners, not elected.

*Colo. Const. art. XIV, § 8; C.R.S. 30-11-118 · usual case · read in statute*

Our data shows a surveyor race in only some counties, so do not list a surveyor
unless race data has one.

### Home rule counties

Voters in any county may adopt a **home rule charter** setting the county's
organization and structure. In a home rule county, the constitutional rules on
commissioners and elected county officers apply only as far as the charter says.
The charter decides which officers are elected or appointed and their terms. A
home rule county must still provide every mandatory county function.

*Colo. Const. art. XIV, § 16(1), (3), (5); C.R.S. 30-11-513 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Does home rule reach elections?** For the offices, yes: a charter may make an
office appointive, change its term or create new elected offices. The Election
Code still governs general and primary elections. On a home rule county page,
take the offices from race data and do not describe the standard structure.

*C.R.S. 1-1-102(1), 30-11-513 · varies by place · read in statute*

Our Weld County page shows a county council race and no treasurer or coroner
race, which is not the standard structure. Take Weld's offices from race data.

### Townships

No township government appears in the Colorado county or municipal law read for
this doc, and our data has no township pages. The elected units below the county
are cities, towns, school districts and special districts.

## City and town government

Colorado municipalities come in three kinds: places incorporated before
statehood that kept their old charter, **statutory** cities and towns organized
under state law, and **home rule** cities and towns governed by their own
charter. The legislature may create no more than four classes, and statutory
places are divided into cities and towns only.

*C.R.S. 31-1-201(1), 31-1-203(1); Colo. Const. art. XIV, § 13 · statutory · read in statute*

### Statutory cities and towns

- A **statutory city** has over 2,000 people.
- A **statutory town** has 2,000 or fewer.

**Class does not follow population automatically.** Any place that was a city or
a town at the end of 1980 keeps that status, whatever its size, unless it
reorganizes. A growing town becomes a city only by electing city officers at a
later regular election. Never infer a Colorado place's type from its population.

*C.R.S. 31-1-101(2), (13), 31-1-204(2) · statutory · read in statute*

**What a statutory town elects:** a board of trustees of a mayor and six
trustees, for two-year terms by default. The board may adopt four-year
overlapping terms by ordinance, and voters may cut the board to four trustees.

*C.R.S. 31-4-301(1), (2), (5), 31-4-301.5(1) · usual case · read in statute*

**What a statutory city elects:** a mayor, clerk and treasurer at large, and two
council members from each ward, for two-year terms by default. The council may
adopt four-year overlapping terms by ordinance, voters may make the clerk and
treasurer appointive, and the city may reorganize into council-manager form.

*C.R.S. 31-4-105, 31-4-107(3), (4)(a), 31-4-201 · usual case · read in statute*

These defaults are often changed. Take each place's offices and terms from race
data.

### Home rule cities and towns

Any city or town of 2,000 or more may adopt a home rule charter. The charter
supersedes conflicting state law on local and municipal matters, and the
constitution names the creation and terms of municipal offices and all matters
of municipal elections (date, nominations, election system and whether races
are nonpartisan) among those matters.

*Colo. Const. art. XX, § 6, 6(a), 6(d) · statutory · read in statute*

**Does home rule reach elections?** Yes, and fully. A home rule city or town
sets its own election date, offices, terms, nomination rules and runoff rules.
State election law fills in only where the charter is silent, and any
municipality may choose to run its elections under the state Election Code
instead of the Municipal Election Code. Never describe a home rule city's
offices or election date from the statutory defaults.

*Colo. Const. art. XX, § 6, 6(d); C.R.S. 1-1-102(1) · varies by place · read in statute*

Our pages do not say which kind a place is, so avoid naming a place statutory or
home rule unless race data or the place itself says so.

## Metro and major-place exceptions

### Denver

`co/denver-county`

The City and County of Denver is one consolidated government. Its charter, not
the county rules above, decides which officers are elected and their terms.
Denver has no board of county commissioners. Our page shows its mayor, city
council, auditor and clerk and recorder races, with city races in the spring of
odd years. Take dates and offices from race data.

*Colo. Const. art. XX, §§ 1, 2 · statutory · read in statute*

Denver Public Schools (`co/denver-county-school-district-1`) has a seven-member
board, five elected from director districts and two at large, in November of
odd-numbered years. Its elections are run by Denver's election commission.

*C.R.S. 22-31-105(1)(b), 22-31-131(1) · statutory · read in statute*

### Broomfield

`co/broomfield-county`

The City and County of Broomfield was created in 2001 from parts of Adams,
Boulder, Jefferson and Weld counties. Its charter and ordinances set its
officers. Do not describe a county commission or a separate city and county
government there.

*Colo. Const. art. XX, §§ 10, 11 · statutory · read in statute*

### Weld County

`co/weld-county`

Shows a county council race and not the standard set of county offices. See
Home rule counties. Take its offices from race data.

### Colorado Springs, Aurora and other large cities

`co/el-paso-county/colorado-springs`, `co/arapahoe-county/aurora`

These are the largest place pages after Denver. Take election dates, offices and
terms from race data, since home rule places set their own. Some Colorado
cities extend into more than one county (Broomfield did before it became a
county). Our pages file each city under one county, so do not write that a city
lies wholly within the county on its page.

## Writing rules

These are the ways Colorado copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never infer a county from a place name.** Of the 9 Colorado place pages
   that share a name with a Colorado county, one sits in a different county:
   the city of Las Animas is in Bent County, not Las Animas County. The full
   list is in
   [`research/places-named-after-another-county.json`](./research/places-named-after-another-county.json).
   Always take the county from the page data.
2. **Name the tier before saying partisan or nonpartisan.** County races are
   partisan. School board, special district and statutory city and town races
   are not. Home rule cities follow their charter.
3. **Use the right calendar for the tier.** Counties: November of even years,
   with a June primary. School boards: November of odd years. Special districts:
   May of odd years. Cities and towns: from race data only.
4. Do not call Colorado primaries open or closed. Unaffiliated voters may choose
   one party's ballot. Party members may not cross over.
5. Do not say a commissioner is elected only by their district's voters, or
   that every county has three commissioners.
6. Do not describe the standard county structure for Denver, Broomfield or Weld
   County, or the statutory city structure for a home rule city.
7. Do not call any Colorado place a township, village or borough. Colorado has
   cities and towns only.
8. Do not infer whether a place is a city or a town, or statutory or home rule,
   from its population.
9. Avoid saying a race has no runoff or ranked voting in a home rule city, and
   do not mention a contest at all when race data shows it was canceled.
10. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
11. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
    contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.

### What search results commonly get wrong

- That Colorado has fully open primaries. Unaffiliated voters choose one
  party's ballot. Party members are limited to their own.
- That all Colorado municipal elections are in April. Only statutory towns that
  kept the default vote then. Cities default to November and home rule places
  choose.
- That county commissioners are elected by district. In three-member counties
  the whole county votes for every seat.
- That Denver has a county commission. It does not.

## Data issues for the data team

Do not write copy around these. They are for the data team to fix.

- **Denver and Broomfield city offices are labeled as county offices.** On
  `co/denver-county` and `co/broomfield-county` the mayor and city council sit
  under "County" (position slugs `county-executive-head` and
  `county-legislature-executive-board`). There is no separate Denver or
  Broomfield city page.
- **Combined offices in the place slot.** Denver's clerk and recorder sits at
  `/elections/co/denver-county/county-clerk/position/recorder-joint`, and Las
  Animas's clerk and treasurer at
  `/elections/co/bent-county/las-animas/city-clerk/position/treasurer-joint`.
- **Cañon City's slug drops the ñ:** `co/fremont-county/caon-city` and
  `co/caon-city-school-district-re-1`. The page heading spells it correctly.
- **Special districts have almost no pages.** All 175 Colorado district pages
  are school districts. Fire, community college and utility boards appear only
  as positions on a few county or city pages (for example Larimer, Mesa and
  Kiowa counties).
- Our data has 125 city and town pages, far fewer than Colorado's
  municipalities, so many small towns have no page.
