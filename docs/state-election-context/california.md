# California: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across California so you do not have to research the same
ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which
offices are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race
data, which always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean.

**The one thing to know first:** every county, city, school and judicial office
in California is nonpartisan by the state constitution, including in charter
cities and charter counties. No local race on our California pages carries a
party label. What varies is timing: state law sets default dates, but charter
cities, charter counties and many districts have moved their elections, so
only race data can say when a given place votes.

## Legislative calendar

The Legislature meets year-round in two-year sessions, so a change can pass in
almost any month. It next convenes on 7 December 2026 (noon on the first Monday
in December of each even-numbered year, Cal. Const. art. IV, § 3(a)), and that
session ends on 30 November 2028. In the first year, the governor has 30 days
after the Legislature's autumn joint recess to act on bills they hold; in the
second year, bills passed before 1 September become law unless returned by
30 September (Cal. Const. art. IV, § 10(b)). A regular-session law usually takes
effect on 1 January after a 90-day period from enactment (Cal. Const. art. IV,
§ 8(c)).

Re-check this doc: November each year, once the autumn signing window has
closed. Last researched: October 2026. Reviewed by Emily: 28 October 2026.

*Cal. Const. art. IV, §§ 3(a), 8(c), 10(b) · statutory · read in statute*

## How elections work in California

### Election days

State law allows elections only on a short list of **established election
dates**: the first Tuesday after the first Monday in March (presidential years,
and odd years), the second Tuesday of April in even years, the first Tuesday
after the first Monday in June (even years that are not presidential years),
and the first Tuesday after the first Monday in **November of every year**.
County, city, district and school district elections must use one of them.

*Cal. Elec. Code §§ 1000, 1002 · statutory · read in statute*

The statewide primary is in **June** in governor's-race years and in **March**
in presidential years. The statewide general election is in **November of even
years**.

*Cal. Elec. Code §§ 1200, 1201 · statutory · read in statute*

**County.** County offices are filled at the statewide primary, not in
November. The district attorney and sheriff are elected at the presidential
primary (March). Other county offices default to the governor's-year primary
(June), and a board of supervisors may move any of them except the county
superintendent of schools to the presidential primary. A county office goes to
a November runoff only if nobody wins a majority at the primary (see Winning).

*Cal. Elec. Code § 1300 · statutory · read in statute*

**City.** A general law city holds its election on an established election
date, and its council may move it by ordinance to the statewide primary, the
statewide general election, the school district date, March of odd years, or
April. The move takes effect when the county board of supervisors approves it,
and it may not lengthen or shorten any term by more than 12 months.

*Cal. Elec. Code §§ 1301, 10403.5(b) · statutory · read in statute*

**School and special districts.** School boards, community college boards and
county boards of education default to November of **odd** years, and each may
move by resolution to the statewide primary, the statewide general election
or the city's election date. Special districts also default to November of odd
years unless their own act says otherwise, and may move to the statewide
general election.

*Cal. Elec. Code §§ 1302, 1303 · usual case · read in statute*

**Pressure toward even years.** Since 1 January 2018, a city, school district, community
college district or other district may not keep voting off-cycle if doing so
has already cut its turnout at least 25 percent below its average for the last
four statewide general elections.

*Cal. Elec. Code §§ 14051, 14052, 14057 · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** the defaults above are not what most places actually do. Many
cities and districts have moved to even-year November elections, and charter
cities and charter counties are not bound by the state's election-date chapter
at all where their charter says otherwise. Never write "California cities vote
in odd years" or "school board elections are in November of odd-numbered
years". Let race data say when a place votes.

*Cal. Elec. Code § 1003(b) · varies by place · read in statute*

### Primary type

California runs two different kinds of primary.

**Top-two (voter-nominated) primary** for Congress and state offices: governor
and the other statewide offices, the Board of Equalization, U.S. Senate and
House, state senate and Assembly. Every candidate is on one ballot, any voter
may vote for any candidate whatever their own or the candidate's party
preference, and the top two vote-getters go to November regardless of party.
A candidate's ballot line reads either their party preference or "Party
Preference: None".

*Cal. Const. art. II, § 5(a), (b); Cal. Elec. Code §§ 359.5, 8002.5 · statutory · read in statute*

**Partisan primary** for president and party committees only. A voter who
registered without a party preference gets a ballot with only the nonpartisan
races, top-two races and measures, unless they ask for a party's presidential
ballot and that party has chosen to allow it.

*Cal. Const. art. II, § 5(c); Cal. Elec. Code §§ 13102(b), 2151(b) · statutory · read in statute*

For our audience: in California, a voter with no party preference loses nothing
on any local, state or congressional race. They vote for any candidate in every
one of them. Only the presidential primary can be limited.

### Partisanship

**Every local office is nonpartisan, by the constitution.** All judicial,
school, county and city offices are nonpartisan. Parties may not nominate
anyone for them, and a candidate's party preference may not appear on the
ballot. This holds in charter cities and charter counties too, because it is in
the state constitution, not in general law.

*Cal. Const. art. II, § 6; Cal. Elec. Code § 334 · statutory · read in statute*

This is true at every tier we have pages for: county offices, city offices,
school boards, community college boards and county boards of education.
Special district boards are elected under the Uniform District Election Law
with no party primary; they are not listed in the constitutional sentence, so
say "no party primary" rather than quoting the constitution for them.

*Cal. Elec. Code §§ 10500, 1303 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** a party may still endorse a local candidate, and candidates often
say which party they belong to. "Nonpartisan" here means no party label on the
ballot and no party nomination, not that the race has no party politics.

### Winning

**County offices (and judges): majority or runoff.** A candidate who wins a
majority at the primary is elected outright and the office does not appear in
November. Otherwise the top vote-getters, twice the number of open seats, go to
the November ballot.

*Cal. Elec. Code §§ 8140, 8141, 1300 · statutory · read in statute*

**Cities, school boards and special districts: plurality by default.** The
candidate with the most votes wins, in one round, unless a city or county
charter or the law a city is organized under provides otherwise.

*Cal. Elec. Code § 15452 · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** charter cities can and do run their own systems, such as a
primary with a runoff or ranked choice voting. Never write "one round" or "most
votes wins" on a charter city page, and never write "no runoff" on a county
page.

### Getting on the ballot as an independent

Because local races are nonpartisan, every local candidate gets on the ballot
the same way: a declaration of candidacy plus nomination papers signed by
registered voters. There is no separate "independent" route and no party
nomination to compete with.

*Cal. Elec. Code §§ 8020(a), 10220 · statutory · read in statute*

Signers must be voters in the jurisdiction the candidate would represent. A
voter may not sign for more candidates than there are seats for that office;
on city nomination papers, no voter may sign more than one paper for the same
office.

*Cal. Elec. Code §§ 8068, 8041(a), 10220 · statutory · read in statute*

For a nonpartisan office decided at a primary, a candidate can be added for
the November ballot by other means only if nobody was nominated or elected at
the primary for that office.

*Cal. Elec. Code § 8300 · statutory · read in statute*

## County government

Our data has **58** county pages for California, and every municipal page sits
under one of them. The constitution divides the whole state into counties, so
there is no land outside a county.

*Cal. Const. art. XI, § 1(a); our page data (pages-by-state.csv, 27 October 2026) · statutory · read in statute*

### Board of supervisors

Every county has an elected governing body, sheriff, district attorney and
assessor. In a general law county, the governing body is a **board of
supervisors of five members**, elected **by district** to staggered **four-year
terms**.

*Cal. Const. art. XI, § 1(b); Cal. Gov. Code §§ 25000(a), 25040 · statutory · read in statute*

By default the voters also elect the treasurer, county clerk, auditor, tax
collector, recorder, public administrator and coroner. County voters may make
any of these appointive, but never the sheriff, district attorney or assessor.

*Cal. Gov. Code § 24009 · usual case · read in statute*

**Combined offices.** A board of supervisors may combine county offices, so a
single elected official is often a "clerk-recorder", "treasurer-tax collector",
"auditor-controller" or "sheriff-coroner". Use the office name exactly as race
data gives it.

*Cal. Gov. Code § 24300 · varies by place · read in statute*

The **county superintendent of schools** is elected at the governor's-year
election in some counties and appointed by the county board of education in
others, as each county's voters decided.

*Cal. Const. art. IX, § 3 · varies by place · read in statute*

### Charter counties

Any county may adopt a charter by majority vote. A charter county must still
have a governing body of **at least five** members (by district, at large, or
at large with a district residency rule) and an elected sheriff, district
attorney and assessor. The charter sets everything else: terms, and which other
officers are elected or appointed. Charter counties are not bound by the
state's election-date chapter where their charter differs. Their offices stay
nonpartisan.

*Cal. Const. art. XI, §§ 3(a), 4(a), (c); Cal. Elec. Code § 1003(b) · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never write "five supervisors" as a fact about every California
county. It is the general-law rule and a charter minimum, not a cap.

### Townships and other sub-county units

California has no townships. Below the county, the only general-purpose
local governments are cities. Outside city limits, local elected offices are
school boards and **special districts** (fire, water, hospital, parks,
sanitation, community services and many more), each with its own board.

*Cal. Const. art. XI, §§ 1, 2 · usual case · read in statute*

Special district elections run under the Uniform District Election Law, and
some districts let only landowners vote.

*Cal. Elec. Code § 10500 · varies by place · read in statute*

In our data, special districts do **not** have their own pages. Their offices
appear as positions on the county page or on a place page (see "Pages that are
not cities" below).

## City government

California cities come in two kinds, and there are no population classes:

- **General law cities** follow the Government Code.
- **Charter cities** (the state calls them "chartered cities") run their
  municipal affairs under their own charter.

*Cal. Gov. Code §§ 34101, 34102 · statutory · read in statute*

"Town" is only a name. A city council can swap "city" for "town" in its
official name, and a "town" is legally the same as a city. Use whichever word
the place's own name uses.

*Cal. Gov. Code § 34502 · statutory · read in statute*

Unincorporated communities, however large, are not cities and have no city
government.

### General law city

A **city council of at least five**, a **city clerk** and a **city treasurer**.
Voters may make the clerk and treasurer appointive, and many have.

*Cal. Gov. Code §§ 36501, 36508 · usual case · read in statute*

**The mayor.** By default, after each city election the council picks one of
its own members as mayor; the mayor is not elected by voters. Voters may instead choose
a directly elected mayor, for a two- or four-year term, who sits as a member of
the council.

*Cal. Gov. Code §§ 36801, 34900, 34903 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never write "voters elect the mayor" on a general law city page.
Only race data can say whether a city's mayor is on the ballot. Our data has an
elected-mayor position for only 185 of California's cities (sitemap shard 5,
27 October 2026).

Councils may be elected at large or by districts. District councils have five,
seven or nine districts, or four, six or eight with a separately elected mayor,
and serve four-year terms unless otherwise provided. A council may switch to
districts by ordinance, without a public vote, to comply with the California
Voting Rights Act.

*Cal. Gov. Code §§ 34871, 34879, 34886 · usual case · read in statute*

The Voting Rights Act chapter was amended in 2026 (Senate Bill 1164), mainly on
how a district change is ordered. It does not amend the sections cited here.

*Stats. 2026, ch. 338 (SB 1164) · statutory · read in statute*

Councils or voters may also set term limits for council members and an elected
mayor.

*Cal. Gov. Code § 36502(b) · varies by place · read in statute*

### Charter cities (home rule)

**Any** city may adopt a charter; there is no population threshold. A charter
may set how, when and for how long its officers are elected, and expressly may
provide for the "conduct of city elections". So a charter city can set its own
election dates, council size, terms and runoff rules. It cannot make its
offices partisan: the constitution's nonpartisan rule still applies.

*Cal. Const. art. XI, §§ 3(a), 5(b); Cal. Gov. Code § 34450; Cal. Elec. Code §§ 1003(b), 15452(a) · statutory · read in statute*

A charter may also set how a city's board of education is elected or
appointed, so a few big-city school boards follow the city charter instead of
the Education Code.

*Cal. Const. art. IX, § 16(a) · varies by place · read in statute*

## School districts and county boards of education

These are the largest group of California pages (see below).

- **School district boards:** five members elected at large by default, four-year
  staggered terms. A unified district may have seven. Small districts may have
  three. A district may instead elect by trustee area.
  *Cal. Educ. Code §§ 35012(a), (b), 5019(a), 5017, 5030 · usual case · read in statute*
- **Community college boards:** five or seven members, four-year terms.
  *Cal. Educ. Code § 72023 · usual case · read in statute*
- **County board of education:** every county except a city and county has one,
  elected by trustee area. In a charter county, the charter or the supervisors
  decide how it is chosen.
  *Cal. Educ. Code § 1000(a) · usual case · read in statute*

All are nonpartisan (see Partisanship).

## Metro and major-place exceptions

### San Francisco

`ca/san-francisco-county`, `ca/san-francisco-county/san-francisco`

California's only consolidated **city and county**: one government that is
both a charter city and a charter county, with city powers winning where the
two conflict. Never describe a separate San Francisco city council and county
board, and never write about "San Francisco County government" as if it were
distinct from the city. It has no separate county board of education.

*Cal. Const. art. XI, § 6; Cal. Educ. Code § 1000(a) · statutory · read in statute*

The city and county's offices come from its charter, which this doc did not
read. Let race data supply offices, seat counts and the voting method.

In our data the city page, `ca/san-francisco-county/san-francisco`, lists the
city attorney plus the county's offices (board of supervisors, sheriff,
district attorney, assessor-recorder, treasurer-tax collector), and site search
for "San Francisco" lands there. Election-api has no mayor's race for San
Francisco, so do not mention the mayor's office unless race data shows it.

*Our page data and goodparty.org search, checked 28 October 2026*

### Los Angeles, San Diego, San Jose and other large cities

`ca/los-angeles-county/los-angeles`, `ca/san-diego-county/san-diego`,
`ca/santa-clara-county/san-jose`, `ca/alameda-county/oakland`,
`ca/sacramento-county/sacramento`, `ca/fresno-county/fresno`,
`ca/los-angeles-county/long-beach`

Large California cities usually govern themselves under a charter, which this
doc did not read for any city. A charter can set election dates, council size,
the mayor's office, runoffs and ranked choice. Assume a charter, describe only
what race data shows, and never apply the general law city rules above to
them. Their races are still nonpartisan.

*Cal. Const. art. XI, § 5(b) · varies by place · read in statute*

### Charter counties

Some counties have charters; this doc did not count or read them. Do not
state the size of a county's board of supervisors or which officers it elects
from this doc; let race data supply them.

*Cal. Const. art. XI, § 4 · varies by place · read in statute*

### Pages that are not cities

27 of our 506 California municipal-tier pages have no city council position.
One is San Francisco (its city page lists the county's offices, see above). The
other 26 are Census county divisions: areas the Census Bureau draws for
counting, with no government of their own. Election-api matched special
district races to them by name, so pages such as `ca/marin-county/bolinas`,
`ca/san-diego-county/fallbrook`, `ca/contra-costa-county/central-contra-costa`
and `ca/riverside-county/coachella-valley` list only special district boards
(the Central Contra Costa Sanitary District on that page, for example). The
pages never call themselves cities, and the races on them are real. Never call
these places cities or towns, never describe a city government for them, and
describe only the district offices race data shows.

*Our page data (sitemap shard 5, checked 27 October 2026); Census TIGERweb county subdivisions, all 26 named "<name> CCD" with no functioning government (checked 28 October 2026)*

## Writing rules

These are the ways California copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never put a party label on a local California race** and never describe a
   local candidate as a party's nominee. County, city, school and judicial
   offices are nonpartisan by the constitution, everywhere.
2. **Never state a local election date as a rule.** The state defaults (odd
   years for cities, schools and districts) are widely overridden. Let race
   data say when a place votes.
3. **Never write that county offices are decided in November.** They are
   decided at the March or June primary, and go to November only as a runoff.
4. **Never write "voters elect the mayor" for a general law city** unless race
   data shows a mayor's race.
5. **Never apply general law city rules to a charter city**, and assume a large
   city has a charter unless race data suggests otherwise.
6. **Never describe San Francisco as having separate city and county
   governments.**
7. **Never infer a county from a place name.** One California municipal page is
   named after a county it is not in: San Joaquin (Fresno County), not San
   Joaquin County. Always take the county from the page data.
8. **Say "no party preference", not "independent", for voters.** "Registered
   independent" is ambiguous here: the American Independent Party is a real
   party, and voters who think they registered independent may have joined it.
   *Secretary of State, Qualified Political Parties · usual case · Secretary of State publication*
9. **Never call an unincorporated community a city or town.** See "Pages that
   are not cities".
10. Use combined county office names ("sheriff-coroner", "clerk-recorder")
    exactly as race data gives them.
11. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
12. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
    contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.

### What search results commonly get wrong

- California is often said to have "open primaries" for everything. Top-two
  covers Congress and state offices only; local races have no party primary at
  all, and the presidential primary is partisan.
- Summaries often say independents "can't vote in California primaries". A
  no-party-preference voter can vote in every race except, depending on party
  rules, the presidential primary.
- Older guides list odd-year November as the date for city and school
  elections. That is only the default, and many places have moved to even
  years since the 2018 turnout law.
- General law cities are often described as having an elected mayor. By default
  the council picks the mayor.

## Data issues for the data team

These are problems in our own page data, noted so the data team can fix them.
Do not write copy around them.

- **Special districts matched to Census county divisions.** 26 municipal-tier
  pages are Census county divisions that election-api attached special district
  races to (list in "Pages that are not cities"). Our code cannot fix this
  without hiding real races; the fix is giving these special districts their
  own places in election-api.
- **No San Francisco mayor's race.** Election-api carries no mayor's race for
  San Francisco. The rest of its offices show on the city page.

Two things that looked like data issues turned out not to be (checked 28
October 2026):

- **Combined county offices in the web address.** 159 California position
  pages carry a county office where a place name would sit, such as
  `ca/el-dorado-county/county-sheriff/coroner/position/public-administrator-joint`.
  That is how election-api names combined offices. The pages' titles and
  breadcrumbs are correct, so nothing needs fixing.
- **The combined statewide office**
  `ca/state-insurance-commissioner/position/fire-safety-commissioner-joint`
  named the state twice and linked a breadcrumb to a page that does not exist.
  That was our code. PR #518 fixes it, along with five similar pages in other
  states, once it merges and is released.
- **Duplicate school district names.** 11 district slugs carry an ID suffix to
  tell same-name districts apart, for example
  `ca/jefferson-elementary-school-district-0618870`, plus one odd slug,
  `ca/bishop-unified-school-district-9-12`. Never put the suffix in copy.

**Bulk-access recipe.** The Legislature publishes its whole database at
`https://downloads.leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/`. `pubinfo_<session year>.zip`
(e.g. `pubinfo_2025.zip`, about 1.2 GB, refreshed weekly) holds every code
section: `LAW_SECTION_TBL.dat` is a tab-separated index (law code, section,
history note, constitution article, file name) and each section's text is a
small `LAW_SECTION_TBL_<n>.lob` file in the same zip. Download with curl
(Python's default client gets 403), keep the rows with `ELEC`, `GOV`, `EDC` and
`CONS`, and the history column doubles as the amendment check.
`BILL_VERSION_TBL.dat` with status `Chaptered` lists the session's enacted
bills, the quickest way to find new laws not yet merged into the codes.
**leginfo.legislature.ca.gov is browser only**: it sits behind a Cloudflare
check that returns 403 to scripts, so `verify-ledger.ts` cannot fetch it. Run
`verify-ledger.ts --browser` and paste the snippet into any leginfo page in the
in-app browser. Ledger URLs are single sections:
`/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=ELEC&sectionNum=1000.`, and for the
constitution `lawCode=CONS&sectionNum=SEC.%206.&article=II`.

Aggregator sites were not used and must not appear in published copy. Statutes
may be cited by name and number.
