# Nebraska: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Nebraska so you do not have to research the same
ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which
offices are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race
data, which always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean. Every claim below has a verbatim
quote in [`research/nebraska.json`](./research/nebraska.json).

**The one thing to know first:** Nebraska's famous one-house, nonpartisan
Legislature says nothing about local races. **Counties are partisan.** Cities,
villages, school boards, township boards and most districts are nonpartisan.
Almost every local office is on the even-year statewide ballot, with the
primary in May and the general in November. The two big exceptions are Omaha
and Lincoln, whose city elections run on their own odd-year spring schedule by
default. Always check whether the page is a county, township, city, village or
district before writing a sentence about elections.

## Legislative calendar

Next regular session: convenes 6 January 2027 (10 a.m. on the first Wednesday
after the first Monday in January each year, Neb. Const. art. III, § 10). The
session is limited to 90 legislative days in odd-numbered years and 60 in
even-numbered years, unless four-fifths of the members vote to extend it, so the
2027 session usually ends in late May or early June.

The governor has five days, Sundays excepted, to act on a bill. A bill caught by
adjournment must be filed with the governor's objections within five days after
adjournment, or it becomes law (Neb. Const. art. IV, § 15). So the session's
laws are settled about a week after it ends. New laws take effect three calendar
months after adjournment, unless two-thirds of the members pass an emergency
clause (Neb. Const. art. III, § 27).

## How elections work in Nebraska

### Election days

Nearly every local office is filled at the **statewide elections**: the primary
on the first Tuesday after the second Monday in May of even-numbered years, and
the general on the first Tuesday after the first Monday in November of
even-numbered years.

*Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 32-401, 32-403 · statutory · read in statute*

By tier:

- **County offices** (commissioners or supervisors, clerk, treasurer, sheriff,
  county attorney and the rest) are nominated in May and elected in November of
  even years, on staggered four-year terms.
  *§§ 32-517 to 32-529 · statutory · read in statute*
- **Township boards** (in township counties only) are elected in November every
  four years, on the cycle that started in 1994, so in the years the governor is
  elected. There is no primary for them.
  *§§ 32-530, 32-606(2) · statutory · read in statute*
- **Villages** elect their trustees at the November statewide election, with no
  primary.
  *§§ 32-532, 32-606(2) · statutory · read in statute*
- **Cities** that do not have a home rule charter must elect their officers with
  the statewide primary and general election.
  *§ 32-556 · statutory · read in statute*
- **Home rule charter cities** hold their elections as the charter says, within
  the Election Act, and may join the statewide dates. Omaha and Lincoln are the
  cases that matter (see the exceptions below).
  *§ 32-556 · varies by place · read in statute*
- **School boards** in ordinary districts (Class I, II and III) are nominated in
  May and elected in November of even years. Omaha's district (Class V) also
  uses the statewide dates. Lincoln's district (Class IV) votes with the Lincoln
  city council.
  *§§ 32-543, 32-544, 32-545 · statutory · read in statute*
- **Other districts** (natural resources districts, community colleges,
  educational service units, public power districts) are on the statewide
  ballot. Local hospital district boards are elected at the May primary itself.
  *§§ 32-512 to 32-515, 32-550 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never write that Nebraska local elections are held in odd years
or in the spring. That is true only for Omaha and Lincoln city races (and the
Lincoln school board). Everywhere else, local races share the even-year state
ballot.

### Can a place move its election?

A county, village, township or ordinary city cannot. Only a home rule charter
city can set its own schedule, and state law spells out the choices for the
two largest: Omaha may move its city elections to presidential years by charter,
and Lincoln may move its general city election to the November statewide
election by charter. Special elections are also barred in most months of
even-numbered years.

*Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 32-556, 14-201(2), 15-301, 32-405 · statutory · read in statute*

### Primary type

**Party members vote their own party's primary; unaffiliated voters get only the
nonpartisan ballot, unless a party opens its primary to them.** Every voter at
the May primary gets the nonpartisan ballot, which carries the city, village,
school and district races. A voter registered with a party also gets that
party's ballot. Since 2026, a party may file a rule letting unaffiliated voters
vote in its primary, but the rule covers only the next primary. Separately, an
unaffiliated voter may always ask for one party's ballot for the U.S. Senate and
House races.

*Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 32-912, 32-702.01 · statutory · read in statute*

A party may also require its primary candidates to be registered party members.

*§ 32-702 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not call the primary simply "closed" or "open". Write that
registered party members choose their party's nominees, and that whether
unaffiliated voters may join in depends on the party's rule for that year. Never
say which party has opened its primary; that changes every cycle.

### Partisanship

- **Counties:** partisan. Commissioners, supervisors and every elected row
  office (clerk, treasurer, sheriff, county attorney, assessor and so on) run on
  the partisan ballot.
  *§§ 32-517 to 32-529 · statutory · read in statute*
- **Township boards:** nonpartisan.
  *§ 32-530 · statutory · read in statute*
- **Cities and villages:** nonpartisan by default, but a city or village may
  adopt a partisan ballot by ordinance.
  *§ 32-557 · usual case · read in statute*
- **School boards:** nonpartisan, always. The ordinance option covers cities and
  villages only.
  *§ 32-557 · statutory · read in statute*
- **Districts** (natural resources, community college, educational service
  unit, public power, hospital, reclamation): nonpartisan.
  *§§ 32-512 to 32-516, 32-550 · statutory · read in statute*
- **The Legislature:** nonpartisan, by the constitution. Context only; our pages
  are local.
  *Neb. Const. art. III, § 7 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** the most common mistake about Nebraska is to stretch the
nonpartisan Legislature to everything else. County races are partisan. For a
city or village, write "Nebraska city races are usually nonpartisan" and let
race data show if a party label appears.

### Plurality, not majority

The candidate with the most votes wins. No runoff exists for local offices.

*Neb. Rev. Stat. § 32-1033 · statutory · read in statute*

For nonpartisan offices with a primary, the top two vote-getters for each seat
go on to the November ballot. When no more than two candidates per seat file for
city, school and some district offices, the primary is skipped for that race.
Unopposed party candidates for county office also skip the primary ballot.

*§§ 32-814(2), 32-811(1) · statutory · read in statute*

### Getting on the ballot without a party

For a **partisan** office (any county office), a candidate who did not run in the
primary and was not registered with a party on or after March 1 of the election
year may reach the November ballot by petition. The ballot then shows "By
Petition" after the name.

*Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 32-616(1), 32-617(3) · statutory · read in statute*

Signers must be registered voters living in the district, and nobody may sign
twice for the same petition. No signer is barred because of how they voted in
the primary or because of their party.

*§§ 32-617(1), 32-629, 32-630(3) · statutory · read in statute*

For **nonpartisan** offices there is no "independent" route because there is no
party route. Every candidate files for the nonpartisan ballot, and a petition
route to the November ballot also exists.

*§§ 32-617(1), 32-618 · statutory · read in statute*

## County government

93 counties, each with a page (`research/pages-by-state.csv`). The statutes
describe each one, Adams through York. The constitution allows counties and
other local governments to merge, but only with a majority vote in each place
being merged. No merged city-county government was found in the statutes read
for this doc.

*Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 22-101 to 22-193; Neb. Const. art. XV, § 18 · statutory · read in statute*

### Two kinds of county

Nebraska has **commissioner counties** and **township counties**. Any county may
adopt township organization by a vote at a general election, and may vote it
away again later.

*Neb. Const. art. IX, § 5; Neb. Rev. Stat. § 23-148(2) · statutory · read in statute*

- **Commissioner county:** a **board of county commissioners**,
  three members by default. Voters may choose five. A county over 400,000 people
  has seven. Commissioners serve staggered four-year terms, are elected by
  district, and run on the partisan ballot.
  *§§ 23-148, 23-151, 32-528 · usual case · read in statute*
- **Township county:** a seven-member **board of supervisors**, one per
  supervisor district, four-year terms, partisan. Inside the county, each
  township has its own three-member township board (see below).
  *§§ 23-202, 32-529 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** in Nebraska, "board of supervisors" is right only for a township
county. Do not use it for a commissioner county, and do not state a board's size
for a named county. Let race data supply both.

### Other elected county offices

Every county elects a **clerk, treasurer, sheriff and county attorney**, each on
the partisan ballot for four years. Other offices depend on the county's size or
choices: an **assessor** in counties over 3,500 people unless the voters have
handed the job to the clerk, a **register of deeds** only over 20,000, a **clerk
of the district court** at 7,000 and up (or when chosen in smaller counties), a
**public defender** over 100,000 or when the board approves, plus a surveyor or
engineer in some counties. A county may also merge several of these into one
office by a vote.

*Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 32-517 to 32-526, 22-417 · usual case · read in statute*

Never list which offices a named county elects from this doc. Race data has it.

### Charters

Nebraska counties cannot adopt a charter. The constitution leaves county and
township offices to the Legislature, so county elections are always state law.

*Neb. Const. art. IX, § 4 · statutory · read in statute*

### Townships

Townships exist **only in township counties**. Each township elects a board of
three officers, in nonpartisan races, at the November election every four years.
The three winners decide among themselves who is clerk, treasurer and chair.
Township electors also meet once a year at an annual town meeting.

*Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 32-530, 23-224 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not call these officers "trustees" or "supervisors". They are
the township board: a clerk, a treasurer and a chair. And never give a township
page in a commissioner county; if one exists, it is a data problem.

## City and village government

Nebraska sorts municipalities into five classes by population.

*Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 14-101, 15-101, 16-101, 17-101, 17-201 · statutory · read in statute*

| Class | Population | Governing body |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Metropolitan class city | 400,000 or more (Omaha) | Mayor and seven council members by district |
| Primary class city | over 100,000, under 400,000 (Lincoln) | Council elected at the city election; charter sets the rest |
| First class city | over 5,000, up to 100,000 | Mayor and council, unless commission or manager plan |
| Second class city | over 800, up to 5,000 | Mayor and a council of 4 to 12 |
| Village | 100 to 800 | Board of trustees, three or five members |

### Villages

A village is run by a **board of
trustees** of three or five members, elected at the November statewide election
for four-year terms, with no primary. The board chooses its own **chairperson**
and appoints the clerk, treasurer and other officers. A village has no elected
mayor.

*Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 17-202, 17-204, 17-208, 32-532, 32-606(2) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never write that a village elects a mayor or a city council.
Our position labels call the village board "city legislature". Call it the
village board of trustees.

### Second class cities

An elected **mayor** and a **city council** of 4 to 12 members, usually two per
ward. Every elected officer is nominated at the May primary and elected in
November for four years.

*Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 17-103, 17-104, 17-107, 32-533 · usual case · read in statute*

### First class cities

By default, an elected **mayor** at large and a **council** of at least four,
elected by ward or partly at large, for four-year terms. The council may make the
clerk and treasurer elected offices by a two-thirds vote. A first class city may
instead run under the **commission plan** (open to cities of 2,000 or more) or
the **city manager plan** (cities of 1,000 to under 200,000), where an elected
council hires a manager.

*Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 32-534, 16-302.01, 19-401, 19-602, 32-538 · usual case · read in statute*

Do not state a city's form of government, council size or whether it has wards.
Let race data show which seats are on the ballot.

### Home rule

Any city over 5,000 may frame a **home rule charter**, "consistent with and
subject to" state law. A city over 100,000 may adopt its existing charter as a
home rule charter.

*Neb. Const. art. XI, §§ 2, 5 · statutory · read in statute*

Does home rule reach elections? Partly. The Election Act declares elections and
candidate qualifications a matter of statewide concern regardless of any
charter. But it also lets a charter city run its own elections except where the
act says otherwise, set the council size, and set its own filing deadlines.

*Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 32-501, 32-556, 32-537, 32-606(3) · varies by place · read in statute*

So for a home rule city, the timing and council size can differ from the
general rules above. Write about it only from race data.

### Does class follow population?

**Mostly, yes, with opt-outs.** A second class city that passes 5,000 becomes a
first class city by proclamation. A village that passes 800 may vote to stay a
village, and a second class city may vote to become one. Shrinking works the
same way: a first class city that falls under 4,000 becomes second class, and a
second class city that falls under 800 may choose by ordinance to stay a city.

*Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 16-102, 17-306, 17-312, 17-301, 17-310 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not infer a place's class from its size. A place of 900
people may be a village or a city. Use what race data shows (a mayor and council
means a city; a board of trustees means a village).

### Can terms or council size change locally?

Within limits. Voters may switch a city (not home rule), village, county or
school board between at-large and ward or district elections. A village may
move between three and five trustees. A county may move from three to five
commissioners by a vote. Terms are four years for nearly every local office and
are set by state law, not locally.

*Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 32-554, 17-202, 23-148 · statutory · read in statute*

### School and special districts

School boards are nonpartisan everywhere.

*§ 32-557 · statutory · read in statute*

- **Class I, II and III** districts (everything outside Lincoln and Omaha): 5 to
  9 members on staggered four-year terms, about half elected each even-year
  November. A few districts in the Omaha-area learning community nominate by
  caucus instead and elect a six-member board at the May primary.
  *§§ 32-543, 79-549 · usual case · read in statute*
- **Class IV** (the district containing Lincoln): members elected by district,
  with the Lincoln city council.
  *§§ 32-544, 79-102 · statutory · read in statute*
- **Class V** (the district containing Omaha): members elected by district at
  the even-year statewide elections.
  *§§ 32-545, 79-102 · statutory · read in statute*

Other elected boards include natural resources districts (four-year terms),
community colleges, educational service units, public power districts and local
hospital districts, all nonpartisan. A city airport authority board has five
members serving six-year terms, elected like the city's own officers.

*§§ 32-512 to 32-515, 32-547, 32-550 · statutory · read in statute*

## Metro and major-place exceptions

Nebraska has 1,089 location pages: 93 county, 242 district and 753 municipal
(`research/pages-by-state.csv`). About 208 of the municipal pages are townships
(slug ending in `-township`), in 20 counties. The rest are villages and cities,
and the slug does not say which.

### Omaha and Douglas County

`ne/douglas-county/omaha`, `ne/douglas-county`, `ne/omaha-public-schools`

Omaha is Nebraska's only metropolitan class city and governs itself under a home
rule charter. It elects a **mayor and seven council members by district** for
four-year terms. By default its primary is the first Tuesday of April and its
general election is the first Tuesday after the second Monday in May, every four
years on the cycle from 1993 (odd years). A 2025 law lets Omaha's charter move
these to the statewide elections of presidential years instead.

*Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 14-101 (annotation), 14-201, 32-536 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not state the year or month of Omaha's next city election from
this doc. Whether the city has used its new option was not checked. Let race
data supply it.

Douglas County, being over 400,000, has seven commissioners elected by district,
partisan.

*§§ 23-151, 32-528 · statutory · read in statute*

### Lincoln and Lancaster County

`ne/lancaster-county/lincoln`, `ne/lancaster-county`, `ne/lincoln-public-schools`

Lincoln is a primary class city with a home rule charter. A primary class
city's general city election is the first Tuesday in May of odd-numbered years,
unless its home rule charter moves it to the November statewide election. The
Lincoln school district (Class IV) votes at the same time as the city council.

*Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 15-101 and 15-104 (annotations), 15-301, 32-535, 32-544 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** Lincoln is in **Lancaster County**, not Lincoln County. North
Platte (`ne/lincoln-county/north-platte`) is the city our data files under
Lincoln County.

### Other large cities

### Cities with "City" in the name

`ne/otoe-county/nebraska-city`, `ne/butler-county/david-city`,

## Writing rules

These are the ways Nebraska copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never say Nebraska local elections are nonpartisan.** Counties are partisan.
   Cities and villages are nonpartisan unless they adopt a partisan ballot.
   School boards, township boards and districts are always nonpartisan.
2. **Never let the nonpartisan Legislature stand for local races.** Mention it
   only as state context, if at all.
3. **Local races are on the even-year state ballot**, primary in May, general in
   November. Omaha and Lincoln city races are the exceptions.
4. **Check the page type before writing.** A township page ends in `-township`
   and sits in a township county. A village has a board of trustees, not a mayor.
5. **Watch for same-name places.** There are 60 same-name pairs inside one county
   in our data. 42 are a village or city and its township
   (`ne/clay-county/sutton` and `ne/clay-county/sutton-township`), which are
   separate governments. The other 18 are duplicate city pages (see Data notes).
   In our data, township pages appear in 20 of the 93 counties.
6. **Never infer a county from a place name.** 26 Nebraska place names match a
   county name, and 10 sit in a different county: Kearney (Buffalo County),
   Valley (Douglas County), Adams (Gage County), Lincoln (Lancaster County),
   Johnson (Nemaha County), Douglas (Otoe County), Grant (Perkins County), Dawson
   (Richardson County), Morrill (Scotts Bluff County) and Thayer (York County).
   Always take the county from the page.
7. Say "board of supervisors" only for a township county, and "township board"
   (not trustees) for a township.
8. Do not state a city's form of government, council size, wards, or whether its
   clerk is elected.
9. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
10. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
    contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures, and which party has
    opened its primary.

### What search results get wrong about Nebraska

- "Nebraska elections are nonpartisan": only the Legislature and local
  nonpartisan offices. County races are partisan.
- "Nebraska has a closed primary": party members vote their own primary, and a
  party may open its primary to unaffiliated voters for one cycle at a time.
  Unaffiliated voters may always take one party ballot for Congress.
- "The village mayor": villages have a board chair chosen by the trustees.
- "Omaha votes in May of odd years": still the default, but a 2025 law lets the
  charter move Omaha to presidential-year November elections.
- "Lincoln is in Lincoln County": it is in Lancaster County.

  three township board officers are labeled `township-trustee-township-council`.
  Three municipal pages carry a `local-school-board` position.

**Bulk access recipe.** A range of statute sections comes back as one page:
`https://nebraskalegislature.gov/laws/display_html.php?begin_section=<first>&end_section=<last>`
(for example `32-101` to `32-1701`). Both ends must be real sections, or the page
returns an error. Take the section list for a chapter from
`https://nebraskalegislature.gov/laws/browse-chapters.php?chapter=<n>`, and fetch
about 100 sections per request, a few seconds apart; 200 at once occasionally
timed out. Each section shows its source acts and any operative date, so the
currency check is a search for the latest year. A single section is
`https://nebraskalegislature.gov/laws/statutes.php?statute=<section>`, which is
what the ledger cites. Constitution sections are
`https://nebraskalegislature.gov/laws/articles.php?article=<article>-<section>`
(for example `III-10`). Plain scripts with a browser user agent work; no
blocking, no certificate problems.
