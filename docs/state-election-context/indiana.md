# Indiana: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Indiana so you do not have to research the same ground
on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which offices are
actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race data, which
always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean. Every claim below has a verbatim quote
in [`research/indiana.json`](./research/indiana.json).

**The one thing to know first:** Indiana runs two election calendars. County and
township offices are elected in November of even-numbered years. City and most
town offices are elected in November of the odd-numbered year before each
presidential election. Almost every local office is partisan, with a party
primary, including the township trustee. School boards are the exception: no
primary, and since a 2025 law the candidate chooses whether a party label appears
next to their name.

## Legislative calendar

Next regular session: convenes 17 November 2026 for Organization Day (the third
Tuesday after the first Monday of November in even-numbered years, Ind. Code
§ 2-2.1-1-2(a)), then reconvenes no later than 25 January 2027 (the fourth Monday
in January, § 2-2.1-1-2(d)). The Constitution's January start date applies unless
law sets another day, which this statute does (Ind. Const. art. 4, § 9). The
odd-year session must adjourn by 29 April (§ 2-2.1-1-2(e)). The governor has seven
days after a bill is presented to act on it, and an unsigned bill becomes law on
the eighth day (Ind. Const. art. 5, § 14). A one-day technical session on 15 June
2027 (the second Tuesday after the first Monday in June, § 2-2.1-1-2.5) can
override vetoes. New laws usually take effect on 1 July (Ind. Code § 1-1-3-3).
Special sessions last at most 30 session days (§ 2-2.1-1-4).

## How elections work in Indiana

Safe on any Indiana page, at the tier named.

### Election days

**County and township** offices are elected at the general election, held on the
first Tuesday after the first Monday in November of even-numbered years. Each
office is elected every four years. The party primary is held on the first Tuesday
after the first Monday in May of the same year.

*Ind. Code §§ 3-10-2-1, 3-10-1-3, 3-10-2-13 · statutory · read in statute*

**Cities, towns of 3,500 or more, and every town in Marion County** hold a
municipal election every four years, on the first Tuesday after the first Monday
in November of the odd-numbered year before a presidential election. The
municipal primary is in May of that year. All of a city's offices are elected at
once.

*Ind. Code §§ 3-10-6-1, 3-10-6-2(a), 3-10-6-5 · statutory · read in statute*

**Towns under 3,500 outside Marion County** usually follow the same odd-year
schedule, but a town council can adopt an ordinance moving some or all town
offices to even-year general elections, or staggering council terms. A town
election can never be held in the year right after a presidential election, and
an ordinance moving the town's elections cannot be repealed for twelve years. A
town over 10,000 people can also choose a seven-member council elected in two
different years.

*Ind. Code §§ 3-10-6-2.5, 3-10-7-2.9, 3-10-7-3, 36-5-2-4.5 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never write that a town votes in an odd year, or in an even year,
unless race data gives the date. Say "in its municipal election".

**School boards** that are elected are chosen at the November general election in
even-numbered years. Not every school corporation's board is elected: the law
applies "if a plan provides for election".

*Ind. Code § 20-23-4-29.1(b) · usual case · read in statute*

**Can a place move its own election date?** Not cities or counties. Indiana's home
rule law withholds any power over elections unless a statute expressly grants it.
The only statutory switches are the town ordinances above.

*Ind. Code § 36-1-3-8(a)(12) · statutory · read in statute*

### Primary type

Indiana has no party registration. A voter asks for one party's primary ballot.
The law says a voter may vote in a party's primary if they voted for most of that
party's nominees at the last general election, or intend to at the next one. A
voter can be challenged at the polls on party affiliation.

*Ind. Code §§ 3-10-1-6, 3-10-1-10 · statutory · read in statute*

Voting in a primary has a lasting effect for anyone who later wants to run. A
candidate's party affiliation is set by the two most recent primaries they voted
in, unless the county party chair certifies them.

*Ind. Code § 3-8-2-7(a)(4) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not call Indiana's primary simply "open" or "closed". Say voters
choose a party's ballot at the polls, with no party registration.

### Partisanship

**County, township, city and town races are partisan.** County offices, township
trustee and township board, and city and town offices all appear on the party
primary ballot, and the parties nominate every candidate for the November
municipal election in the municipal primary.

*Ind. Code §§ 3-10-1-19(g), 3-10-6-2(b) · statutory · read in statute*

**Small towns nominate by party convention, not a primary.** Towns under 3,500
outside Marion County pick major party candidates at a town convention, unless the
council has adopted a primary by ordinance. The races are still partisan.

*Ind. Code §§ 3-8-5-1, 3-8-5-2(c) · usual case · read in statute*

**School board races have no primary, and party labels are the candidate's
choice.** Every candidate files a petition. On it they state a party affiliation,
that they are independent, or that they choose not to disclose any affiliation,
and that statement appears on the ballot. A straight-party vote does not count
for any school board candidate.

*Ind. Code §§ 3-8-2.5-2(a), 3-8-2.5-2.5(a)(5), 3-12-1-7.1 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** older sources call Indiana school board races nonpartisan. Since the
2025 change, do not describe them as nonpartisan or as partisan. Say candidates
run without a primary and may list a party.

### Winning an election

Plurality: the candidate with the most votes wins. Indiana has no runoffs for
local office.

*Ind. Code §§ 3-12-4-9, 20-23-4-29.1(d) · statutory · read in statute*

### Running as an independent

An independent, or a candidate of a party without primary access, gets on the
ballot by petition. Anyone registered at their address and qualified to vote for
the office may sign. The law does not bar primary voters from signing.

*Ind. Code §§ 3-8-6-1, 3-8-6-2 · statutory · read in statute*

A candidate who loses a party primary or convention cannot then run for the same
office at the next general or municipal election.

*Ind. Code § 3-8-1-5.5 · statutory · read in statute*

## County government

Our data has a page for each of Indiana's 92 counties. There are no county
charters: every county has the same structure, and home rule (which covers
counties, cities, towns and townships alike) gives no power over elections.

*Ind. Code §§ 36-1-2-23, 36-1-3-4, 36-1-3-8(a)(12) · statutory · read in statute*

Indiana splits county government between two elected bodies:

- **The board of commissioners**, three members, is the county executive.
  Commissioners serve four-year staggered terms, with one or two elected at each
  general election.
- **The county council**, normally seven members, is the county fiscal body: it
  controls the budget. Four members are usually elected from districts and three
  at large, for four-year terms.

*Ind. Code §§ 36-2-2-2, 36-2-2-3, 36-2-3-2(a), 36-2-3-3(b), 36-2-3-4(a) · usual case · read in statute*

A few of the largest counties elect their whole council from single-member
districts instead. The statute picks these counties by population band, so take
the seats from race data.

*Ind. Code § 36-2-3-4(b)-(c) · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not call the county council the county's governing body on its
own, or the commissioners a legislature. Commissioners run the county; the council
holds the purse.

Every county also elects a **clerk of the circuit court, auditor, recorder,
treasurer, sheriff, coroner and surveyor**, each for four years, plus a **county
assessor**. The clerk, auditor, recorder, treasurer, sheriff and coroner may serve
no more than eight years in any twelve. All are partisan.

*Ind. Const. art. 6, § 2; Ind. Code § 3-10-2-13 · statutory · read in statute*

### Townships

Every county is divided into civil townships, and each township is its own
elected government. A township elects:

- a **township trustee**, the township executive, for four years, and
- a **township board** of three members (five in Marion County), the township's
  legislative body, for four years.

Both are partisan and elected at the even-year general election. A few townships
also still elect a township assessor.

*Ind. Code §§ 36-6-4-2, 36-6-6-2, 3-10-2-13 · statutory · read in statute*

**Townships are being merged.** A 2026 law has the state score every township
outside Marion County, and every township that scores as "designated" must merge
with another township in its county. All of these mergers take effect by
1 January 2029, and a merged township elects a three-member board at large
starting with the 2030 general election. Which townships merge is not settled
until each county executive names them, by 1 October 2027.

*Ind. Code §§ 36-6-1.7-1, 36-6-1.7-14, 36-6-1.7-16(b), 36-6-1.7-19, 36-6-6-2.1(c) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** a township page may describe a government that will not exist in
its current form after 2028. Do not write that a township "will continue" or
describe its future boundaries. Re-check this section when the 2027 merger lists
are final.

A township is not a town. In 61 cases our data has a town or city page and a
township page with the same base name in the same county (Noblesville and Noblesville
Township, Lawrence and Lawrence Township). They are separate governments with
separate offices.

## City and town government

Indiana has two kinds of municipality: **cities** and **towns**.

*Ind. Code § 36-1-2-11 · statutory · read in statute*

### Cities

Cities are classed by population: first class (600,000 or more), second class (34,000 to 599,999) and third class (under 34,000).
**Class does not follow population automatically.** A second class city stays
second class if its population falls, and a third class city stays third class
when it grows past 34,000, unless its council votes to change.

*Ind. Code §§ 36-4-1-1, 36-4-1-1.1 · statutory · read in statute*

Every city elects a **mayor** (the city executive) and a **common council**, all
for four-year terms.

*Ind. Code §§ 36-4-5-2, 36-4-6-2 · statutory · read in statute*

- **Second class cities** elect a nine-member council (six from districts, three
  at large) and a **city clerk**.
- **Third class cities** elect a council of usually seven (five from districts,
  two at large) and a **clerk-treasurer**. Some third class cities use four
  districts and three at large.

*Ind. Code §§ 36-4-6-3(h), 36-4-6-4(h), 36-4-6-4(k), 36-4-10-2(a) · usual case · read in statute*

### Towns

A town elects a **town council** of three to seven members and a
**clerk-treasurer**, normally for four-year terms. **Towns have no mayor.** The
council picks one of its members as president, who serves as the town executive.

*Ind. Code §§ 36-5-2-2, 36-5-2-3(a), 36-5-2-4.2, 36-5-6-2 · usual case · read in statute*

A town becomes a city only by a vote of its residents, and a town under 2,000
people cannot become one.

*Ind. Code §§ 36-4-1.5-1, 36-4-1.5-2 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not infer class from the name. Several Indiana places have
"City" in their name, and our slugs end in `-city` for them (`michigan-city`,
`columbia-city`, `union-city`). That is the place's name, not its class.

### Home rule

There are no city or town charters in Indiana. Home rule is a single statute
that applies to every county, city, town and township, and it reserves elections
to state law. So timing, partisanship and the plurality rule are flat-assertable
for every Indiana city. Council size and districts are not, because the statutes
themselves offer options.

*Ind. Code §§ 36-1-3-1, 36-1-3-8(a)(12) · statutory · read in statute*

## Metro and major-place exceptions

### Indianapolis and Marion County

`in/marion-county`

Indianapolis and Marion County share one consolidated government. The
consolidated city covers all of Marion County except a handful of excluded
cities and towns. A single **mayor**, elected countywide, is the executive of both
the city and the county. A **city-county council** of 25 members, all elected
from districts, is the legislative body of both. Both are elected in the odd-year
municipal election.

*Ind. Code §§ 36-3-1-4(a), 36-3-3-2, 36-3-4-2, 36-3-4-3 · statutory · read in statute*

Marion County still elects its constitutional county officers (clerk, sheriff,
treasurer and the rest). It has no elected board of commissioners: the treasurer,
auditor and assessor serve as commissioners ex officio.

*Ind. Code § 36-3-1-5(b) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** our data has no Indianapolis page. The mayor and city-county
council appear on the Marion County page, under county position names. Copy for
that page must describe the consolidated government, not a separate county
executive and county council.

**Excluded cities.** Lawrence, Beech Grove and Southport (cities) and Speedway (a
town) are inside Marion County but not part of Indianapolis, and elect their own
governments. From 1 January 2027 the law lists the excluded places by name:
those four plus the town of Cumberland, which our data files under Hancock County.

*Ind. Code § 36-3-1-7 · statutory · read in statute*

`in/marion-county/lawrence`, `in/marion-county/beech-grove`,
`in/marion-county/southport`, `in/marion-county/speedway`,
`in/hancock-county/cumberland`

Marion County's nine townships each have a trustee and a five-member township
board. They are not affected by the 2026 merger law.

*Ind. Code §§ 36-6-6-2(b), 36-6-1.7-1 · statutory · read in statute*

### Big second class cities

`in/allen-county/fort-wayne`, `in/vanderburgh-county/evansville`,
`in/st-joseph-county/south-bend`, `in/hamilton-county/carmel`,

Each of our pages files a city under one county, even where it extends into
others. Do not write that a city lies wholly within the county on its page.

## Writing rules

These are the ways Indiana copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never infer a county from a place name.** 14 of our Indiana municipal pages
   share a name with a different Indiana county. Franklin is in Johnson County,
   Marion is in Grant County, Decatur and Monroe are in Adams County, Jasper is in
   Dubois County, and Lawrence is in Marion County. The full list is in
   [`research/places-named-after-another-county.json`](./research/places-named-after-another-county.json).
   Always take the county from the page data.
2. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
3. **A township is not a town or city.** On Indiana pages a slug ending in
   `-township` is always the township government. Never merge a township and the
   town or city of the same name.
4. **Never call a township trustee a mayor**, even though our position label for
   the trustee is "township mayor". The trustee is the township's executive. The
   label "township trustee" in our data is the township board.
5. **Never give a town a mayor.** If a page has no mayor race it may be a town;
   if it has one, it is a city.
6. **Do not call local races nonpartisan.** Everything except school boards is
   partisan. School board candidates choose whether to show a party.
7. Do not call Indiana's primary simply open or closed.
8. Do not state whether a town votes in an odd or even year unless race data gives
   the date. Cities always vote in the odd year.
9. Do not describe the future of a township, or its boundaries after 2028.
10. Do not infer a city's class from its name or population.
11. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
    contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.
