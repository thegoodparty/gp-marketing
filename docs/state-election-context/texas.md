# Texas: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Texas so you do not have to research the same ground
on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which offices are
actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race data, which
always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean. Every claim below has a verbatim quote
in [`research/texas.json`](./research/texas.json).

**The one thing to know first:** Texas splits cleanly by tier. County races are
partisan, with party primaries. City races are nonpartisan unless a home rule
city's charter says otherwise. School board and special district races are always
nonpartisan. Copy that treats "local" as one thing will be wrong somewhere on
almost every Texas page.

## Legislative calendar

Next regular session: convenes 12 January 2027 (noon on the second Tuesday in
January of odd-numbered years, Tex. Gov't Code § 301.001). The Legislature meets
in regular session only in odd-numbered years, for at most 140 days (Tex. Const.
art. III, § 24(b)). New laws usually take effect 90 days after adjournment unless
two-thirds of each house vote otherwise (Tex. Const. art. III, § 39). The governor
can also convene the Legislature in special session (Tex. Const. art. III, § 5).

## How elections work in Texas

Safe on any Texas page, at the tier named.

### Election days

Texas holds elections only on a short list of uniform dates. For local
governments these are the first Saturday in May and the first Tuesday after the
first Monday in November. An election held on any other date is void, apart from
narrow exceptions such as runoffs and emergencies.

*Tex. Elec. Code §§ 41.001, 41.008 · statutory · read in statute*

**County** officers are elected at the November general election in
even-numbered years, alongside state offices.

*Tex. Elec. Code § 41.002 · statutory · read in statute*

**Cities** elect their officers on a uniform date, either in May or in November.
Which one a given city uses varies. A home rule city can move to November by
resolution even if its charter says otherwise. The general window for that switch closed on 31 December 2025, but
narrower provisions remain.

*Tex. Elec. Code § 41.0052 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never say a Texas city votes in May, or in November, unless race
data gives the date. Say "on one of the state's uniform election dates".

**School boards** must hold trustee elections on the same day as a city election
in the district or the November general election, among a few other options.
Trustees serve staggered three- or four-year terms, so part of a board is elected
every year or every other year.

*Tex. Educ. Code §§ 11.0581(a), 11.059 · statutory · read in statute*

### Primary type

Open in the sense that Texas has no party registration. Any voter can choose
either party's primary. Voting in a primary affiliates the voter with that party
until the end of the year, and they cannot then vote in another party's primary
or runoff that year.

*Tex. Elec. Code §§ 162.003, 162.010, 162.013 · statutory · read in statute*

The general primary is the first Tuesday in March of even-numbered years, with a
runoff primary on the fourth Tuesday in May.

*Tex. Elec. Code § 41.007 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** if a source says Texas primaries are closed, check it. As of
October 2026 the statute still affiliates voters by the act of voting, with no
party registration. Do not call Texas primaries closed.

### Partisanship

**County races are partisan.** The major parties must nominate county candidates
by primary, and those candidates run with a party label in November.

*Tex. Elec. Code § 172.001 · statutory · read in statute*

**City races are nonpartisan by default.** A city candidate's name may appear on
the ballot only as an independent, unless the city is a home rule city whose
charter allows partisan nominations.

*Tex. Elec. Code §§ 143.002, 143.003 · usual case · read in statute*

**School board and special district races are always nonpartisan.** For any
local body other than a city or county, candidates appear only as independents.
No charter or local choice can change this.

*Tex. Elec. Code § 144.002 · statutory · read in statute*

### Winning an election

Plurality is the default: the candidate with the most votes wins. A runoff
happens only where a majority is required.

*Tex. Elec. Code §§ 2.001, 2.021 · statutory · read in statute*

A majority is required in any city that has lengthened its terms beyond two
years. Home rule charters can also require a majority, and a school board can
require one by resolution. So runoffs are common in Texas city and school
elections but cannot be assumed for any one place.

*Tex. Const. art. XI, § 11(b); Tex. Educ. Code § 11.057(c) · varies by place · read in statute*

### Running as an independent

For **county** office, an independent files a declaration of intent and a
petition. Anyone who voted in a party's primary cannot sign the petition for an
office that party nominated a candidate for. A person who voted in a primary also
cannot then run as an independent for an office that party nominated someone for.

*Tex. Elec. Code §§ 142.002, 142.004, 142.009, 162.015 · statutory · read in statute*

This is worth stating plainly on Texas pages. Voting in the March primary closes
off both independent candidacy and signing an independent's petition for that
office in November.

For **city, school board and special district** office, there is no party route
to begin with. Every candidate files an application and appears without a party
label (unless a home rule charter provides otherwise).

*Tex. Elec. Code §§ 143.002, 144.002 · statutory · read in statute*

## County government

Texas has 254 counties, and every one has the same basic structure. There is no county home rule in Texas (the constitutional
provision was repealed in 1969), so Harris County with millions of residents is
governed the same way as the smallest county.

*Tex. Const. art. IX, § 3 (repealed) · statutory · read in statute*

Each county is run by a **commissioners court**. Despite the name, it is the
county's governing body, not a court that tries cases. It has five members: a
**county judge** elected countywide, who presides, and four **county
commissioners**, each elected from one of four commissioners precincts. All serve
four-year terms.

*Tex. Const. art. V, §§ 15, 18(b) · statutory · read in statute*

Counties also elect a **sheriff**, and **justices of the peace and constables**
by precinct, all for four-year terms, along with other county officers. All are
partisan.

*Tex. Const. art. V, §§ 18(a), 23 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** the county judge heads the commissioners court, the county's
governing body, as well as presiding over the county court. Do not describe the
role as only a judge.

Texas has no townships. Below the county there are only municipalities and
special districts.

*Tex. Loc. Gov't Code §§ 5.001-5.005 · statutory · read in statute*

## City, town and village government

Texas law defines five kinds of municipality: Type A, Type B and Type C
general-law municipalities, home rule municipalities, and a few special-law
municipalities.

*Tex. Loc. Gov't Code §§ 5.001-5.005 · statutory · read in statute*

**The name does not tell you the type.** A Type A "town" can rename itself a
city by ordinance with no change in powers, and Type B places incorporate as
towns or villages. Never infer a type from "city", "town" or "village" in the
name.

*Tex. Loc. Gov't Code §§ 5.902, 7.001 · statutory · read in statute*

### Type A general-law

Needs 600 or more people to incorporate. Elects a **mayor and five aldermen**
at large, or a mayor and two aldermen per ward if the city uses wards. Two-year
terms by default. The candidate with the most votes wins.

*Tex. Loc. Gov't Code §§ 6.001, 22.004, 22.031, 22.035 · usual case · read in statute*

### Type B general-law

Smaller places, 201 to 9,999 people at incorporation. Elects a **mayor, five
aldermen and a marshal**. Terms are one year by default, or two-year staggered
terms by ordinance. A Type B place under 5,000 people may abolish the elected
marshal.

*Tex. Loc. Gov't Code §§ 7.001, 23.021, 23.026, 23.029 · usual case · read in statute*

### Type C general-law

Commission form: a **mayor and two commissioners**, two-year terms by default.

*Tex. Loc. Gov't Code §§ 24.021, 24.023 · usual case · read in statute*

### Home rule

A city over 5,000 people may adopt its own charter by a vote of its residents,
and it keeps that charter even if its population later falls. The charter sets
the form of government, council size, whether seats are by district or at large,
and whether city races can be partisan.

*Tex. Const. art. XI, § 5(a); Tex. Loc. Gov't Code § 26.021; Tex. Elec. Code § 143.003 · statutory · read in statute*

**Does home rule reach elections?** Partly. A charter may not conflict with
state law, so election dates stay on the state's uniform dates and filing
deadlines are set by the Election Code. But the Election Code itself lets a
charter allow partisan races, set candidate application requirements, and
require a majority vote. So timing is flat-assertable for every Texas city, and
partisanship and runoffs are not.

*Tex. Const. art. XI, § 5(a); Tex. Elec. Code §§ 41.008, 143.003, 143.005 · statutory · read in statute*

### Terms

Any Texas city, home rule or general law, may lengthen terms to at most four
years. Home rule cities do it by charter, general-law cities by a vote of their
residents. A city that does this must then elect its whole governing body by
majority vote.

*Tex. Const. art. XI, § 11(a)-(b) · statutory · read in statute*

## Metro and major-place exceptions

Texas has fewer structural exceptions than most states, because every county
shares one structure. The risks on big-city pages are naming and county
confusion.

### Houston

`tx/harris-county/houston`

A home rule city, so its council structure and the partisanship of its races are
set by its charter. Houston the city is **not** in Houston County, which is a
separate county. Our Crockett page sits inside Houston County.

*Tex. Const. art. XI, § 5(a) · statutory · read in statute*

### Austin

`tx/travis-county/austin`

A home rule city in Travis County, not in Austin County. Same charter caveats as
Houston.

### Harris, Dallas, Bexar, Tarrant and Travis counties

`tx/harris-county`, `tx/dallas-county`, `tx/bexar-county`, `tx/tarrant-county`,
`tx/travis-county`

The biggest counties have the same five-member commissioners court as every other
Texas county. Do not describe a county executive, county council or county
mayor. Texas has none of these.

*Tex. Const. art. V, § 18(b) · statutory · read in statute*

### Big cities across county lines

Each of our pages files a city under one county, even where the city extends
into others. Do not write that a city lies wholly within the county on its
page.

## Writing rules

These are the ways Texas copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never infer a county from a place name.** 25 of our Texas city pages share a
   name with a different Texas county. Houston is in Harris County, Austin is in
   Travis County, Tyler is in Smith County, Sherman is in Grayson County.
   The full list is in
   [`research/places-named-after-another-county.json`](./research/places-named-after-another-county.json).
   Always take the county from the page data.
2. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
3. **Do not call local races nonpartisan without naming the tier.** County races
   are partisan, city races are usually not, and school and district races never
   are.
4. Do not say whether a city votes in May or November unless race data gives the
   date.
5. Do not infer a city's type from "city", "town" or "village" in its name, or
   from its population.
6. Do not call Texas primaries closed.
7. Do not describe the county judge as only a judge, or the commissioners court
   as a court of law.
8. Do not assume a runoff. Plurality is the default, and runoffs depend on the
   place.
9. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
   contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.
