# Vermont: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Vermont so you do not have to research the same
ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which offices
are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race data, which
always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean. Every claim below has a verbatim quote
in [`research/vermont.json`](./research/vermont.json).

**The one thing to know first:** Vermont runs two election calendars. Towns
elect their officers at the annual town meeting on the first Tuesday of March,
every year. County offices (assistant judges, sheriff, state's attorney,
probate judge, high bailiff) and town justices of the peace are elected at the
November general election in even-numbered years, after an August primary, and
are partisan. Vermont counties have no elected governing board, and almost all
our Vermont place pages are towns.

## Legislative calendar

Next regular session: convenes 6 January 2027 (the first Wednesday after the
first Monday of January, every two years, Vt. Const. ch. II, § 7). The session
has no fixed end date in the constitution, and the General Assembly reconvenes
for a second year in January 2028.

The governor has five days, Sundays excepted, to act on a bill. A bill the
governor neither signs nor returns becomes law, unless the Legislature
adjourns within three days of presenting it, in which case it does not
(ch. II, § 11). So a session's bills are settled within about a week of
adjournment. Each act usually sets its own effective date (`usual case`).

## How elections work in Vermont

Safe on any Vermont page, at the tier named.

### Election days

**Towns** hold their annual meeting on the first Tuesday of March, every year,
to elect town officers and do other business. A town may open the meeting on
one of the three days before, but voting by ballot happens on the Tuesday.

*17 V.S.A. § 2640 · statutory · read in statute*

**County offices and justices of the peace** are elected at the November
general election (the first Tuesday after the first Monday) in even-numbered
years. Assistant judges, sheriffs, state's attorneys and probate judges serve
four-year terms.

*Vt. Const. ch. II, §§ 43, 50, 51 · statutory · read in statute*

**School boards.** The annual town meeting is also the town school district
meeting, though a district may move its meeting to a date between 1 February and
15 June. A town school district board has three directors, one elected each year
for three years, unless the town is part of a unified union district, which
elects its own board. Our Vermont school pages are mostly union and unified
union districts.

*16 V.S.A. §§ 422(a), 423(a) · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** Act 73 of 2025 set out to transform Vermont's education
governance. School district boundaries and boards may change. Avoid describing
how a Vermont school board is organized beyond what race data shows, and
re-check this section after the 2027 session.

*Act 73 (2025) · varies by place · read in statute*

### Primary type

The primary is on the second Tuesday in August in even-numbered years, for
major-party nominations. A voter picks one party's ballot privately, is not asked to state a party, and
may vote for that party's candidates only.

*17 V.S.A. §§ 2351, 2363(a) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** justices of the peace are not nominated in the primary. Each
party's town caucus nominates them.

*17 V.S.A. §§ 2351, 2413(a)(1) · statutory · read in statute*

### Partisanship

**County races and justices of the peace are partisan.** Candidates are
nominated by party primary (or, for justices of the peace, party caucus) or run
as independents.

*17 V.S.A. §§ 2351, 2413 · statutory · read in statute*

**Town races are usually decided at town meeting without party labels**, but
where a town elects by Australian ballot, a candidate files a petition and may
also file a party's endorsement, so a party name can appear. Avoid calling a
Vermont town race partisan or nonpartisan unless race data says which.

*17 V.S.A. §§ 2680(a), 2681(a)(1)(A) · varies by place · read in statute*

**City races** follow each city's charter. Take them from race data.

### Winning an election

**Plurality** throughout: the top vote-getter wins the party primary, the
November general election and Australian-ballot town elections. No runoff was
found in the law.

*17 V.S.A. §§ 2369(a), 2592(h)(1), 2682(c) · statutory · read in statute*

### Running as an independent

For **county and state office**, an independent files a statement of nomination
signed by voters qualified to vote for that office, and may add up to three
words of identification after their name on the ballot.

*17 V.S.A. § 2402 · statutory · read in statute*

For **town office** under Australian ballot, every candidate is nominated by
petition filed with the town clerk.

*17 V.S.A. § 2681(a)(1)(A) · statutory · read in statute*

## County government

Vermont has 14 counties (our data has a page for each). They have **no elected
governing board**. The **assistant judges** (side judges) prepare the
county budget, and each county also elects a **sheriff**, a **state's
attorney**, a **probate judge** and a **high bailiff**.

*24 V.S.A. §§ 1, 133(a); Vt. Const. ch. II, §§ 43, 50, 51 · statutory · read in statute*

Our labels call the state's attorney "district attorney" and the assistant judge
"county court judge". In copy, say state's attorney and assistant judge. Never
describe a county commission or board in Vermont.

**Home rule:** no law read for this doc gives Vermont counties charters or
home rule.

### Towns, gores and unorganized towns

Towns are the main local government. A few **unorganized towns and gores**, in
Essex County and elsewhere, have no town government; state-appointed boards handle their property
appraisal.
Avoid describing a town government for an unorganized town.

*24 V.S.A. § 1401 · statutory · read in statute*

## City, town and village government

Vermont "municipality" covers cities, towns, town school districts, incorporated
school and fire districts, and **incorporated villages**, which sit inside a
town and are a separate government. "Local election" in Vermont law means an
election in a town or village of over 4,000 people.

*1 V.S.A. §§ 117, 126; 24 V.S.A. § 1301 · statutory · read in statute*

**Towns** are governed by town meeting and an elected **selectboard**. By
default the annual meeting elects a moderator, clerk, treasurer, listers and
selectboard members, with one selectboard seat a year on a three-year term. Each
town may change much of this by its own vote, such as appointing the clerk or
treasurer or giving them three-year terms. Our labels call the selectboard "city
legislature"; in copy, say selectboard on a town page.

*17 V.S.A. § 2646 · usual case · read in statute*

**Cities** are governed by their charters. Four of our 11 city pages carry a
mayor race. Take city offices from race data.

### Home rule

Vermont has **no constitutional home rule**. A city, town or village changes its
charter by a local vote and then asks the General Assembly to enact the change.

*17 V.S.A. § 2645(a) · statutory · read in statute*

**Does that reach elections?** Each charter is a law the General Assembly
passed, and charters can and do change local election rules. So city election
rules vary by place. Town meeting timing is set in the general law for every
town.

## Writing rules

These are the ways Vermont copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never infer a county from a place name.** No Vermont place page shares a
   name with a different county, but always take the county from the page data.
2. **Never merge a city and a town of the same name.** Barre, Rutland, St.
   Albans and Newport each have both.
3. **Use the right calendar for the tier.** Towns: the first Tuesday of March,
   every year. County offices and justices of the peace: November of
   even-numbered years.
4. **Do not describe a county commission.** Vermont counties have assistant
   judges, not a governing board. Say state's attorney, not district attorney.
5. Do not call town races partisan or nonpartisan unless race data says which.
   County races and justices of the peace are partisan.
6. Do not mention runoffs. Plurality wins.
7. Say selectboard, not city council, on a town page.
8. Avoid describing school board structure beyond race data while Act 73
   changes are under way.
9. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
10. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
    contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.
