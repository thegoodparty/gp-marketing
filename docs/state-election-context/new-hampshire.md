# New Hampshire: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across New Hampshire so you do not have to research the
same ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like
which offices are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own
race data, which always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean. Every claim below has a verbatim quote
in [`research/new-hampshire.json`](./research/new-hampshire.json).

**The one thing to know first:** New Hampshire local government is mostly towns,
and towns vote every year, usually in March, at or alongside town meeting. Town
and school district elections are never held with the November state election.
County offices are partisan and on the November ballot of even-numbered years,
with a September primary that undeclared voters may vote in. Whether a town's
races carry party labels is each town's choice. Cities set their own election
dates and ballot type by charter. More than half of our New Hampshire location
pages are school districts.

## Legislative calendar

Next regular session: convenes 6 January 2027 (the General Court assembles every
year on the first Wednesday following the first Tuesday in January, N.H. Const.
pt. II, art. 3). The constitution sets no session length. The governor has five
days (Sundays excepted) to act on a bill, or it becomes law, but a bill the
governor holds when the legislature adjourns does not become law (N.H. Const.
pt. II, art. 44).

New laws take effect 60 days after passage unless the act sets another date
(RSA 14:9-a, I).

## How elections work in New Hampshire

Safe on any New Hampshire page, at the tier named.

### Election days

**County** offices are on the state ballot: the state primary is the second
Tuesday in September, and the state general election the first Tuesday after the
first Monday in November, both in even-numbered years.

*RSA 653:7, 653:8, 652:4 · statutory · read in statute*

**Towns** elect officers every year, on the second Tuesday in March unless the
town has adopted another permitted date. A town that uses the official ballot
for all questions (known as SB 2) votes in March, April or May, after a
deliberative session. A charter town may choose March, May or November of odd
years. No town election is held with the November state election.

*RSA 669:1, I-III, 40:13, 40:14, X · varies by place · read in statute*

**Cities** set their election date by law, charter or ordinance, but never with
the November state election.

*RSA 44:11, I, 49-C:4 · varies by place · read in statute*

**School districts** elect their officers at town meeting or at the district's
annual meeting, which falls between 1 March and 25 March unless the district
uses SB 2. School district elections are never held with the November state
election, except in Concord and Laconia. A regular school board has three, five,
seven or nine members serving three-year terms.

*RSA 671:2, 197:1, 671:4 · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never say a New Hampshire town or school board votes in November
of an even year, and avoid naming a month for a town or city without race data.

### Primary type

New Hampshire's state primary is semi-open. A registered party member votes only
that party's ballot. An **undeclared** voter may join a party at the primary and
vote its ballot, then return to undeclared status before leaving. A party member
who changes registration at the primary may not vote any party ballot there, and
registered voters may not change party in the months before the primary.

*RSA 654:34, I(b), II(b), IV · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not call New Hampshire primaries open or closed. Undeclared
voters choose one party ballot. Party members cannot cross over.

### Partisanship

**County races are partisan**, on the state primary and general election
ballots.

*RSA 652:4, 653:1 · statutory · read in statute*

**Town races depend on the town.** A town may adopt a partisan official ballot,
a nonpartisan official ballot, or neither, in which case it elects officers by
unofficial ballot at town meeting. Never call a town's races partisan or
nonpartisan without race data.

*RSA 669:11, 669:12, 669:13 · varies by place · read in statute*

**School district races are nonpartisan**, unless a special law for that
district says otherwise.

*RSA 671:30 · usual case · read in statute*

**City races follow the charter**, which may provide for party-ballot elections
or nonpartisan elections without party labels.

*RSA 49-C:6 · varies by place · read in statute*

### Winning an election

State and county offices go to the candidates with the highest number of votes.
In towns using the partisan or nonpartisan official ballot, a plurality elects.
A town still electing by unofficial ballot at town meeting needs a majority.

*RSA 659:81, 669:12, 669:13, 669:11 · statutory · read in statute*

### Running as an independent

For **county** office, a candidate may skip the party primary and reach the
November ballot by nomination papers, signed only by voters registered for the
state election. No voter may sign nomination papers for more than one candidate
for the same office. A candidate who filed in the state primary cannot then run
by nomination papers.

*RSA 655:40, 655:43, IV · statutory · read in statute*

For **town and school district** office there is no party nomination in
nonpartisan towns and districts: candidates file a declaration of candidacy with
the town or district clerk.

*RSA 669:19, 671:30 · statutory · read in statute*

## County government

New Hampshire has 10 counties, and our data has a page for each.

*RSA 22:1 · statutory · read in statute*

Each county elects **county commissioners** from commissioner districts. In most
counties the voters of each district elect their own commissioner, but Carroll
and Sullivan counties elect every district's commissioner countywide. Terms
alternate between two and four years in most counties.

*RSA 653:1, VI · varies by place · read in statute*

Counties also elect a **sheriff, county attorney, county treasurer, register of
deeds and register of probate**, for two-year terms, except in Rockingham and
Coos counties, where they serve four years.

*RSA 653:1, V · statutory · read in statute*

The county budget is adopted by the **county convention**, which is made up of
the county's state representatives. The convention is not separately elected.

*RSA 24:1 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not describe a county board of supervisors, a county
executive or a county council. Commissioners and the convention are the county
government.

There is no county home rule in the law read for this doc.

## Town and city government

New Hampshire has towns and cities. Any town or city may adopt a charter under
the home rule powers of Part I, article 39 of the constitution: a charter town is
organized under RSA 49-D and a charter city under RSA 49-C. A town cannot change
its governing body, how it is elected or its terms by ordinance.

*RSA 49-B:2, 49-B:8 · statutory · read in statute*

### Towns

The usual town government is a **board of selectmen** of three, with one elected
each year for a three-year term. Voters may enlarge the board to five. Every
town also elects a moderator, supervisors of the checklist, a town clerk, a
treasurer and highway agents unless the town has made them appointive, and may
elect optional offices such as trustees of trust funds and library trustees.

*RSA 41:8, 41:8-b, 669:15, 669:16 · usual case · read in statute*

Charter towns are organized under RSA 49-D and may have a different governing
body, such as a town council. Take a charter town's offices from race data.

*RSA 49-B:2, II · varies by place · read in statute*

### Cities

A city charter provides for either a mayor and board of aldermen or a council
with a manager, and sets terms and ward or at-large seats.

*RSA 49-C:8 · varies by place · read in statute*

### Village districts

Village districts, formed within towns for purposes such as fire protection,
choose their own officers at district meetings. Our data has almost no pages for
them.

*RSA 52:1, 52:3 · statutory · read in statute*

## Writing rules

These are the ways New Hampshire copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never decide from the slug alone whether a page is a town or a city.** Our
   town slugs end in `-town` (`bedford-town`), while city slugs do not
   (`manchester`). Town pages label town offices as "City" offices. Decide from
   the offices on the page.
2. **Use the right calendar for the tier.** Towns and school districts vote every
   year, usually in March, never with the November state election. County
   offices are on the even-year state ballot.
3. **Do not call town races partisan or nonpartisan without race data.** Each
   town chooses.
4. Do not call New Hampshire primaries open or closed.
5. Do not describe a county executive, supervisors or council.
6. Do not call any New Hampshire place a township, village or borough. A village
   district is a special district inside a town, not a municipality.
7. Our data has no place names that match a New Hampshire county name in a
   different county, but still take the county from the page data, never from a
   place name.
8. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
9. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
   contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.

### What search results commonly get wrong

- That New Hampshire local elections are in November. Town and school elections
  are usually in March, and never with the November state election.
- That anyone can vote in any primary. Undeclared voters may choose one party's
  ballot, but party members may not cross over.
- That county government is run by an elected executive. It is run by the
  commissioners and the county convention of state representatives.
