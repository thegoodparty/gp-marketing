# Pennsylvania: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Pennsylvania so you do not have to research the same
ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which
offices are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race
data, which always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean. Every claim below has a verbatim quote
in [`research/pennsylvania.json`](./research/pennsylvania.json).

**The one thing to know first:** Pennsylvania local elections are **partisan**
at every level, school board included, and the primary is **closed**. A voter
registered with no party cannot vote in the spring primary that picks every
party's local nominees. Copy written for a state where "local means
nonpartisan" will be wrong on every Pennsylvania page.

## Legislative calendar

The General Assembly is a continuing body that meets year-round, so a change can
pass in any month. It next convenes at noon on 5 January 2027 (the first Tuesday
of January each year, Pa. Const. art. II, § 4). A bill becomes law if the
governor does not return it within ten days (Pa. Const. art. IV, § 15), and a
law with no effective date of its own takes effect 60 days after final enactment
(1 Pa.C.S. § 1701).

## How elections work in Pennsylvania

The timing, primary and partisanship rules below hold on any Pennsylvania page,
including Philadelphia, Pittsburgh and every home rule place. A home rule
charter cannot change the conduct of elections (see "Home rule" below).

### Election days

All county, city, ward, borough and township officers are elected at the
**municipal election**, held in November of **odd-numbered** years. School
directors and the local election board (judge and inspectors of election) are
elected at the same election. The constitution lets the legislature move the
day, but only within an odd-numbered year. So Pennsylvania local offices never
share a ballot with a presidential or governor's race.

*Pa. Const. art. VII, §§ 2, 3; 25 P.S. § 2752 (Election Code § 602) · statutory · read in statute*

The **municipal primary** is the third Tuesday of May in the same odd-numbered
year, and every office filled at the municipal election is nominated there.

*25 P.S. § 2754 (Election Code § 604) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** publish the rule, not a date. The odd-year rule is locked in the
constitution; the specific May and November Tuesdays are set by statute and can
be moved.

All three tiers (county, municipal, school district) vote on the same odd-year
cycle, so a county page and a borough page in the same county are about the same
election.

### Primary type

**Closed.** Only a voter registered and enrolled in a party may vote in that
party's primary, and only enrolled party members may sign a candidate's primary
petition.

*25 P.S. §§ 2832, 2868 (Election Code §§ 802, 908) · statutory · read in statute*

Party candidates for every elective office in the state are nominated at the
primary, and the candidate with the most votes in each party's primary wins its
nomination (plurality, no runoff).

*25 P.S. §§ 2862, 2882 (Election Code §§ 902, 922) · statutory · read in statute*

For our audience: because local races are partisan and the primary is closed, an
unaffiliated Pennsylvanian has no say in the round that usually decides local
races. This is the most directly relevant structural fact in the state.

### Partisanship

**Partisan at every level**: county, city, borough, town, township, school board
and the local election board. The November ballot prints the nominating party or
political body beside every candidate's name.

*25 P.S. § 2963(b) (Election Code § 1003(b)) · statutory · read in statute*

**School board and local judges: cross-filing.** Candidates for school director,
for judge of the court of common pleas and the Philadelphia Municipal Court, and
for magisterial district judge may seek the nomination of more than one party at
the same primary. Everyone else must swear they are running in one party's
primary only. A school board candidate who wins both major primaries appears on
the November ballot under both party names.

*25 P.S. §§ 2870(f), 2936(d) (Election Code §§ 910, 976) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** school board races in Pennsylvania are **not** nonpartisan. They
carry party labels, they just allow cross-filing. Never write "nonpartisan
school board" on a Pennsylvania page.

### Winning

The candidates with the most votes win, up to the number of seats. A tie is
settled by casting lots. There is no runoff, and since charters cannot touch
election conduct, no Pennsylvania city can adopt ranked choice voting on its own.

*25 P.S. §§ 3167, 3168 (Election Code §§ 1417, 1418); 53 Pa.C.S. § 2962(a)(5) · statutory · read in statute*

Two places use **limited voting** to guarantee seats to the minority party:
county commissioners in every commissioner county (see County government) and
Philadelphia's at-large council seats (see Philadelphia).

The local election board shows this in miniature: each voter votes for one
inspector of election, and the runner-up becomes the minority inspector.

*25 P.S. § 2671 (Election Code § 401) · statutory · read in statute*

### Getting on the ballot as an independent

A candidate outside the two primaries files **nomination papers** signed by
qualified electors of the district. Signers do not have to belong to any party,
but a voter may sign only one nomination paper per office (or as many as there
are seats to fill).

*25 P.S. § 2911(a), (c) (Election Code § 951) · statutory · read in statute*

A nomination-paper candidate must not have been registered in a party at any
time from 30 days before the primary through the November election, and must not
have filed primary petitions for any office that year. In practice, a party
member who wants to run as an independent has to leave the party more than a
month before the May primary.

*25 P.S. §§ 2911(e), 2911.1 (Election Code §§ 951, 951.1) · statutory · read in statute*

### School districts and special districts

Most school districts have a board of **nine school directors**, elected at the
municipal election on four-year terms, at large unless the district has adopted
a plan to elect some or all by region. One vote per seat; cumulative voting is
abolished.

*24 P.S. §§ 3-301, 3-303 (Public School Code §§ 301, 303) · usual case · read in statute*

Philadelphia is the exception: its Board of Education is **appointed by the
mayor** with council's consent, not elected. We have no Philadelphia school
district page, which is consistent with this.

*Philadelphia Home Rule Charter § 12-201 · varies by place · read in city charter*

Pennsylvania's special-purpose bodies are mostly **municipal authorities** (water,
sewer, parking and so on), whose boards are appointed by the governments that
created them. They are not elected.

*53 Pa.C.S. § 5610(a) · statutory · read in statute*

### Other offices on local pages

Many borough, township and city pages show **constables**, elected in each
borough or township and in each ward of second and third class cities, and
**judges and inspectors of election**, elected in each election district for
four-year terms. They are real elected offices; describe them plainly.

*44 Pa.C.S. §§ 7112, 7113; 25 P.S. § 2671 · statutory · read in statute*

## County government

Pennsylvania has **67 counties**, and our data has a page for each. Every
municipal page sits under one of them. There is no place in the state outside a
county; Philadelphia is a city and county in one (see below).

*16 Pa.C.S. § 301 · statutory · read in statute; our page data (sitemap, 4 November 2026)*

### Board of county commissioners

The default county government is a board of **three county commissioners**,
elected at the municipal election on four-year terms. Counties also elect a
district attorney, treasurer, sheriff, coroner, prothonotary, clerk of courts,
register of wills, recorder of deeds, and either three auditors or a controller.
Some still elect jury commissioners.

*Pa. Const. art. IX, § 4; 16 Pa.C.S. § 12301(a) · usual case · read in statute*

Which row offices are separate, combined or appointed varies by county (our race
data has combined "register and recorder" offices in several). Let race data
supply the list.

### Limited voting

In electing county commissioners, each voter may vote for **no more than two**
candidates, and the top three win. In practice one party cannot take all three
seats. If a commissioner leaves office, the court fills the seat with a member of
the departing commissioner's party.

*Pa. Const. art. IX, § 4; 16 Pa.C.S. § 12501 · usual case · read in statute*

This minority-seat guarantee is written into the state constitution and is worth
using on county pages. It applies only to commissioner counties: the
constitution exempts any county with a home rule charter or optional plan.

### County classes

Nine classes by population. Philadelphia is the only first class county. The
County Code (16 Pa.C.S., which in 2024 replaced the 1955 County Code act) does
not apply to first or second class counties except for a few listed sections.
Prefer describing the government to naming a class number.

*16 Pa.C.S. §§ 102, 102.1, 310 · statutory · read in statute*

### Home rule counties

Any county may adopt a home rule charter or an optional plan by referendum, and
the charter then sets the form of government. Home rule does **not** reach
elections: the timing, partisan primary and ballot rules above still apply.

*Pa. Const. art. IX, §§ 2, 4, 14; 53 Pa.C.S. §§ 2901(b), 2962(a)(5) · statutory · read in statute*

Our race data shows a **county council** on the Allegheny, Delaware, Erie,
Luzerne and Northampton county pages, and a **county executive** in Allegheny,
Erie, Lehigh and Northampton. The other 61 county pages show a "County
Commission". For any county page whose offices include a county executive or a
county council, do not write "three commissioners" or mention limited voting.

*Our page data (sitemap, 4 November 2026) · varies by place*

**Watch out:** a "County Commission" label does not always mean the statutory
three-member board. Lehigh County's page shows a commission **and** a county
executive, so it is a charter county: describe only the offices shown.
Lackawanna County is the reverse case: it has a home rule charter but kept the
commissioners and their limited voting.

*DCED, Home Rule in Pennsylvania · varies by place · state agency publication*

### Townships

Townships are full municipal governments, not sub-units of the county, and have
their own pages (see below).

## City, borough, town and township government

Pennsylvania has about 2,555 municipalities of five kinds: 56 cities, 953
boroughs, one town and about 1,545 townships, plus 500 school districts. State
data counts 110 home rule jurisdictions. Our data has 2,551 municipal pages,
1,544 of them townships.

*DCED, Local Government in Pennsylvania fact sheet (17 March 2026) · statutory · state agency publication; our page data (sitemap, 4 November 2026)*

The type determines both the offices and the vocabulary, so take the type from
the page's offices and name, never assume it.

### Borough

The most common type of town government. Elects a **borough council** (seven
members where the borough has no wards, otherwise one or two per ward), a
**mayor**, a **tax collector**, and three **auditors** on six-year terms or a
controller. Council members serve four-year terms, about half elected at each
municipal election. Voters in a borough without wards can change council to
three, five or seven seats by petition and referendum.

*8 Pa.C.S. §§ 802, 806, 811, 818 · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** a borough mayor is **not** the head of the government. Council
holds the general powers. The mayor presides only at council's organization,
votes only to organize council or break a tie, and has full charge of the
police. Never write that a borough mayor runs the borough.

*8 Pa.C.S. §§ 1003, 1123.1 · usual case · read in statute*

### Township of the second class

The most common type overall (about 1,452). Elects three **supervisors**, three
**auditors** and a **tax collector**. Supervisors and auditors serve
**six-year** terms, and **one supervisor seat** comes up at each municipal
election. Voters may expand the board to five supervisors by referendum.

*53 P.S. §§ 65402, 65403, 65404 (Second Class Township Code §§ 402-404) · usual case · read in statute*

The single-seat, six-year rhythm is the most useful municipal fact in the state:
a second class township ballot is usually short, and one seat carries a lot of
weight.

The Second Class Township Code is still the 1933 act, cited as 53 P.S. It has
not been consolidated.

### Township of the first class

A township with at least 300 people per square mile that has chosen first class
status by referendum. Any township that is not first class or home rule is
second class. Elects at least five **township commissioners** on four-year
terms, at large where the township has no wards (two or three seats at each
municipal election) or one per ward where it has five or more wards. Also elects
a **tax collector** and auditors on six-year terms or a controller, unless it
appoints an auditor.

*73 Pa.C.S. §§ 105, 106, 1703 · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** a statutory first class township has **no mayor**; the board of
commissioners governs. The First Class Township Code moved to 73 Pa.C.S. in
2026; the 1931 act is repealed and its old URLs no longer work.

*73 Pa.C.S., enactment note; Act 331 of 1931 law information page · statutory · read in statute*

### City of the third class

Covers almost every Pennsylvania city. The default elects a **mayor**, four
**council members**, a **controller** and a **treasurer**, each for four years.
The mayor sits on council. Voters may add two council seats by referendum.
Third class cities may also run under an optional charter or a home rule
charter, which can change all of this.

*11 Pa.C.S. §§ 10107, 10701, 11002 · usual case · read in statute*

City class follows the federal census, and a city does not drop a class on one
census alone. Cities of 250,000 to 1,000,000 are second class (Pittsburgh), and
a city of 80,000 to 250,000 can become second class A by saying so in a home
rule charter.

*11 Pa.C.S. §§ 201, 202 · statutory · read in statute*

### Incorporated town

State data counts one town, and Bloomsburg is the one place our data labels a
town ("Town of Bloomsburg", "Town Council"). An incorporated town runs under its
own local or special act and may have adopted parts or all of the Borough Code,
so do not give it borough copy by default.

*8 Pa.C.S. § 107.1; DCED fact sheet · varies by place · read in statute*

### Home rule municipalities

Any county, city, borough, town or township may replace its statutory form with
a home rule charter adopted by its own voters. A charter can set the council
size, district or at-large seats, and which offices are elected. It **cannot**
change voter registration or the conduct of elections. Philadelphia's charter
falls under a separate act with the same effect: it cannot override election
laws that apply across the state.

*Pa. Const. art. IX, § 2; 53 Pa.C.S. §§ 2901(b), 2924, 2962(a)(5); 53 P.S. § 13133 (First Class City Home Rule Act § 18) · statutory · read in statute*

Six township pages in our data show a **mayor** (Penn Hills, Bensalem, Upper
Darby, Whitehall, Wilkes-Barre Township and Bedminster). A home rule township
can have one. Where race data shows offices that do not match the templates
above, the place has a charter: describe the offices on the page, not the
template.

## Metro and major-place exceptions

Pennsylvania's biggest places are each legally unique rather than members of a
class.

### Philadelphia

`pa/philadelphia-county`

A **consolidated city-county**. The constitution abolished every county office in
Philadelphia, and the city performs all county functions. There are no county
commissioners and no commissioner limited voting.

*Pa. Const. art. IX, § 13 · statutory · read in statute*

City Council has **seventeen** members on four-year terms: ten by district and
seven at large. Each voter votes for one district member and **five** of the
seven at-large seats, and no party may nominate more than five at-large
candidates, which guarantees the minority party at least two at-large seats.

*Philadelphia Home Rule Charter §§ 2-100, 2-101 · varies by place · read in city charter*

The school board is appointed by the mayor (see School districts).

This is the highest-risk single page in the state. It is the **only**
Philadelphia page we have: `pa/philadelphia-county/philadelphia` does not exist,
and the county page carries the mayor, City Council, controller, district
attorney and other city offices. Whatever the county page says is the whole of
our Philadelphia copy.

*Our page data, checked 4 November 2026*

### Allegheny County

`pa/allegheny-county`

A second class county under a home rule charter approved by voters in 1998, with
an elected **county executive** and a **fifteen-member county council** on
four-year terms, thirteen elected by district and two county-wide. Never write
"three commissioners" here.

*Allegheny County, County Council page · varies by place · local government publication; DCED, Home Rule in Pennsylvania · state agency publication*

### Pittsburgh

`pa/allegheny-county/pittsburgh`

The state's only second class city, governed under a home rule charter. Its
council has been elected by district since a 1987 charter amendment. None of the
third class city template applies.

*11 Pa.C.S. § 201(2); DCED, Home Rule in Pennsylvania · varies by place · state agency publication*

### Scranton

`pa/lackawanna-county/scranton`

Governed under its own home rule charter, so the third class city template does
not apply. Describe the offices race data shows.

*DCED, Home Rule in Pennsylvania · varies by place · state agency publication*

### Bloomsburg

`pa/columbia-county/bloomsburg`

Labelled a town in our data (see Incorporated town). Our data splits it across
two pages; see Data issues.

## Data issues

Found on the live site on 4 November 2026. These are problems in our data, not
facts to write around. Never write copy that explains or hides them.

1. **Bloomsburg has two pages.** `pa/columbia-county/bloomsburg` holds the mayor,
   council and town tax collector. `pa/columbia-county/bloomsburg-town` ("Town of
   Bloomsburg") holds the town council again plus a constable and tax collector
   labelled "Bloomsburg Township". Bloomsburg is a town, not a township.
2. **Smethport has two pages.** `pa/mckean-county/smethport` holds the borough
   offices. `pa/mckean-county/smethport-borough` holds a single office labelled
   "Smethport Township Auditor".
3. **Combined offices broke their URLs.** Seven position pages for offices whose
   names contain a slash have an extra path segment, for example
   `pa/indiana-county/county-register-of-wills/recorder-of-deeds/position/clerk-of-orphans-court-joint`
   (also Jefferson, Mifflin, Susquehanna and Venango counties),
   `pa/allegheny-county/indiana-township/township-supervisor/position/auditor-joint`
   and `pa/lancaster-county/upper-leacock-township/township-supervisor/position/constable-joint`.
   The pages load, but the path above each one (such as
   `pa/indiana-county/county-register-of-wills`) returns 404.
4. **Township label on a borough page.** `pa/allegheny-county/etna` (a borough)
   lists "Etna Township Auditor" beside the borough auditor.

No Census `-city` or `-village` twins: the 17 `-city` slugs (Oil City, Grove
City and others) and 3 `-village` slugs are those places' real names, with no
plain-name sibling. The 483 district pages are all school districts.

## Writing rules

These are the ways Pennsylvania copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never say local elections here are nonpartisan**, and never call a school
   board race nonpartisan. Every local race carries party labels; school board
   candidates may simply run in both primaries.
2. **Never imply an unaffiliated voter can vote in the primary.** It is closed.
3. **Never infer a county from a place name.** 11 Pennsylvania municipal pages
   are named after a county they are not in, for example Bradford (McKean
   County), McKean (Erie County), Chester (Delaware County) and Northampton
   (Bucks County). Always take the county from the page data.
4. **Keep "Township" in township names.** 142 Pennsylvania pages share a name with
   another place in the same county, usually a borough and the township around
   it (`athens` and `athens-township` in Bradford County). They are different
   governments with different offices.
5. **Never call a borough mayor the head of the government**, and never give a
   statutory first class township a mayor. Home rule townships may have one; let
   race data decide.
6. **Never write "three commissioners" or mention limited voting** for
   Philadelphia, Allegheny County or any county whose page shows a county
   executive or council (Lehigh included).
7. **Never describe Philadelphia's school board as elected.**
8. Do not state seat counts or term lengths as fixed. Boroughs, townships and
   cities can change them by referendum, and charters change everything except
   election rules.
9. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
10. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
    contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.

### What search results commonly get wrong

- Pennsylvania school board races are often described as nonpartisan. They are
  partisan with cross-filing.
- Summaries sometimes cite the old County Code (16 P.S.) or First Class Township
  Code (cited as 53 P.S.). Both were repealed and replaced by 16
  Pa.C.S. (2024) and 73 Pa.C.S. (2026).
- Borough mayors are often described as running the borough. Council governs.
