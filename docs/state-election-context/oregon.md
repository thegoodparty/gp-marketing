# Oregon: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Oregon so you do not have to research the same
ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which
offices are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race
data, which always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean. Every claim below has a verbatim quote
in [`research/oregon.json`](./research/oregon.json).

**The one thing to know first:** Oregon has two local election calendars, and
they split by type of government. **Counties and cities** elect their officers
at the state's even-year elections: the May primary and the November general.
**School boards and special districts** (fire, water, parks, library, ports and
dozens more) elect their boards in **May of odd-numbered years**. Every election
in Oregon is conducted by mail. And "Oregon local races are nonpartisan" is only
mostly true: the sheriff, clerk and district attorney are nonpartisan by
statute, but county commissioners run with party labels unless the county's
charter says otherwise.

## Legislative calendar

Next regular session: convenes 19 January 2027 (in odd-numbered years, the
Tuesday after the Martin Luther King, Jr. holiday, ORS 171.010(1)). The
Legislature meets every year: an odd-year session may last at most 160 calendar
days, so the 2027 session ends by 27 June 2027, and an even-year session at most
35 days (Or. Const. art. IV, § 10(1)). The 2028 short session convenes
1 February 2028 (ORS 171.010(2)).

While the Legislature is in session, the governor has five days, Saturdays and
Sundays excepted, to act on a bill. After adjournment, the governor has thirty
days, Saturdays and Sundays excepted, to veto a bill, or it becomes law
(Or. Const. art. V, § 15b(3)). For a 2027 session that runs its full length,
that deadline falls on 6 August 2027. New laws usually take effect on 1 January
of the year after passage, unless the act says otherwise (ORS 171.022), and
never sooner than 90 days after the session ends unless the act declares an
emergency (Or. Const. art. IV, § 28).

## How elections work in Oregon

Safe on any Oregon page, at the tier named.

### Election days

**General election:** the first Tuesday after the first Monday in November, in
even-numbered years. **Primary election:** the third Tuesday in May, in
even-numbered years. The primary nominates major party candidates for November,
and it is also the "nominating election" for nonpartisan offices (see Winning an
election below).

*ORS 254.056 · statutory · read in statute*

**All elections are by mail.** County clerks conduct every election in Oregon
by mail.

*ORS 254.465(1) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not mention polling places or "going to the polls". Say
"returning a ballot" or "voting in the May election".

**County tier.** County officers are elected at the May primary and the
November general election. A county may not hold an election for a county
office on any date except the second Tuesday in March, the third Tuesday in
May, the fourth Tuesday in August, or the November general election date,
apart from a declared emergency.

*ORS 203.085(1); 204.005(1) · statutory · read in statute*

**Municipal tier.** The constitution requires cities to hold their nominating
and regular elections for city officers at the same time as the state primary
and general elections, so city officers are elected in even-numbered years. A
city charter can shape how a city votes but cannot move that timing. Some
cities hold no May nominating election, and their candidates go straight to the
November ballot.

*Or. Const. art. II, § 14a; ORS 221.180(2), 221.230(1), 254.035(2) · statutory · read in statute*

**School and special district tier.** School boards and the boards of special
districts (water, fire, parks and recreation, library, cemetery, port, sanitary,
transit, soil and water conservation, community college and many others) are
elected at the **regular district election**: the third Tuesday in May of
odd-numbered years. A district may hold only one board election a year, and new
board members take office on 1 July.

*ORS 255.012, 255.335(1), (2), (4); 332.118(1)(a) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** a school board or fire district race is never on a November
ballot or in an even-numbered year. Never tie a district board race to the
"general election" or "primary".

### Primary type

Oregon's party primaries are **closed**. Only voters registered with a major
party can vote for that party's candidates, and they get only their own party's
ballot. A major party can choose to open its primary to voters not affiliated
with any party. Nonaffiliated voters who do not use that option get a primary
ballot with only the city, county and nonpartisan races and the measures.

*ORS 254.365(1), (2); 254.470(3)(d) · statutory · read in statute*

To be a party's nominee, a candidate must have been a member of the party for
at least 180 days before the filing deadline. A candidate who loses a party
primary cannot run in November for the same office as another party's nominee
or as a nonaffiliated candidate.

*ORS 249.046(1), 249.048 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not call Oregon's primaries open, and do not say any voter can
choose a party ballot. Do not describe the May nonpartisan races as a party
primary: they are on the same ballot, without party labels.

### Partisanship

Partisanship splits by office, not by type of government.

**Nonpartisan by statute:** the county clerk, county assessor, county surveyor,
county treasurer, sheriff, district attorney, justice of the peace, any county
judge who exercises judicial functions, and the elected offices of a metropolitan service district
(Metro). These appear without a party label.

*ORS 249.002(7), 254.125(1) · statutory · read in statute*

**County commissioners are partisan unless a charter makes them nonpartisan.**
County commissioners, and county judges who have no judicial duties, are not on
the state's list of nonpartisan offices. State law treats the office as
partisan in some counties and nonpartisan in others: a home rule charter can
designate any office nonpartisan.

*ORS 236.210(2), 236.215(1), 249.002(7) · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never say a county commission race is partisan or nonpartisan
unless race data shows the party labels (or their absence). Never describe an
Oregon county's government as partisan or nonpartisan as a whole: in most
counties the sheriff and clerk are nonpartisan while the commissioners carry
party labels.

**City offices: nonpartisan in practice, set by each charter.** State law
governs how city candidates are nominated and leaves room for a party
nomination, so party labels on city races come from the city's own charter. The
usual case is nonpartisan. Portland's charter, for example, makes its council,
mayor and auditor nonpartisan.

*ORS 221.180(1), 249.002(7), 254.565(1)(c); Portland City Charter § 3-105 · usual case · read in statute*

**School and special district boards** are nonpartisan by design: candidates are
nominated only by filing a petition or a declaration of candidacy with the
district's elections officer. There is no party route.

*ORS 255.235(1), 332.118(1)(a) · statutory · read in statute*

### Winning an election

**Plurality is the default.** The candidate with the most votes is nominated or
elected.

*ORS 254.065(1) · statutory · read in statute*

**Nonpartisan offices can be won in May.** At the May nominating election, a
nonpartisan candidate who wins a majority is **elected outright** and does not
appear in November. Without a majority, the top two go to November. A home rule
charter can change this rule.

*ORS 249.088 · usual case · read in statute*

**Except the sheriff, county treasurer and county clerk.** For these three, a
May majority makes the candidate the only nominee, not the winner: the election
is still decided in November. With only one or two candidates, the race skips
the May ballot entirely.

*ORS 249.091(1), (2)(b) · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not write that a nonpartisan race "goes to a runoff" or "is
decided in November". Many are settled in May. Never say an office was won in
May unless race data shows it.

**Ranked choice voting** is not state law. Portland uses it by charter (see
Metro and major-place exceptions). Other charter cities or counties may use it
too, so never say "Oregon uses plurality voting" on a charter place's page.

*ORS 254.065(1); Portland City Charter § 3-102 · varies by place · read in statute*

### Running as an independent

For a **nonpartisan office** (most city, school and special district races, and
the nonpartisan county offices), there is no party route. Every candidate files
a nominating petition or a declaration of candidacy. Any voter may sign a
nonpartisan candidate's petition.

*ORS 249.020(1), 249.076(2)(a), 255.235(1) · statutory · read in statute*

For a **partisan office** (such as a partisan county commission seat), a
candidate who is not a major party's nominee can be nominated for November by a
minor party, by an assembly of voters, or by individual voters signing a
certificate of nomination. Any voter may sign a nonaffiliated candidate's
certificate, including someone who voted in a party primary, and a voter may
sign for more than one candidate for the same office. Only a major party
candidate's own petition is limited, to members of that party.

*ORS 249.705, 249.076 · statutory · read in statute*

## County government

Oregon has **36 counties**. Our data has no consolidated city-county or
independent city pages.

*ORS 201.010 to 201.360 · statutory · read in statute*

**Governing body.** By default, a county is governed by a **county court**: an
elected county judge and two county commissioners. A county whose county judge has no
judicial duties can replace the county judge with a third commissioner,
forming a **board of county commissioners**, and our race data shows a county
judge in only four counties. A board has three commissioners unless a charter
or ordinance sets another number. Commissioner seats are numbered positions
(Position No. 1, 2 and 3), with four-year terms.

*ORS 203.111, 203.230(1), 203.240(1)(b), 204.010(1), 204.013(1) · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** a "county judge" in Oregon is usually a member of the county's
governing body, not a courtroom judge. Do not describe the office as a judicial
one unless race data says so.

**Other elected county offices.** Sheriff, county clerk, county assessor and
county treasurer, each for four years by default. The county surveyor is
appointed unless the charter or an ordinance makes it elected. The district
attorney is elected countywide, and some counties elect justices of the peace.

*ORS 204.005, 204.010(1), 249.002(7) · usual case · read in statute*

**County home rule.** Voters in any county can adopt a county charter by
majority vote. A charter sets the organization of the county government,
including the number of officers and whether they are elected or appointed. It
can designate offices nonpartisan and can change the May majority rule. It
cannot change how judges, justices of the peace or district attorneys are
selected.

*Or. Const. art. VI, § 10; ORS 203.720(1), 249.002(7), 249.088(2) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** charter counties elect offices that general-law counties do not
(our race data shows, for example, an elected county chair or auditor in some).
Take office names from race data, never from this doc.

**Below the county:** Oregon has no townships, and our data has no township
pages. Outside cities, the local governments are special districts and school
districts.

## City government

Oregon has one kind of municipality: the **city** (the constitution's older
phrase is "city and town"). The city statutes set no population classes. A few
cities have "village" in their name (Adair Village, Wood Village), but they are
cities.

*Or. Const. art. XI, § 2; ORS chapter 221 · usual case · read in statute*

**Home rule.** The constitution gives the voters of every city the power to
enact and amend their own charter, and forbids the Legislature from writing or
amending city charters. Charters are adopted and amended by simple majority
vote. So a city's form of government, the size of its council, whether the
mayor is elected, and its officers' terms all come from its charter, and they
vary from city to city.

*Or. Const. art. XI, § 2; ORS 221.210(2)(a) · statutory · read in statute*

**The statutory fallback.** Only a city incorporated under the general law that
has no charter of its own uses the state default: five councillors, three of
them elected every two years, with the council choosing a mayor from among its
members.

*ORS 221.110, 221.120(1), 221.130(2) · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never say how many council seats a city has, how long the terms
are, or whether the mayor is elected, unless race data shows it.

**Does home rule reach elections?** Partly. State election law governs the
conduct of all city elections, and the constitution fixes their timing to the
state's even-year primary and general. But the Election Code itself defers to
charters in several places: a charter can designate offices nonpartisan, change
the May majority rule for nonpartisan offices, set how candidates file, and
(as in Portland) set the voting method.

*Or. Const. art. II, § 14a; ORS 221.180(3), 221.200, 249.002(7), 249.088(2) · statutory · read in statute*

## School and special districts

Covered under election days and partisanship above. The details that matter for
copy:

- **School boards** are elected at the May odd-year district election, for
  four-year terms. Boards of districts under 300,000 people have five or seven
  members, elected at large unless the board has created zones.
- **School, education service and community college districts** can change
  their electoral system (for example from at-large to zones) to comply with
  Oregon's voting rights law, so board structure varies.
- **Special districts** (fire, water, parks, library and the rest) elect their
  boards at the same May odd-year election, with no party labels.

*ORS 255.335, 255.400(4), 255.405(1)(b), 332.011, 332.018(1), 332.124(1), 332.138 · usual case · read in statute*

## Metro and major-place exceptions

### Portland

`or/multnomah-county/portland`

Portland's charter, as amended by its voters on 8 November 2022, sets its own
election method, the clearest example in Oregon of a charter doing so:

- **Twelve councilors** from four districts, three per district, elected by
  **single transferable vote**, a proportional form of ranked choice voting.
- The **mayor and the auditor** are elected citywide by **instant runoff**
  ranked choice voting.
- All three offices are **nonpartisan** and elected at the November general
  election for four-year terms.

*Portland City Charter §§ 3-101, 3-102, 3-105, 3-107 · statutory · read in city charter*

**Watch out:** do not describe Portland as having city commissioners or a
commission form of government; that ended with the charter change. Do not say
Portland's races are decided in May.

### Multnomah County

`or/multnomah-county`

A charter county. Race data shows offices that general-law counties do not
have, such as a county chair (which our labels call a county executive) and a
county auditor. Take office names and partisanship from race data.

*Or. Const. art. VI, § 10 · varies by place · read in statute*

### Counties with a county court

`or/gilliam-county`, `or/grant-county`, `or/harney-county`, `or/sherman-county`

Our data shows an elected county judge in these four counties, so they still
have a county court rather than a three-member board. See writing rule 6.

*ORS 203.111 · varies by place · read in statute*

### Unincorporated communities

`or/multnomah-county/corbett`, `or/clackamas-county/mulino`,
`or/clackamas-county/colton`, `or/lane-county/marcola`,
`or/tillamook-county/beaver`, `or/benton-county/north-albany`,
`or/curry-county/harbor`, `or/hood-river-county/parkdale`,
`or/curry-county/agness`, `or/malheur-county/owyhee`

These ten municipal-tier pages are not cities. Each holds only a special
district board (water, fire, sanitary, library or cemetery). Never call the
place a city or mention a mayor or council.

### Big cities across county lines

Each of our pages files a city under one county, even where the city extends
into others. Do not write that a city lies wholly within the county on its
page.

## Writing rules

These are the ways Oregon copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never infer a county from a place name.** 3 of our Oregon municipal pages
   share a name with a different Oregon county: Jefferson (Marion County),
   Wasco (Sherman County) and Wheeler (Tillamook County). The full list is in
   [`research/places-named-after-another-county.json`](./research/places-named-after-another-county.json).
   Always take the county from the page data.
2. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
3. **Do not call local races nonpartisan without naming the office.** County
   commissioners usually carry party labels.
4. **Never put a school board or special district race in November or in an
   even-numbered year.** They are elected in May of odd-numbered years.
5. **Do not mention polling places.** Oregon votes by mail.
6. **Do not call a county judge a judicial office**, and do not say a county
   has a board of commissioners or a county court unless race data shows which.
7. Do not call Oregon primaries open, and do not say any voter can pick a party
   ballot.
8. Do not say a nonpartisan race goes to a runoff. A May majority usually wins
   outright, except for sheriff, clerk and treasurer.
9. **Do not say Oregon uses ranked choice voting**, or that it never does.
   Portland does by charter; the state default is plurality.
10. Do not call Adair Village or Wood Village a village. They are cities.
11. Do not mention townships. Oregon has none.
12. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
    contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.
