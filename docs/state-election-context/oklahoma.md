# Oklahoma: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Oklahoma so you do not have to research the same
ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which
offices are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race
data, which always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean. Every claim below has a verbatim
quote in [`research/oklahoma.json`](./research/oklahoma.json).

**The one thing to know first:** in Oklahoma, the **type of government** decides
both the year and the party label. County races are partisan and run on the
state's even-year calendar: a closed party primary in June, a runoff in August
if no one wins a majority, and the general election in November. Cities and
towns are nonpartisan by default and vote in April of odd-numbered years.
School boards vote every April with no party primary at all. Charter cities
write some of their own rules. Always check whether the page is a county, a
city or town, or a school district before writing a sentence about elections.

## How elections work in Oklahoma

### Election days

Every election a county election board runs is on a Tuesday. Counties, cities,
towns, school districts and other local bodies may hold an election only on
these dates:

- the second Tuesday of February
- the first Tuesday of April
- the third Tuesday of June
- the fourth Tuesday of August
- the first Tuesday after the first Monday of November
- the second Tuesday of December, but only every fourth year (the cycle that
  started in December 2025)

*26 O.S. § 3-101(A), (B) · statutory · read in statute*

Charter cities are bound by the same list.

*26 O.S. § 13-103(E) · statutory · read in statute*

Which date applies depends on the tier:

- **County offices** are on the November general election ballot in
  even-numbered years. Party nominees are chosen at the primary on the third
  Tuesday in June, with a runoff on the fourth Tuesday of August.
  *26 O.S. §§ 1-101, 1-102, 1-103 · statutory · read in statute*
- **Statutory cities and towns** (those without a charter) hold their general
  municipal election on the first Tuesday in April of odd-numbered years.
  *11 O.S. § 16-103 · statutory · read in statute*
- **Charter cities** do not follow that April rule. They choose among the dates
  above, under their own charter.
  *11 O.S. § 16-102(A); 26 O.S. § 13-103(E) · varies by place · read in statute*
- **School boards** elect **every year**: the general election is the first
  Tuesday of April, and a primary, when needed, is the second Tuesday in
  February. In a year when the presidential primary falls in February, the
  school primary moves to that day.
  *26 O.S. § 13A-103(A)(1), (2) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never write "Oklahoma local elections are held in odd years" or
"in November". County races are in even-year Novembers, statutory cities and
towns vote in odd-year Aprils, and school boards vote every April. For a charter
city, let race data give the date.

### Can a place move its election?

Only a charter city has real choice, and only among the dates on the state list.
Counties, statutory cities and towns, and school districts have their dates
fixed by law.

*26 O.S. §§ 3-101(B), 13-103(E); 11 O.S. § 16-102(A) · statutory · read in statute*

### Small towns that elect at a town meeting

A municipality of fewer than 2,000 residents that has no charter elects its
officers at a **town meeting** of its voters, held on the first Tuesday in April
of odd-numbered years, unless it has passed an ordinance to have the county
election board run its elections instead.

*11 O.S. §§ 16-302(A), 16-303 · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not write "voters go to the polls" or describe ballots and
polling places for a small town's own offices. Some small towns vote at a town
meeting. Keep the wording general ("residents choose their town officers") unless
race data shows a county-run election.

### Primary type

**Closed.** A voter may vote only in the primary of the party they are registered
with.

*26 O.S. § 1-104(A) · statutory · read in statute*

A recognized party may choose to let voters registered as **Independents** vote
in its primary. Each party decides every odd-numbered November, and the choice
covers the next two calendar years. An Independent may vote in only one party's
primary.

*26 O.S. § 1-104(B) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never say Independents can vote in "the primary" without
qualification, and never name which parties currently admit them. That changes
every two years.

### Partisanship

- **Counties:** partisan. County commissioners and the other county officers are
  nominated at the party primary, and the county election board certifies each
  party's nominees.
  *26 O.S. §§ 1-102, 8-101 · statutory · read in statute*
- **Judges on county ballots:** associate district judges and district judges
  are elected **without party designation**, even though they appear on the same
  ballots as partisan county offices.
  *26 O.S. § 11-108 · statutory · read in statute*
- **Statutory cities and towns:** nonpartisan by default. Every candidate files
  as an independent and there is no primary, unless the municipality has passed
  an ordinance making its elections partisan.
  *11 O.S. § 16-105.1; 26 O.S. § 13-103(B) · usual case · read in statute*
- **Charter cities:** the charter or an ordinance may set something else.
  *11 O.S. § 16-105.1; 26 O.S. § 13-103(B) · varies by place · read in statute*
- **School boards:** no party nomination at all. Candidates file directly, and
  every registered voter living in the district votes on the same ballot.
  *26 O.S. §§ 13A-103(A), 13A-105, 13A-106(B) · statutory · read in statute*

Running in a nonpartisan city or town election does not change a candidate's
own party registration.

*11 O.S. § 16-110 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never write that Oklahoma local elections are nonpartisan, or that
they are partisan. Counties are partisan by law. Cities and towns are
nonpartisan only by default, and any of them can opt into party elections. Do
not state a city's partisanship unless race data shows party labels.

### Majority, runoffs and plurality

- **County party primaries** need a **majority**. If no one wins one, the top two
  meet in the August runoff primary.
  *26 O.S. § 1-103 · statutory · read in statute*
- **School boards** with three or more candidates go to the February primary.
  More than half the vote there wins the seat outright; otherwise the top two
  meet in April. With only two candidates, the race goes straight to April.
  *26 O.S. § 13A-103(A)(3), (4) · statutory · read in statute*
- **Associate district judges** with a majority at the June primary are elected
  then and do not appear in November.
  *26 O.S. § 11-113 · statutory · read in statute*
- **Statutory cities and towns** with nonpartisan elections hold one election
  with no primary. Town trustees elected at large win by plurality.
  *11 O.S. §§ 16-105.1, 12-103.2(C) · statutory · read in statute*
- **Charter cities** set their own rules, which may include a runoff.
  *varies by place*

Anyone unopposed is declared nominated or elected and does not appear on the
ballot at all.

*26 O.S. § 6-102 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** "Oklahoma has runoffs" is only true for party primaries, school
boards (in effect) and some charter cities. Do not promise a runoff for a city or
town race.

### Getting on the ballot without a party

For a **county office**, an independent candidate must have been registered to
vote as an Independent for the six months before filing. They file in the same
filing period as party candidates, with either a filing fee or a supporting
petition, and go straight to the November ballot. A party candidate must
likewise have been registered with that party for six months.

*26 O.S. §§ 5-105(A), (B), 5-112(A), 6-106 · statutory · read in statute*

Nothing in the filing law limits who may sign by how they voted in a primary.
Do not write that primary voters are barred from signing.

*26 O.S. § 5-112 · statutory · read in statute*

For **city and town** offices with nonpartisan elections there is no separate
independent route, because every candidate files as an independent. Municipal
candidates pay no filing fee and file no petition.

*11 O.S. § 16-105.1; 26 O.S. § 13-104 · statutory · read in statute*

For **school boards**, every candidate files the same declaration with the county
election board.

*26 O.S. § 13A-105 · statutory · read in statute*

## County government

77 counties, each with a page (`research/pages-by-state.csv`).

*26 O.S. § 2-110 · statutory · read in statute*

### Board of county commissioners

Every county is governed by a **board of county commissioners** with **three
members**. The county is split into three commissioner districts, and each
commissioner is elected by the voters of their own district, in a partisan
election, for a four-year term.

*Okla. Const. art. XVII, § 2; 19 O.S. §§ 131(C), 321(A) · statutory · read in statute*

The terms are staggered: districts 1 and 3 are elected in the years Oklahoma
elects its governor, and district 2 in presidential years.

*19 O.S. § 131(C) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never call this body a county council, a commission court or a
board of supervisors, and never give it more than three members. Our position
label for it is "county legislature executive board"; do not use that wording in
copy.

### Other elected county offices

Every county elects, for four-year terms:

- **in presidential years:** a county clerk, a court clerk and a sheriff
- **in governor's-race years:** a county assessor and a county treasurer

*19 O.S. § 131(A), (B) · statutory · read in statute*

County pages also carry **associate district judge** races. These are judges of
the state district court, elected without party labels. Never describe them as
county officers or give them a party.

*26 O.S. §§ 11-108, 11-113 · statutory · read in statute*

The district attorney is elected every four years in a "district attorney's
district", not by county. Do not describe the office as a county office.

*19 O.S. § 215.20 · statutory · read in statute*

### County charters

Oklahoma does not give counties general home rule. Only a county of fewer than
550,000 people that contains a metro area of 250,000 or more may adopt a County
Home Rule Charter. Such a charter may reshape the governing body, but that body
must still be elected, for terms of no more than four years, and the charter may
not conflict with state public policy. Elections stay on the state's dates.

*19 O.S. §§ 8.2, 8.4(A), 8.5; 26 O.S. § 3-101(B) · statutory · read in statute*

Do not name which counties qualify or say that any county has adopted a charter.
Neither was checked for this doc.

### Townships

The constitution let each county vote to abolish township government. Our data
has no Oklahoma township pages, and no township offices appear in our race data.
Do not write about township government in Oklahoma. "Township" in the
constitution's county boundary descriptions is a survey line, not a government.

*Okla. Const. art. V, § 5a; art. XVII, § 8 · statutory · read in statute*

## City and town government

### Cities and towns

Oklahoma has two kinds of municipality: **cities** and **towns**. A place needs
at least 1,000 inhabitants to incorporate as a city.

*11 O.S. §§ 1-102(5), 4-101 · statutory · read in statute*

A statutory city runs under one of three forms. A town has one.

- **Aldermanic city:** a mayor elected at large, plus one or two councilmembers
  per ward. By default it also elects a clerk, treasurer, marshal and street
  commissioner, but its voters may make any of those appointed. This is why some
  of our city pages show a police chief or street commissioner on the ballot.
  *11 O.S. §§ 9-102, 16-201, 16-203, 16-207(A) · usual case · read in statute*
- **Council-manager city:** one councilmember per ward plus one at large. The
  council chooses the mayor from among its members and appoints a city manager.
  *11 O.S. §§ 10-102, 10-104, 10-112 · statutory · read in statute*
- **Strong-mayor-council city:** a mayor elected at large, who also sits as the
  at-large councilmember, plus one councilmember per ward.
  *11 O.S. § 11-102 · statutory · read in statute*
- **Town (board of trustees):** three or five trustees. The trustees choose the
  mayor from among themselves. By default a town also elects a clerk and a
  treasurer, but its voters may make them appointed.
  *11 O.S. §§ 12-102, 12-104, 16-205, 16-207(B) · usual case · read in statute*

In statutory cities and towns, a councilmember or trustee "from a ward" is still
**elected by the voters of the whole municipality**. The ward sets where the
candidate lives, not who votes.

*11 O.S. § 16-104; 11 O.S. § 12-102 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not say a city or town "elects its mayor". In council-manager
cities and in towns, the council or trustees pick the mayor. Do not say "voters
in Ward 2 elect" for a statutory city or town either. Let race data show which
seats are on the ballot, and say the place "has a mayor and a council (or board
of trustees)".

### Does class follow population?

No. A town that reaches 1,000 people stays a town unless its voters approve
becoming a city. Population only sets who may apply.

*11 O.S. § 4-103 · statutory · read in statute*

### Charter cities (home rule)

Any city or town of 2,000 or more may adopt a charter, which the governor must
approve. Where a charter conflicts with state municipal law, the charter wins,
and the state's municipal election rules do not apply to charter cities at all,
except the list of allowed dates.

*Okla. Const. art. XVIII, § 3(a); 11 O.S. §§ 13-101, 13-109, 16-102(A); 26 O.S. § 13-103(E) · statutory · read in statute*

So home rule here reaches elections: a charter city may set its own council
size, terms, wards, partisanship and runoff rules. Only the election **dates**
stay with state law.

*varies by place · read in statute*

### Can terms or council size change locally?

The default term for an elected municipal official is four years, with seats
staggered so roughly half come up at each April election.

*11 O.S. §§ 8-102, 16-201, 16-205 · usual case · read in statute*

Statutory places can change some things by a vote of their residents: an
aldermanic city between one and two councilmembers per ward, a town between
three and five trustees, and either one whether its clerk or treasurer is
elected. Charter cities change anything their charter allows.

*11 O.S. §§ 9-102, 12-102, 16-207 · statutory · read in statute*

### School and special districts

School districts are **independent** (with a high school) or **elementary**
(kindergarten through eighth grade).

*70 O.S. §§ 5-101, 5-103 · statutory · read in statute*

Independent district boards usually have five members on five-year terms, so
one seat is up each April. Seven-member boards (four-year terms) exist and may
shrink to five. Elementary district boards have three members.

*70 O.S. § 5-107A(A), (C) · usual case · read in statute*

Most boards are elected by **board district**, one member per district. A
district with fewer than 1,800 students may elect all members at large. In a
district with more than 10,000 students, the board-district race runs in two
steps, and a district over 30,000 students may add a board chair elected at
large.

*70 O.S. §§ 5-107A(B), 5-107B(A) · varies by place · read in statute*

Do not state a board's size or term for a named district. Let race data supply
it.

Other special districts (fire protection, conservation and similar) follow
their own statutes. Several of our county pages carry conservation district
seats. Describe them only from race data.

*varies by place*

### Cities that cross county lines

A city may spread over more than one county; its elections are run by the
county where its central offices are. Our page sits under one county slug,
which may not be the only county the city is in. Do not write "the city of X in
Y County" as if it were the whole story.

*26 O.S. § 13-110 · statutory · read in statute*

Only counties containing a large metro area qualify, and a charter county's
governing body may not be the usual three commissioners. If race data on a
county page shows something other than three commissioners, the county may be
using a charter. Do not describe it from this doc.

*19 O.S. §§ 8.2, 8.4(A) · varies by place · read in statute*

## Writing rules

These are the ways Oklahoma copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Check the page type before writing.** County, city or town, and school
   district pages each have their own year, primary and party rules.
2. **Never infer a county from a place name.** 12 Oklahoma place names match a
   county name but sit in a different county: Cherokee (Alfalfa County), Carter
   (Beckham County), Caddo (Bryan County), Noble (Cleveland County), McCurtain
   (Haskell County), Marshall (Logan County), Haskell (Muskogee County), Choctaw
   (Oklahoma County), Cleveland (Pawnee County), Kiowa (Pittsburg County),
   Comanche (Stephens County) and Dewey (Washington County). Always take the
   county from the page.
3. **Never say Oklahoma local elections are nonpartisan** (or partisan) without
   naming the tier. Counties are partisan by law; cities and towns are
   nonpartisan by default only.
4. **Never call Oklahoma primaries open.** They are closed, and a party may
   choose to admit Independents.
5. **Do not give a city or town race a runoff or a November date** unless race
   data shows one.
6. **School boards vote every April**, not in November and not every other year.
7. A county board of commissioners always has three members elected by
   district. Never call it a council or board of supervisors.
8. Do not say a city or town elects its mayor, and do not say a ward's voters
   alone elect a councilmember.
9. Never call an associate district judge a county officer or give the race a
   party.
10. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
11. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
    contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures, and which parties
    currently admit Independents to their primaries.

### What search results get wrong about Oklahoma

- "Oklahoma has open primaries": they are closed; parties may admit Independents.
- "Primary day is the last Tuesday in June": the law now says the third Tuesday
  in June. Older material gives other dates.
- "City elections are partisan" or "all local elections are nonpartisan": both
  are wrong somewhere in the state.
- "School board elections are in November": they are in April, with a February
  primary.
- "County commissioners serve two-year terms" or "five-member county boards":
  three members, four-year terms.

For the data team, not for copy.

- `ok/choctaw/nicoma-park-schools` is the Choctaw-Nicoma Park school district.
  The slash in its name split the slug, so it sits under a fake parent
  (`ok/choctaw`) and is counted as a municipal page. It is a school district.
- 44 position URLs have the form
  `ok/<county>/<city>/city-clerk/position/treasurer-joint` (for example
  `ok/jackson-county/altus/city-clerk/position/treasurer-joint`). The joint
  clerk-treasurer office was split into a fake place slot, and the parent
  location URL (`.../altus/city-clerk`) returns 404.
- District slugs `ok/atoka`, `ok/arcadia` and `ok/asher` lack the usual
  "-schools" suffix, and `ok/atoka` and `ok/atoka-public-schools` both exist.
  Possible duplicate districts.
- `ok/wilson-public-schools-4032880` carries an id suffix, presumably to
  separate two districts named Wilson.
- Five school district pages carry district attorney races
  (`burns-flat-dill-city-schools`, `frontier-public-schools`,
  `oklahoma-union-public-schools`, `thomas-fay-custer-unified-schools`,
  `timberlake-public-schools`). District attorneys are elected by multi-county

  district pages.
- Our label "city legislature" is used on town pages too. Oklahoma towns have

`https://www.oklegislature.gov/OK_Statutes/CompleteTitles/os<title>.pdf` (for

`https://www.oklegislature.gov/OKStatutes/CompleteTitles/AllOKConstitutionArticles.rtf`.

(`PDFDocument(url:)!.string`) turns a title into text in about 30 seconds, and
`textutil -convert txt` handles the RTF. Each section ends with its amendment
history ("Laws 2025, c. ..."). Strip the "Oklahoma Statutes - Title N ... Page N"
header lines before matching quotes. Do not use the RTF statute files for quote
checks: formatting codes split sentences.

the in-app browser alike. Use it sparingly and never try to pass the check. For
that reason every ledger entry is marked manual and cites the Legislature's

those files with the same matching `verify-ledger.ts` uses (96 of 96 found).

Aggregator sites were not used and must not appear in published copy. Statutes
