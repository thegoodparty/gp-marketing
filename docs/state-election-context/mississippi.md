# Mississippi: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Mississippi so you do not have to research the same
ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which
offices are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race
data, which always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean. Every claim below has a verbatim quote
in [`research/mississippi.json`](./research/mississippi.json).

**The one thing to know first:** Mississippi runs three local calendars, and
none of them is the even-year November most readers expect. County offices are
elected every four years, in the same November election as the governor (the
year before a presidential election), after party primaries in August. Cities,
towns and villages vote every four years in June, the year after a presidential
election, after party primaries in April. School boards are elected in
November of whichever year a seat's term ends, and judges in November on their
own cycles, all without party labels. County and
city races are partisan contests with party primaries, and independents get on
the ballot by petition. Since 2025 a county candidate needs a majority in
November, or faces a runoff four weeks later.

## Legislative calendar

Next regular session: convenes 5 January 2027 (the Tuesday after the first
Monday in January, every year, Miss. Const. art. 4, § 36). The 2027 session is
limited to 90 calendar days, so it is due to end in early April. Only every
fourth session (2024, 2028) runs 125 days. Each house can extend a session by 30
days at a time with a two-thirds vote, with no limit on extensions (§ 36).

The governor has five days, Sundays excepted, to act on a bill during the
session, or it becomes law without a signature. When adjournment prevents its
return, the governor has 15 days, Sundays excepted, after presentment to veto it
(Miss. Const. art. 4, § 72). So the last bills of a 90-day session are settled
by late April, later if the session is extended.

New laws take effect on the date each act sets in its own effective-date
clause. The 2025 county runoff law, for example, took effect for elections from
1 January 2025 (Laws 2025, HB 291). Check the act rather than assuming a date
(usual case).

## How elections work in Mississippi

Safe on any Mississippi page, at the tier named.

### Election days

**County offices** are elected at the general state election, held every four
years counting from 2023: the same November ballot as the governor and the
Legislature. That puts county races in the year before a presidential election.
Terms are four years. The general election is on the Tuesday after the first
Monday of November.

*Miss. Code Ann. § 23-15-193(1); Miss. Const. art. 12, § 252 · statutory · read in statute*

The offices on that ballot include the five supervisors, the circuit and
chancery clerks, the sheriff, coroner, tax assessor, surveyor, justice court
judges and constables.

*Miss. Code Ann. § 23-15-193(1) · statutory · read in statute*

**County party primaries** are on the first Tuesday after the first Monday of
August in the election year. A candidate needs a majority to win the
nomination; otherwise the top two meet in a second primary four weeks later,
where the most votes wins.

*Miss. Code Ann. § 23-15-191 · statutory · read in statute*

**Cities, towns and villages** hold a general municipal election every four years
counting from 1985, on the first Tuesday after the first Monday of June. That
puts city races in the year after a presidential election. Party primaries come
first, on the first Tuesday in April, with any second primary on the fourth
Tuesday in April. Officers take office on the first weekday of July after the
election and serve four years.

*Miss. Code Ann. §§ 23-15-171(1), 23-15-173(1), 21-15-1 · usual case · read in statute*

The exception is a city with a special or private charter whose board declined
the state schedule by 25 June 1952. It holds its primaries and general election
when its charter says. Some Mississippi cities therefore vote at other times.
Never give a city's election month unless race data shows it.

*Miss. Code Ann. §§ 23-15-171(2), 23-15-173(3) · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** municipal primaries moved from May to April in 2017 (Laws 2017,
HB 467). No later law has moved them. Older material that says May is out of
date.

*Miss. Code Ann. § 23-15-171(1) (Laws 2017, HB 467, struck text) · statutory · read in statute*

**School boards and school trustees** are elected at the November general
election in whichever year a seat's term ends, so some seats are on the ballot
almost every November, odd years included. Trustee elections are on the first
Tuesday after the first Monday in November of each year. County board of
education members serve six-year terms.

*Miss. Code Ann. §§ 37-7-215, 37-5-7(1) · usual case · read in statute*

**Judges** (including the county's justice court judges and, where a county has
one, its county court judge) and **county election commissioners** are also
elected in November without a party primary (see Partisanship).

### Primary type

Mississippi parties nominate county and city candidates in primaries. The
Election Code limits primary voting this way: a voter may vote in only one
party's primary on a given day, and a voter who voted in one party's first
primary may not vote in another party's second primary.

*Miss. Code Ann. § 23-15-575 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not write that voters must belong to a party to vote in its
primary, and do not describe primary voting rules beyond the two limits above.

Ranked-choice voting is banned in statewide, county, local, municipal and
school district elections.

*Laws 2024, SB 2144 · statutory · read in statute*

### Partisanship

Partisanship splits by office, not by county versus city:

- **County offices** (supervisors, sheriff, clerks, assessor, tax collector,
  coroner, surveyor, constables): partisan. Party primaries in August, party
  labels in November, independents by petition.

  *Miss. Code Ann. §§ 23-15-191, 23-15-359 · statutory · read in statute*

- **City, town and village offices**: partisan by law. Party nominations for
  every elective municipal office are made in primaries, and a candidate cannot
  qualify with a party that has no organized municipal executive committee in
  that place. Independents run by petition and are listed as independents, so
  never assume a race has a party nominee.

  *Miss. Code Ann. §§ 23-15-309(1), (3), 23-15-361 · statutory · read in statute*

- **Judges, including justice court judges**: nonpartisan. Since 2024 justice
  court judges are elected under the nonpartisan judicial election law, from
  single-member districts drawn by the board of supervisors. Judicial
  candidates may not align themselves with a political party during a campaign.

  *Miss. Code Ann. §§ 23-15-973, 23-15-975, 9-11-2(2) · statutory · read in statute*

- **School boards and school trustees**: nonpartisan, on the November ballot
  only, with no party designation. Trustee candidates qualify by nominating
  petition.

  *Secretary of State, 2026 County Election Handbook, p. 11; Miss. Code Ann. § 37-7-225 · statutory · Secretary of State publication*

- **County election commissioners**: no primary. Candidates qualify by
  petition, and each candidate's party affiliation, if any, is printed on the
  ballot.

  *Miss. Code Ann. § 23-15-213(3) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** "local races are nonpartisan" is wrong for every county office
and every city office in Mississippi. "Mississippi local races are partisan" is
wrong for judges and school boards.

### Winning an election

- **County offices:** since 1 January 2025 a candidate needs a majority at the
  November general election. If nobody has one, the top two meet in a runoff
  four weeks later.

  *Miss. Code Ann. § 23-15-193(2) (Laws 2025, HB 291) · statutory · read in statute*

- **Party primaries** (county and city): majority, with a second primary
  between the top two. County runoffs are four weeks later, city runoffs on the
  fourth Tuesday in April (three weeks later).

  *Miss. Code Ann. §§ 23-15-191, 23-15-171(1) · statutory · read in statute*

- **City general elections:** there is no runoff. The candidate with the most
  votes wins.

  *Secretary of State, 2026 County Election Handbook, p. 11 n.1; Miss. Code Ann. § 21-9-19 · usual case · Secretary of State publication*

- **Judges and school trustees:** majority, with a runoff four weeks later.

  *Miss. Code Ann. §§ 23-15-981, 37-7-215; Laws 2024, SB 2144 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** older material says runoffs are three weeks later and that county
races in November go to whoever has the most votes. Both changed in 2024 and
2025.

### Running as an independent

For **county and supervisors district offices**, an independent files a
petition with the circuit clerk, signed by qualified electors of the county or
the supervisors district, and appears on the ballot as an independent.

*Miss. Code Ann. § 23-15-359(1), (2)(a), (4) · statutory · read in statute*

For **city offices**, an independent files a petition with the municipal clerk
by the party candidates' qualifying deadline, and is listed as an independent.

*Miss. Code Ann. § 23-15-361(1)-(2) · statutory · read in statute*

Judicial, school board and election commissioner candidates are not nominated
in party primaries at all: see Partisanship.

## County government

Mississippi has 82 counties, and our data has a page for each.

*Miss. Code Ann. §§ 19-1-1 to 19-1-163 · statutory · read in statute*

### Board of supervisors

Every county is divided into five districts, and the voters of each district
elect one **supervisor** every four years. The five supervisors form the
**board of supervisors**, the county's governing body. The chancery clerk is
the board's clerk. The board may redraw the districts by a three-fifths vote.

*Miss. Const. art. 6, § 170; Miss. Code Ann. § 19-3-1 · statutory · read in statute*

State law also calls a supervisors district a **beat** (each supervisor
nominates a board member "from each such beat"; constables "of any beat").

*Miss. Code Ann. §§ 41-13-29, 37-106-39 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** our county pages label the board "County Council". The
constitution makes the board of supervisors the governing body in every county.
Always write "board of supervisors", and "supervisor" for a member.

### Other elected county offices

Each county elects a **sheriff, coroner, tax assessor, tax collector and
surveyor** for four-year terms. The Legislature may combine any of these
offices in a county, and many counties have a single **tax assessor and
collector**.

*Miss. Const. art. 5, § 135; Miss. Code Ann. § 23-15-193(1) · statutory · read in statute (the "many counties" part is from our race data: 55 county pages carry a joint assessor-collector office)*

Counties also elect a **circuit clerk** and a **chancery clerk**, at least two
**justice court judges** (two to five, by population) from single-member
districts, and **constables**.

*Miss. Code Ann. §§ 23-15-193(1), 9-11-2; Miss. Const. art. 6, § 171 · statutory · read in statute*

Each county also elects five **election commissioners**, one from each
supervisors district, for four-year terms. Districts One, Three and Five vote
in presidential-election Novembers; Districts Two and Four move to the
midterm-election Novembers after a one-time shortened term.

*Miss. Code Ann. § 23-15-213(1)-(2) · statutory · read in statute*

Our race data also shows a county prosecuting attorney on most county pages and
a county court judge on 28 of them. Take those from race data; do not say every
county has them.

### Home rule

Boards of supervisors have statutory home rule over county affairs that general
law does not cover, but it expressly does not let a county change the
requirements, practices or procedures for county elections, or create a new
elective office. So everything above holds for every county. None of the county
law read for this doc gives a county a charter option.

*Miss. Code Ann. § 19-3-40(1), (3)(c) · statutory · read in statute*

### Townships

None of the county law read for this doc creates townships. The units below
the county that matter for elections are the five supervisors districts
(beats) above.

## City, town and village government

State law names three kinds of municipality: **city, town and village**. Use the
name on the page. Do not infer which one a place is from its population.

*Miss. Code Ann. § 23-15-173(1); Miss. Const. art. 4, § 88 · statutory · read in statute*

Mississippi municipalities are either **code charter** (governed by the general
municipal law) or **special or private charter** (governed by its own charter).

*Miss. Code Ann. §§ 21-3-3, 21-3-7(2), 23-15-173(3) · statutory · read in statute*

### What a code charter municipality elects

The default form is a **mayor and board of aldermen**. The elective offices are
the mayor, the aldermen, the municipal judge, the marshal or chief of police,
the tax collector and the tax assessor. By ordinance, the board may make every
office except mayor and aldermen appointive, but not within 90 days before a
regular election. Our race data shows an elected police chief or marshal in
only 10 Mississippi towns, so take these offices from race data.

*Miss. Code Ann. § 21-3-3 · usual case · read in statute*

- **Under 10,000 people:** five aldermen, either all at large or one from each
  of four wards plus one at large. A town of 500 or fewer may cut to three by a
  vote of its residents.
- **10,000 or more:** seven aldermen, either all at large or one from each of
  six wards plus one at large.
- Voters decide between wards and at large in a special election if 20% of
  them petition for one.

*Miss. Code Ann. § 21-3-7(1)-(3) · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** under this law even a ward alderman is chosen by the whole
town's electorate, from candidates who live in the ward. Do not write that only
a ward's residents vote for its alderman unless race data says so.

*Miss. Code Ann. § 21-3-7 · usual case · read in statute*

### Other forms

A municipality may instead adopt the **mayor-council** form: a mayor and a
council of five, seven or nine, elected by ward or by ward plus at large, at
the regular June election. Under the **council-manager** form the voters elect
a mayor and councilmen, and the candidate with the most votes wins. Under
either form the office is "council member", not "alderman".

*Miss. Code Ann. §§ 21-8-7(1), (2), (4)(a), 21-9-19 · varies by place · read in statute*

Special charter cities keep the number of aldermen, council members or
commissioners their charter sets.

*Miss. Code Ann. § 21-3-7(2) · varies by place · read in statute*

### Home rule

Every municipality has statutory home rule over municipal affairs, but it
expressly does not let a city change the requirements, practices or procedures
for municipal elections, or create a new elective office. So a code charter
city cannot move its election date or make its races nonpartisan. The only
timing exception is the pre-1952 charter rule above.

*Miss. Code Ann. § 21-17-5(1), (2)(c) · statutory · read in statute*

## School districts

Mississippi school boards come in several kinds, and not all are elected by
voters:

- **County school districts** are run by a **county board of education**
  elected by supervisors district for six-year terms in November.
- **Consolidated and other school districts** elect trustees in November.
- **Municipal separate school districts** (city school districts) have five
  trustees with five-year terms. By default the **city's governing body chooses
  them**. Only trustees for "added territory" outside the city limits are
  elected by voters, and in some mayor-council cities the mayor appoints the
  trustees and the council confirms.

*Miss. Code Ann. §§ 37-5-7(1), 37-7-203(1), (3), 37-7-215 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never write that voters elect a city school district's board.
Some seats are elected and many are not. Use race data: if a school board race
is on the page, it is an elected seat.

## Metro and major-place exceptions

### Jackson

`ms/hinds-county/jackson`

The capital is in Hinds County, not in Jackson County. Jackson's mayor and council are on the June municipal calendar. Hinds
County's board of supervisors and county offices are on the November
county calendar, and the city's page also lists them. Do not mix the two
calendars in one sentence.

*Miss. Code Ann. §§ 23-15-173(1), 23-15-193(1) · statutory · read in statute*

### Cities with special or private charters

Some of the older cities hold elections under their own charters (see Election
days). Do not state a city's election month, form of government or council size
unless race data shows it.

### Delta levee boards

Our county pages in the Delta (Bolivar, Coahoma, DeSoto, Holmes, Humphreys,
Issaquena, Leflore, Quitman, Sharkey, Sunflower, Tallahatchie, Tunica,
Washington and Yazoo) carry levee board races. Describe them only from race
data.

### Cities across county lines

Each of our pages files a city under one county. Do not write that a city lies
wholly within the county on its page.

## Writing rules

These are the ways Mississippi copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never infer a county from a place name.** Six of our Mississippi place
   pages share a name with a different Mississippi county: Jackson (in Hinds
   County), Quitman (Clarke County), Prentiss (Jefferson Davis County), Marion
   (Lauderdale County), Union (Newton County) and Walthall (Webster County). The
   list is in
   [`research/places-named-after-another-county.json`](./research/places-named-after-another-county.json).
   Always take the county from the page data.
2. **Use the right calendar for the office.** County offices: November, every
   four years, the year before a presidential election, August primaries. City
   offices: June, every four years, the year after a presidential election,
   April primaries. School boards and judges: November of the year a term ends.
   Never say Mississippi local elections are in even years.
3. **Call the county governing body the board of supervisors**, never a county
   council or commission, whatever our label says.
4. **Do not call county or city races nonpartisan.** Judges and school boards
   are nonpartisan; county and city offices are not.
5. Do not call Mississippi primaries closed, and do not describe primary voting
   rules beyond the two limits in Primary type.
6. **Runoffs:** county races need a majority in November (since 2025) and party
   primaries need a majority. City general elections have no runoff. Runoffs
   are four weeks later, except city primaries (fourth Tuesday in April).
7. Do not say voters elect a city school district's board, or that every county
   elects a superintendent of education. Our data has no superintendent races.
8. Do not give a special charter city's election month, or any city's form of
   government, unless race data shows it.
9. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
10. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
    contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.

### What search results get wrong about Mississippi

- "Mississippi local elections are nonpartisan": wrong for every county and
  city office.
- "Municipal primaries are in May": they moved to April in 2017.
- "Runoffs are three weeks later": four weeks since 2024, except city
  primaries.
- "County races are won by plurality": a majority has been required since 2025.
- "County commission" or "county council": Mississippi has boards of
  supervisors.
- "Jackson County" for the capital: Jackson is in Hinds County.

  the table of contents.
- **Bulk source used instead:** the Legislature's enacted bills, which print
  every amended or "brought forward" section in full. The list of every measure
  in a session is at
  `https://billstatus.ls.state.ms.us/<year>/pdf/all_measures/allmsrs.xml`; each
  bill's HTML is the same path as its PDF link with `/html/` and `.htm`
  (e.g. `documents/2025/html/HB/0200-0299/HB0291SG.htm`). Deleted words are
  inside `<s>` tags with `display:none`, so quotes are taken from unchanged
  text. All enacted bills from 2011 to 2026 were downloaded and indexed by the
  sections they amend; each claim cites the latest act that printed the
  section. The site sends an incomplete certificate chain: download
  `http://secure.globalsign.com/cacert/gsrsaovsslca2018.crt`, convert it with
  `openssl x509 -inform der`, and run the checker with `NODE_EXTRA_CA_CERTS`.
- **Constitution:** Secretary of State PDF, updated May 2022,
  `https://www.sos.ms.gov/sites/default/files/publications/Mississippi_Constitution.pdf`
  (text via macOS PDFKit; ledger entries are manual).
- **Secretary of State publication:** 2026 County Election Handbook (revised
  December 2025), for the nonpartisan school board rule and the absence of
  municipal general election runoffs.
- **Currency check:** every enacted bill from 2011 to 2026 was indexed, and
  each cited section is quoted from the latest act that printed it. The 2026
  First Extraordinary Session dealt only with youth court. Relevant
  changes: runoffs moved from three to four weeks and ranked-choice voting
  banned (2024, SB 2144); justice court judges made nonpartisan (2024, HB 1035);
  county offices need a majority in November (2025, HB 291); election
  commissioner cycles changed (2025, HB 289). No law since 2017 moved municipal
  elections or primaries.
- **Page data:** goodparty.org sitemap shard 25, 28 October 2026, read in the
