# Iowa: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Iowa so you do not have to research the same ground
on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which offices are
actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race data, which
always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean.

**The one thing to know first:** Iowa has exactly one class of municipality.
There are no villages, boroughs or townships to sort out and no population tiers.
Everything is a "city". What varies instead is the city's *form* of government,
and that is not published anywhere.

## Legislative calendar

## How elections work in Iowa

Safe on any Iowa page, at any tier.

### Election days

Cities hold their regular city election on the first Tuesday after the first
Monday in November of each **odd-numbered** year. It shares a ballot with the
school election, so Iowans often call it the city-school election.

*Iowa Code § 376.1(1) · statutory · read in statute*

This one is unusually safe: no Iowa city charter of any kind can move it, which
makes it the one municipal fact that holds on all Iowa city pages regardless of a
city's form. Worth leading with.

County offices are filled at the general election, the first Tuesday after the
first Monday in November of each **even-numbered** year. Supervisors serve
four-year staggered terms, so some seats come up each cycle.

*Iowa Code §§ 39.1, 39.18, 331.201(4) · statutory · read in statute*

### Primary type

Partially open. The party primary is held in June of even-numbered years. A voter
receives the ballot of the party they are registered with, but any registered
voter may change or declare a party affiliation at the polls on election day and
vote in that primary.

*Iowa Code ch. 43 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** the change is sworn and entered on the voter's registration
record, so it persists after the election. Do not describe Iowa as a simple open
primary; a voter who switches at the polls stays switched.

### Partisanship

County elections in Iowa are **partisan**. Supervisors, auditor, sheriff,
treasurer, recorder and county attorney are nominated by the parties at the June
primary and carry a party label in November.

*Iowa Code §§ 43.6, 39.17, 331.238(3) · statutory · read in statute*

Four county-level offices are elected on a **nonpartisan** basis even though the
rest of the county ballot is partisan: county public hospital trustees, soil and
water conservation district commissioners, county agricultural extension council
members, and township officers. The statutory list is closed, so this can be
relied on.

*Iowa Code §§ 39.21, 39.22(2)(a) · statutory · read in statute*

City elections are nonpartisan. Candidates do not appear with a party label.

*usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** one narrow exception survives in statute. A city that held a
special charter in 1973 and has over 50,000 people may run partisan city
elections unless it has voted otherwise. Nobody has confirmed whether any city
still uses it. On pages for Iowa's largest cities, either hedge this or leave
partisanship out of city copy entirely.

## County government

99 counties, all on the same model.

Every Iowa county is governed by a **board of supervisors**, elected to four-year
staggered terms. Counties also elect an auditor, sheriff, treasurer, recorder and
county attorney, all partisan.

*Iowa Code §§ 331.201, 39.17 · statutory · read in statute*

### Board size

Three supervisors, or five if the county has voted to expand. Those are the only
two sizes.

*Iowa Code §§ 331.201(1), 331.203 · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not state a supervisor count for a named county. Say the county
is run by a board of supervisors and let race data supply the seats.

### How supervisors are elected

One of three plans, chosen by the county: at large with no residence requirement,
at large but with each member residing in a district, or from single-member
districts. Which plan a county uses is not published anywhere we can reach.

*Iowa Code §§ 331.206, 331.207 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never say whether an Iowa county elects supervisors at large or by
district unless race data says so. The representation rules were also reworked by
the legislature in 2025 with a transition running through the 2026 election, so
this is actively moving.

### Townships

Iowa townships elect trustees and a clerk on a nonpartisan basis at the general
election. A county may put the offices on an appointment basis by a vote of the
township.

*Iowa Code §§ 39.22, 39.21(4) · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** townships are **not** cities under Iowa law. In our data, township
offices appear in the municipality slot of position URLs, and many of those
township names have no place page at all. Never treat a township as a
municipality.

## City government

"City" is the only class of Iowa municipality. There are roughly 940 of them and
they range from a few dozen people to Des Moines. Size does not change a city's
legal class.

*Iowa Code § 362.2(4) · statutory · read in statute*

Every Iowa city has a mayor and a council, and the council is always elected by
the voters. Council size and whether seats are at large or by ward depend on the
city's form.

*Iowa Code § 372.13(11) · statutory · read in statute*

### The mayor is not always elected

Under the council-manager-at-large form the council picks one of its own members
as mayor rather than the voters electing one. Under the other forms the mayor is
elected. Since the form is not knowable per city, neither is this.

*Iowa Code §§ 372.6, 372.7 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not write that an Iowa city elects its mayor. Say the city has
a mayor and a council, and let race data say whether a mayoral seat is on the
ballot. Only about 145 of our 939 Iowa city pages carry a mayoral position at
all, which fits.

### The clerk is usually appointed

The council appoints the city clerk. This is the opposite of some neighbouring
states, so copy reused across states will otherwise put a clerk on an Iowa ballot
where there is not one.

*Iowa Code § 372.13(3) · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** a special charter city may elect its clerk and treasurer, so hedge
rather than saying "never elected".

### Forms of government

Iowa cities choose among eight forms: mayor-council (with or without an appointed
manager), commission, council-manager-at-large, council-manager-ward, home rule
charter, special charter, city-county consolidated, and community commonwealth. A
newly incorporated city starts as mayor-council and keeps that form until it
adopts another.

*Iowa Code §§ 372.1(1), 372.1(2) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** which form a given city uses is not published anywhere we can
reach, and the form determines council size, whether the mayor is elected, and
term length. So do not name an Iowa city's form of government. Describe the
offices on the ballot instead.

### Terms

Two years by default, but the council-manager forms set four-year terms by
statute and any city may move to four years by petition and election.

*Iowa Code § 376.2(2) · usual case · read in statute*

Do not state an Iowa city term length. It depends on the form.

## Metro and major-place exceptions

Iowa has fewer of these than Missouri or Pennsylvania, because there is one
municipal class and no charter counties. The exceptions that exist are about form
and about size, not about class.

### Iowa's largest cities

Des Moines, Cedar Rapids, Davenport, Sioux City, Iowa City, Waterloo, Council
Bluffs, Ames, West Des Moines, Ankeny.

Large Iowa cities are more likely to use a council-manager form, which means the
mayor may be chosen by the council rather than elected, terms may be four years
rather than two, and seats may be a mix of at-large and ward. None of that is
knowable per city from anything we hold, so these are the pages where the general
templates are most likely to be wrong. Lean hardest on race data here.

*varies by place · read in statute*

### Special charter cities over 50,000

The only Iowa cities that could still hold **partisan** city elections, and the
only ones that may elect a clerk or treasurer. The class is closed (special
charter cannot be newly adopted) and probably dormant, but it has not been
confirmed either way.

*Iowa Code §§ 376.3, 420.41(1)(d), 43.112 · statutory · read in statute*

This is why city partisanship is hedged rather than flat. If a page is for one of
Iowa's biggest cities, do not assert nonpartisan elections.

### Counties containing a state regents university

Johnson County (Iowa City), Story County (Ames), Black Hawk County (Cedar Falls).

Required to elect supervisors from single-member districts rather than at large.
This was changed by the legislature in 2025 with a transition running through the
2026 election, so treat it as moving and do not build copy on it yet.

*Iowa Code § 331.206(2)(a) · statutory · read in statute*

## Writing rules

These are the ways Iowa copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never infer a county from a place name.** 36 Iowa county names are also city
   names and 17 of those cities sit in a *different* county. Des Moines the city
   is in Polk County. Jefferson is in Greene County. Greene is in Butler County.
   Always take the county from the page data.
2. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
3. Do not name an Iowa city's form of government. It controls council size,
   whether the mayor is elected and term length, and it is not published.
4. Do not say an Iowa city elects its mayor. Say it has a mayor and a council.
5. Do not state a city term length or a supervisor count.
6. Do not put a city clerk on an Iowa ballot. The council appoints one.
7. Never describe a township as a city. Iowa law excludes townships from the
   definition, and our position data mixes them into the municipality slot.
8. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
   contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.
