# Minnesota: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Minnesota so you do not have to research the same
ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which
offices are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race
data, which always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean.

**The one thing to know first:** every local office in Minnesota is nonpartisan
by statute, **county offices included**. Partisan offices are only federal,
statewide and legislative. And more than half of our Minnesota municipal pages
are **townships**, not cities, with their own election day in March.

## How elections work in Minnesota

Safe on any Minnesota page, at any tier, except where a home rule charter city
is noted.

### Nonpartisan at every local level

Minnesota law defines "nonpartisan offices" as all judicial, county, municipal,
school district and special district offices. "Partisan offices" are federal
offices, constitutional (statewide) offices and legislative offices, and nothing
else.

*Minn. Stat. § 200.02, subds. 27, 28 · statutory · read in statute*

Nobody can be nominated by petition for a nonpartisan office, so there is no
"independent" route for a local race. Every candidate files the same affidavit
of candidacy and appears without a party label.

*Minn. Stat. § 204B.04, subd. 3 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** this is the opposite of Iowa, where county races are partisan.
Never attach a party to a Minnesota county commissioner, sheriff, county
attorney, mayor, council member, township supervisor or school board member.
For home rule charter cities, see "City government" below: a charter controls
how its own city offices are nominated and elected.

### Election days, by tier

**County.** County offices are on the state general election ballot, the first
Tuesday after the first Monday in November of **even-numbered** years. Counties
cannot move it.

*Minn. Stat. § 204D.03, subd. 2 · statutory · read in statute*

**City.** The default is November of **even-numbered** years. A city may switch
to odd-numbered years by ordinance, passed at least 180 days before filing opens,
and voters can force a referendum on the switch. A city holds its elections in
one or the other, never both.

*Minn. Stat. § 205.07, subds. 1, 3 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** many searches say Minnesota cities vote in odd years. The law
says even years unless the city chose otherwise, so never state which year a
named Minnesota city votes in. Let race data supply it.

**Town (township).** The town general election is the second Tuesday in
**March**. A town may move to November of an even or odd year, but only with a
vote of the town's electors.

*Minn. Stat. § 205.075, subds. 1, 2 · usual case · read in statute*

**School district.** November of either the odd- or even-numbered year, and the
school board may change the year by resolution.

*Minn. Stat. § 205A.04, subds. 1, 3 · statutory · read in statute*

**Special districts.** Soil and water conservation district supervisors are
elected at the state general election in November of even years, with no
primary. Hospital district boards, where they exist, elect one member from each
city and town in the district plus one at large.

*Minn. Stat. §§ 103C.305, 447.32 · statutory · read in statute*

### Primaries

**State and county.** The state primary is the second Tuesday in August of
even-numbered years. On the partisan part of the primary ballot a voter picks
one party's column; a ballot marked for more than one party is not counted on
that section. Election judges may not record which party a voter belongs to or
voted for, so choosing a column commits the voter to nothing afterward.

*Minn. Stat. §§ 204D.03, 204D.08, 204C.13, 204C.18 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** the presidential nomination primary is the one exception. There
the voter asks for a party's ballot and that choice is recorded. Do not carry
that rule over to state or local primaries.

*Minn. Stat. § 207A.12 · statutory · read in statute*

County offices sit on the nonpartisan section of the same August ballot. The top
two per seat advance to November, and when no more than twice as many candidates
file as there are seats, the office skips the primary entirely.

*Minn. Stat. §§ 204D.07, subd. 3, 204D.10, subd. 3 · statutory · read in statute*

**City and school.** A city primary is optional. It runs on the second Tuesday in
August only if the city has chosen to nominate by primary, and only for offices
with more than twice as many candidates as seats. The top two per seat advance.
School boards work the same way.

*Minn. Stat. §§ 205.065, 205A.03 · statutory · read in statute*

So most Minnesota local races go straight to November with no primary at all.
Do not write that a candidate must "win the primary" unless race data shows one.

### Winning

Plurality. The candidate with the most votes is declared elected; there is no
runoff in state law.

*Minn. Stat. §§ 204C.33, subd. 1, 447.32, subd. 4 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** a home rule charter controls nominations and elections for its
city's offices, so a charter city can use a different method. Never describe the
counting method for a named home rule charter city.

### Getting on the ballot

Local candidates file an affidavit of candidacy and pay a filing fee, or file a
petition in place of the fee. Only voters eligible to vote for the candidate may
sign, and nobody may sign for more candidates for one office than there are
seats.

*Minn. Stat. §§ 205.13, 204B.11, subd. 2, 204B.08, subd. 2 · statutory · read in statute*

### Running as an independent for a partisan office

This applies only to the partisan offices: federal, statewide and state
legislative. Location pages rarely carry them, but use this wherever copy
mentions a state house or senate race.

A candidate who is not seeking a major party's nomination skips the August
primary and gets on the November ballot by **nominating petition**, filed in the
same window as primary candidates' affidavits. Someone who runs in the primary
cannot then petition for the same office.

*Minn. Stat. §§ 204B.03, 204B.04, subd. 2, 204B.09, subd. 1 · statutory · read in statute*

The petition names the candidate's party or political principle in three words
or fewer, and that label is printed after the candidate's name in November. It
may not use any part of a major party's name or the word "nonpartisan", but the
word "independent" is allowed.

*Minn. Stat. §§ 204B.07, subd. 1, 204D.13, subd. 3 · statutory · read in statute*

Who may sign: only voters eligible to vote for that candidate, and each voter
may sign petitions for only as many candidates for one office as there are
seats. Each signer also swears that they do not intend to vote in the primary
for that office.

*Minn. Stat. §§ 204B.08, subd. 2, 204B.07, subd. 4 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** that oath is the Minnesota equivalent of "primary voters may not
sign". Phrase it as the oath, since it is a promise about the coming primary,
not a check against a past vote.

## County government

We have 87 county pages, one per county, and no independent city or
consolidated city-county pages. Some land is unorganized territory, outside any
city or town, and the county runs its elections directly.

*Minn. Stat. § 204B.15 · statutory · read in statute*

### The county board

A board of **county commissioners**, one elected from each district, each living
in the district, on four-year terms. Five members is the default. Anoka,
Hennepin, Ramsey and St. Louis counties have seven by law, a county of 100,000
or more may move to seven by resolution, and any county may choose three, five,
seven or nine.

*Minn. Stat. §§ 375.01, 375.025, 375.056, 375A.09 · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not state a commissioner count for a named county. Say the
county is governed by a board of commissioners elected by district.

### Other elected county offices

By default a county elects an auditor, treasurer, sheriff, recorder and county
attorney to four-year terms. A county may combine the auditor and treasurer or
make the auditor, treasurer or recorder appointed.

*Minn. Stat. §§ 382.01, 375A.1205 · usual case · read in statute*

Our race data agrees: every Minnesota county page carries a sheriff and a county
attorney, but only 32 carry a recorder, 3 a treasurer and 2 an auditor. Name only
the county offices race data shows.

### Charters and optional forms

General law does not give counties home rule. A local government may adopt a
charter only when authorized by law, and the only county with that authorization
is **Ramsey**, by special law. Other counties may adopt optional forms (an
elected executive, a county manager and similar plans) by referendum, though
that chapter does not apply to Hennepin County, which has its own special laws.

*Minn. Const. art. XII, § 4; Minn. Stat. §§ 383A.551, 383A.554, 375A.001 · statutory · read in statute*

None of this changes partisanship or the election day: county offices stay
nonpartisan and on the November even-year ballot.

### Townships

Minnesota calls them **towns** in law; residents and our data say township. A
town is a municipality in election law, alongside cities.

*Minn. Stat. § 200.02, subd. 9 · statutory · read in statute*

A town elects a board of **three supervisors** on three-year staggered terms,
plus a clerk and a treasurer on two-year terms. Towns that vote in November
elect to four-year terms instead, and may move supervisors to six. A town may
also enlarge its board to five, or make the clerk and treasurer appointed.

*Minn. Stat. §§ 367.03, 367.30 · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** the office is **supervisor**. Our data files most Minnesota
township board seats as "township trustee" (1,115 positions) and only 50 as
"township supervisor". Copy should say supervisor regardless of the position
label, and must never call a township a city.

## City government

### One kind of municipality: the city

Every incorporated place in Minnesota is a **city**. Villages and boroughs were
folded into one city code in 1973-74, every former village became a city, and
the word "village" is no longer used. What divides cities is not size but
whether they have a charter:

- **Statutory cities** run under the general city code.
- **Home rule charter cities** have adopted their own charter. Any city may do
  this; there is no population threshold.

*Minn. Stat. §§ 412.015, 412.016, 410.015, 410.04 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never call a Minnesota place a village or borough.

### Statutory cities

A statutory city runs under one of three plans:

- **Standard plan** (the default): voters elect a mayor, three or five council
  members, a clerk, and a treasurer unless the clerk and treasurer are combined.
- **Optional Plan A**: the council appoints the clerk and treasurer, and the
  council is a mayor plus four or six members.
- **Optional Plan B**: the council-manager plan, open to cities over 1,000
  people, again with a mayor plus four or six members.

*Minn. Stat. §§ 412.541, 412.631 · usual case · read in statute*

Council members serve four-year terms. The mayor serves two or four years, as
the council sets by ordinance. Voters may move the council between five and seven
seats, and a city may elect its council by ward.

*Minn. Stat. §§ 412.02, subds. 6, 7, 412.022 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** which plan a city uses is not published anywhere we read. Do not
say a Minnesota city elects its clerk or treasurer; only 62 of our city pages
carry a clerk position and 61 a treasurer. Do not state a mayoral term.

### Home rule charter cities

A charter must provide for a mayor and a council elected by the people, and
otherwise may set any form of government, including whether seats are at large
or by ward.

*Minn. Stat. §§ 410.07, 410.16 · statutory · read in statute*

Home rule here does **not** reserve elections to state law. The opposite: a
charter controls nominations, primaries and elections for city offices even
where it conflicts with general law.

*Minn. Stat. § 410.21 · statutory · read in statute*

The state election chapter still applies to charter cities except for the
sections it lists, so the November date rule (even years, or odd by ordinance)
still holds for them. But party labels on the ballot, the primary and the
counting method can all be set by charter.

*Minn. Stat. § 205.02, subd. 2 · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** for a home rule charter city, never assert that city races have
no party label, that there is a primary, or how the winner is chosen. Stick to
the offices race data shows. Which cities have charters is not something we
hold, so treat any large Minnesota city as possibly a charter city.

### Population classes

Cities fall into four classes by population (first class above 100,000), for
legislative purposes only. The class does not set the form of government. A
first class city keeps its class unless its population falls by 25 percent.

*Minn. Stat. § 410.01 · statutory · read in statute*

Do not use the class number in copy. It explains why some laws name "cities of
the first class" but says nothing a reader can use.

## School and special districts

These are part of the third election tier described above.

An independent school district board has **six** elected members on four-year
terms, and voters may make it seven. School races are nonpartisan, usually in
November without a primary.

*Minn. Stat. §§ 123B.09, subd. 1, 205A.03 · usual case · read in statute*

Soil and water conservation district supervisors are nominated by supervisor
district but elected at large. Our data has a soil and water board on 85 pages
and a hospital district board on 129.

*Minn. Stat. § 103C.311 · statutory · read in statute*

`mn/hennepin-county`

`mn/ramsey-county`

Seven commissioners by statute and the only county with a law authorizing a

## Writing rules

These are the ways Minnesota copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never say a Minnesota local race is partisan**, and never attach a party,
   including the DFL, to a county, city, township, school or special district
   candidate. Only federal, statewide and legislative offices are partisan.
2. **Never infer a county from a place name.** 13 Minnesota municipal pages are
   named after a county they are not in. Ramsey is in Anoka County, Faribault is
   in Rice County, Blue Earth is in Faribault County, Marshall is in Lyon County,
   and Cook is in St. Louis County. Always take the county from the page data.
3. **Watch for same-name places.** 211 of our Minnesota pages share a name with
   another place in the same county, almost always a city and a township. The
   bare slug is usually the city. The township's slug ends in `-township`, or in
   **`-city`**: `olmsted-county/rochester-city` and `stearns-county/albany-city`
   carry township offices, not city ones. Real city names also end in "City"
   (Lake City, Pine City). Decide city or township from the offices on the page,
   never from the slug.
4. **Call township board members supervisors**, whatever the position label says.
5. Never call a Minnesota place a village or borough, and never call a township
   a city.
6. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
7. Do not state which year a named city or school district votes in, a council
   or county board size, a mayoral term, or whether a city elects its clerk or
   treasurer. All of these vary by place.
8. Do not describe a primary, runoff or ranked count for a home rule charter
   city. Most Minnesota local races have no primary at all.
9. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
   contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.

## Data issues

Logged for the data team. Never write copy around them.

- `mn/anoka-county/ramsey-county` is an empty second page for the city of Ramsey,
  next to the real `mn/anoka-county/ramsey`. Its slug reads as Ramsey County.
- `mn/hennepin-county/st-anthony-stearns-county` carries city offices under
  Hennepin County with "Stearns County" in the slug, so it is unclear which
  place the page is for.
- 14 slugs ending in `-city` carry township offices (rule 3).
- Most township board seats are labeled "township trustee" rather than
  supervisor (rule 4).
