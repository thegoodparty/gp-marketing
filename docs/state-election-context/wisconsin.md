# Wisconsin: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Wisconsin so you do not have to research the same
ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which
offices are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race
data, which always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean. Every claim below has a verbatim quote
in [`research/wisconsin.json`](./research/wisconsin.json).

**The one thing to know first:** Wisconsin has two election seasons, and they
split by party label. The **spring election** in April is nonpartisan and covers
almost all local government: county board, county executive, cities, villages,
towns, school boards and judges. The **fall election** in November is partisan
and covers only the county "row offices" (sheriff, clerk, treasurer, register of
deeds, clerk of circuit court, district attorney, and where elected, surveyor
and coroner). So "Wisconsin local races are nonpartisan" is true for most
offices, and wrong for the sheriff.

## Legislative calendar

Next regular session: convenes 4 January 2027 (the first Monday of January in
odd-numbered years, Wis. Stat. § 13.02(1)), with regular business from 12
January 2027 (the first Tuesday after 8 January, § 13.02(2)) unless the
Legislature's own work schedule sets other dates. The Legislature meets
year-round: it meets every year, and bills not finished in the odd year carry
over to the even year (§ 13.02(4)). The governor can also call special sessions
(Wis. Const. art. IV, § 11).

The governor has 6 days, Sundays excepted, to act on a bill once it is
presented. A bill not returned in time becomes law, unless the Legislature's
final adjournment prevents its return, in which case it dies (Wis. Const.
art. V, § 10(3)). New laws take effect the day after publication unless the act
says otherwise (Wis. Stat. § 991.11).

## How elections work in Wisconsin

Safe on any Wisconsin page, at the tier named.

### Election days

**Spring election:** the first Tuesday in April, every year. It elects judges,
school boards, city, village and town officers, and the nonpartisan county
offices (county board supervisors and county executive). A nonpartisan **spring
primary** on the third Tuesday in February narrows the field where one is
needed.

*Wis. Stat. § 5.02(21), (22) · statutory · read in statute*

**General election:** the Tuesday after the first Monday in November, in
even-numbered years. Apart from state and federal offices, it elects district
attorneys and the county officers other than supervisors and county executives.
A partisan primary on the second Tuesday in August nominates party candidates
for it.

*Wis. Stat. § 5.02(5), (12s) · statutory · read in statute*

Which spring each local office is on:

- **County board:** two-year terms, elected in April of even-numbered years.
  A county board can choose staggered terms instead, so some counties elect
  half the board every April.
- **Towns and villages:** most town and village officers are elected in April
  of odd-numbered years. Village trustees serve two-year terms with half the
  board elected each year. Villages in Milwaukee County elect in even years
  instead.
- **Cities:** two-year terms by default, but a city can change this by charter
  ordinance and can stagger council seats into four-year terms. Some city seats
  may be up in any given April.
- **School boards:** elected at the spring election for three-year terms by
  default, so part of a board is up most years.

*Wis. Stat. §§ 59.10(1)(b), (3)(d); 60.30(1)(a); 61.19; 61.20(1); 62.09(5)(b)-(c); 120.06(1), (3) · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never say a particular office is up "this April" or "every two
years" unless race data gives the date or term. Say "at Wisconsin's spring
election in April".

### Primary type

**Spring (nonpartisan) primary:** no parties. For city office, a primary is
held only when the council calls one, a charter ordinance requires one, or
voters petition for one, and never when the candidates do not exceed twice the
number of seats. Villages and the county have similar rules.

*Wis. Stat. § 8.11 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Partisan primary (August):** open in the sense that voters do not register by
party. Each voter privately chooses one party's ballot at the polls and cannot
vote in more than one party's primary. Choosing a party's primary ballot does
not commit the voter to anything later.

*Wis. Stat. §§ 5.37(4), 5.62(1)(a) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not call Wisconsin's primaries closed, and do not say voters
"register with a party". Do not describe the February primary as a party
primary.

### Partisanship

**County board, county executive, city, village, town, school board and
judicial races are nonpartisan.** They are all elected at the spring election,
and the ballot carries no party designation. City ballots are nonpartisan by
statute, and no charter can change that. Town and village candidates are
nominated by caucus or nomination papers, with no party route at all.

*Wis. Stat. §§ 5.02(21), 5.60(1), 5.60(3)(am), 8.05 · statutory · read in statute*

**County row offices are partisan.** The sheriff, county clerk, treasurer,
register of deeds, clerk of circuit court, district attorney, and the surveyor
and coroner where those are elected, are nominated in the August party primary
and elected in November with a party label.

*Wis. Stat. §§ 5.02(5), 5.62(1)(a), 59.20(2) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** the county executive and the county board are nonpartisan, even
though the sheriff and clerk of the same county run with party labels. Do not
describe a Wisconsin county government as partisan or nonpartisan as a whole.

### Winning an election

Plurality wins everywhere: the candidate with the most votes is elected.
Wisconsin has no runoffs. Where a field is crowded, the February or August
primary narrows it, usually to twice the number of seats for nonpartisan
offices.

*Wis. Stat. §§ 5.01(3)(a), 8.11(1)(d), 120.06(2)(a) · statutory · read in statute*

### Running as an independent

For a **spring (nonpartisan) office**, there is no party route. Every candidate
files nomination papers, and goes through the spring primary if one is held.
In towns and villages, candidates are nominated either by a January caucus of
voters or by nomination papers, whichever the town or village uses.

*Wis. Stat. §§ 8.05, 8.10(1) · statutory · read in statute*

For a **partisan county office** in November, an independent files nomination
papers. A person who files as a party's candidate cannot also file as an
independent for the same office at the same election. Voting in the August
primary does not bar anyone from signing an independent's papers. The only
signing rule is one candidate per office: if someone signs for two candidates
for the same office, only the earlier signature counts. Since 2026, circulators
must be Wisconsin voters.

*Wis. Stat. §§ 8.04, 8.15(7), 8.20(1)-(3); 2025 Wis. Act 126 · statutory · read in statute*

## County government

Wisconsin has 72 counties, and every part of the state is in one. There are no
independent cities and no consolidated city-counties. Menominee County is a
special case: the whole county is a single town.

*Wis. Stat. §§ 2.01, 60.09(2) · statutory · read in statute*

Each county is governed by a **county board of supervisors**, elected from
districts. Board size has a ceiling set by population (at most 21 supervisors in
the smallest counties, up to 47 below 750,000 people). Supervisors serve
two-year terms, at the nonpartisan spring election.

*Wis. Stat. § 59.10(3)(a), (d) · usual case · read in statute*

**County executive.** Milwaukee County (750,000 or more people) must have an
elected county executive. Any other county may create one by board resolution
or by petition and referendum, or appoint a county administrator instead, or
have neither. The executive serves a four-year term and is elected at the
nonpartisan spring election.

*Wis. Const. art. IV, § 23a; Wis. Stat. §§ 59.17(1), 59.18(1) · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never say a Wisconsin county has, or lacks, an elected county
executive unless race data shows the office.

**Row offices**, elected at the partisan November election for four-year terms:
sheriff, county clerk, treasurer, register of deeds, clerk of circuit court,
and in counties that still elect them, a surveyor and a coroner. The district
attorney is also elected countywide in November, though state law treats it as
a state office.

*Wis. Const. art. VI, § 4; Wis. Stat. §§ 5.02(5), 5.02(23), 59.20(2) · statutory · read in statute*

**County home rule** is administrative only: a county can organize its own
offices, but only within laws of statewide concern that apply to every county.
Counties cannot change how or when county officers are elected.

*Wis. Stat. § 59.03(1) · statutory · read in statute*

**Below the county:** Wisconsin's **towns** are what other states call
townships. County land outside cities and villages is town territory (a county
with no towns counts as one town), and towns elect their own officers (see
below). A town is formally the "Town of" its name.

*Wis. Stat. §§ 60.01(1), 60.09(1) · statutory · read in statute*

## City, village and town government

Wisconsin has three kinds of municipality: **cities**, **villages** and
**towns**. All three elect nonpartisan officers at the spring election.

### Towns

Towns elect a **town board** of three supervisors, one of whom is designated on
the ballot as **town board chairperson**, in April of odd-numbered years. Most
also elect a town clerk and treasurer, unless the town has made those appointed
offices by referendum. A town board can grow to five members in some towns.

*Wis. Stat. §§ 60.21, 60.30(1)(a), (1e) · usual case · read in statute*

### Villages

Villages elect a **village president** and a **village board of trustees**.
The default is six trustees on two-year terms, three elected each year, with
only two trustees in villages of 350 or fewer people. A village may change the
number of trustees by ordinance.

*Wis. Stat. §§ 61.19, 61.20 · usual case · read in statute*

### Cities

Cities elect a **mayor** and a **common council** of alderpersons from
districts. Other city officers, such as the clerk and treasurer, may be
appointed or elected, depending on the city.

*Wis. Stat. § 62.09(1), (3) · usual case · read in statute*

Cities are divided into four classes by population (first class at 150,000 and
over, down to fourth class under 10,000). A city does not change class just by
growing: it passes into a new class only after a census, the needed changes to
its government, and a published mayoral proclamation.

*Wis. Stat. §§ 62.05, 990.001(15) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never infer a city's class from its population.

### Home rule

Cities and villages (not towns) have home rule under the constitution: they can
decide their own local affairs, subject to state laws of statewide concern that
apply uniformly. They exercise it by **charter ordinance**, which needs a
two-thirds vote of the council or board and can be put to a referendum.

*Wis. Const. art. XI, § 3(1); Wis. Stat. § 66.0101(2)(a) · statutory · read in statute*

**Does home rule reach elections?** Only where the election laws allow it. The
general election laws apply to all elections, so a city or village cannot make
its races partisan or move them off the spring election. But the statutes
themselves let a city, by charter ordinance, change the length of officers'
terms, cut the council to one alderperson per district, and require a primary
when three or more candidates file. So partisanship and election timing are
flat-assertable for every Wisconsin city and village, and terms and council
size are not.

*Wis. Stat. §§ 5.01(2), 5.60(3)(am), 8.11(1)(b), 62.09(1)(b), 62.09(5)(b) · statutory · read in statute*

## Metro and major-place exceptions

### Milwaukee County

`wi/milwaukee-county`

The statutes' 750,000-population county rules are written as the Milwaukee
County rules. It must have an
elected county executive, it elects a comptroller at the spring election, and
it elects no surveyor or coroner. Its villages elect their officers in April of
even-numbered years, not odd. Supervisors serve two-year terms.

*Wis. Stat. §§ 59.10(2), 59.17(1)(a), 59.20(2), 61.19 · statutory · read in statute*

### Milwaukee

`wi/milwaukee-county/milwaukee`

A first class city. Its school board (the board of school directors) has an
at-large seat on the city ballot and district seats on a separate ballot. All
races are nonpartisan, like every other Wisconsin city.

*Wis. Stat. § 5.60(3)(am), (4)(b) · statutory · read in statute*

### Menominee County

`wi/menominee-county`

The whole county is one town, the town of Menominee. The county board is made
up of the town board, plus a supervisor from any village.

*Wis. Stat. §§ 59.10(5), 60.09(2) · statutory · read in statute*

### Same-name towns and cities

`wi/brown-county/green-bay` and `wi/brown-county/green-bay-town`,
`wi/st-croix-county/hudson` and `wi/st-croix-county/hudson-town`

220 of our Wisconsin place names appear twice in the same county, almost always
as a city or village and a separate town of the same name. They are separate
governments with separate offices. A page whose slug ends in `-town` is the town.

### Big cities across county lines

Each of our pages files a city under one county, even where the city extends
into others. Do not write that a city lies wholly within the county on its
page.

## Writing rules

These are the ways Wisconsin copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never infer a county from a place name.** 7 of our Wisconsin municipal
   pages share a name with a different Wisconsin county: Washburn (Bayfield
   County), Portage (Columbia County), Juneau (Dodge County), Monroe (Green
   County), Marquette (Green Lake County), Jackson (Washington County) and
   Vernon (Waukesha County). The full list is in
   [`research/places-named-after-another-county.json`](./research/places-named-after-another-county.json).
   Always take the county from the page data.
2. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
3. **Do not confuse a town with the city or village of the same name.** A town
   is a separate government, usually next to or around the city. Say "the Town
   of Hudson", never just "Hudson", on a `-town` page.
4. **Do not call a village a city.** Our data files village offices under a
   "City" category, so the category and the slug do not tell you which it is.
   Use the office name from race data ("Village President", "Village Trustee").
5. **Do not call a town a township** in published copy. Wisconsin's own word is
   town. Our office labels say "township"; the copy should not.
6. **Do not call local races nonpartisan without naming the office.** The
   sheriff, clerk, treasurer, register of deeds, clerk of circuit court and
   district attorney run with party labels.
7. Do not call Wisconsin primaries closed, and do not say voters register by
   party.
8. Do not describe a runoff. Wisconsin has none.
9. Do not say a county has an elected county executive unless race data shows
   the office (Milwaukee County always does).
10. Do not infer a city's class from its population.
11. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
    contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.
