# Maine: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Maine so you do not have to research the same
ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which offices
are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race data, which
always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean. Every claim below has a verbatim quote
in [`research/maine.json`](./research/maine.json).

**The one thing to know first:** Maine is a state of towns. Most of our Maine
place pages are towns governed by an elected select board and an annual town
meeting, and each town sets its own election day, terms and board size. County
offices are partisan and elected in November of even-numbered years after a
June party primary, but counties do relatively little. State law uses
ranked-choice voting only for state and federal offices, but a city charter can
adopt it for local races, and Portland's does. And our school pages are filed at the state level, not under a county.

## Legislative calendar

The Legislature elected in November 2026 convenes its first regular session on
2 December 2026 (the first Wednesday of December after the general election,
Me. Const. art. IV, pt. 3, § 1), and works through the first half of 2027. It
must adjourn by 16 June 2027 (the third Wednesday in June), with short
extensions allowed (3 M.R.S. § 2). The second regular session convenes
5 January 2028 (the first Wednesday after the first Tuesday of January) and is
limited to budget, emergency and a few other kinds of bills (art. IV, pt. 3,
§ 1; 3 M.R.S. § 2). The Legislature may also be called back by its presiding
officers.

The governor has 10 days, Sundays excepted, to act on a bill. A bill the
governor holds when the Legislature adjourns becomes law unless it is returned
within three days after the same Legislature next meets, so a few first-session
bills are only settled in January 2028 (art. IV, pt. 3, § 2). Laws take effect
90 days after the session recesses unless passed as emergency measures by
two-thirds (art. IV, pt. 3, § 16).

## How elections work in Maine

Safe on any Maine page, at the tier named.

### Election days

**County offices** are elected at the November general election (the Tuesday
after the first Monday) in even-numbered years. County commissioners serve
four-year terms. Sheriffs, judges and registers of probate also serve four
years. District attorneys are elected every fourth year by prosecutorial
district, which covers more than one county.

*30-A M.R.S. §§ 61(2), 62(1), 251(1); Me. Const. art. VI, § 6, art. IX, § 10 · statutory · read in statute*

**Towns** elect their officials at the **annual town meeting**: by law at
least a moderator, the select board and the school committee, by ballot. A town
may add other elected officials. State law sets no common date. Each town picks
its own, so never give a Maine town's election date or month unless race data
shows it.

*30-A M.R.S. § 2525 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Cities** hold elections called under their charters, and their charters set
the timing.

*30-A M.R.S. §§ 2501, 2551 · varies by place · read in statute*

**School boards** are elected locally. A town not in a school district elects a
school committee at its annual meeting. School administrative district
directors are nominated like municipal officials, elected from each member town
for three years (four where the town votes every two years), on a board of at
least five that includes every member town.

*20-A M.R.S. §§ 2302, 1251, 1253 · usual case · read in statute*

### Primary type

The state primary is on the second Tuesday of June in even-numbered years.
Voters enrolled in a party vote in that party's primary. Unenrolled voters may
also vote in one party's primary without enrolling, but in only one.

*21-A M.R.S. §§ 339, 341 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not call Maine's primary closed. Do not call it open either:
enrolled voters still vote only in their own party's primary.

### Partisanship

**County races are partisan.** Candidates are party nominees from the June
primary or petition candidates, and a vacancy in a primary-nominated seat goes
to an appointee of the same party.

*21-A M.R.S. § 339; 30-A M.R.S. § 63 · statutory · read in statute*

**City races** carry a party label only if the city charter allows it. A
petition candidate may use a political designation only where the charter
permits.

*30-A M.R.S. § 2553 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Town races** are usually nominated by nomination papers, with no party
label. A town may instead accept nomination of its officials by party caucus,
but only by a separate vote of the town.

*30-A M.R.S. § 2528(1)(A) · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** "local races are nonpartisan" is wrong on every Maine county
page and not reliable for any one city. Avoid calling a city or town race
partisan or nonpartisan unless race data says which.

### Winning an election

**Ranked-choice voting** is used only in primaries for U.S. Senate, U.S. House,
governor and the Legislature, in federal general elections and in presidential
elections. Never say a county, city, town or school race in Maine is decided by
ranked-choice voting under state law.

*21-A M.R.S. § 1(27-C) · statutory · read in statute*

**A city charter can adopt it.** Portland's charter uses ranked-choice voting,
with an instant runoff, for mayor, city council and school board. Other cities
could do the same, so avoid saying a Maine city race does or does not use
ranked-choice voting unless race data or the city's charter shows it.

*Portland City Charter art. II, § 3 · varies by place · read in city charter*

Everywhere else the rule is **plurality**: county primaries and county general
elections, and city elections unless the charter says otherwise. Sheriffs and
probate officers are elected by plurality under the constitution.

*21-A M.R.S. § 723; 30-A M.R.S. § 2555; Me. Const. art. VI, § 6, art. IX, § 10 · statutory · read in statute*

**Towns are the exception.** Unless its charter says otherwise, a town of more
than 4,000 people elects by plurality, while a town of 4,000 or fewer needs a
majority. A town that votes by secret ballot uses plurality, with a recount and
a new vote only to break a tie.

*30-A M.R.S. §§ 2526(1), 2528(10) · varies by place · read in statute*

### Running as an independent

For **county office**, a candidate not running in a party primary files a
nomination petition. A candidate enrolled in a party must first withdraw that
enrollment by a deadline early in the election year. A petition candidate may
list a political designation of up to three words, but not the name of a party
that holds primaries. County candidates must live and vote in the district they
seek.

*21-A M.R.S. §§ 352, 353, 354(1) · statutory · read in statute*

## County government

Our data has a page for each of Maine's 16 counties. Each is governed by a
**board of county commissioners**: by default a chair and two others, each
elected by the voters of a commissioner district they live in, for four-year
terms. The commissioners choose their own chair every year. York County has
five commissioners.

*30-A M.R.S. §§ 61, 61-A, 62(1) · usual case · read in statute*

Counties also elect a **sheriff**, a **judge of probate** and a **register of
probate**, each for four years, and usually a **county treasurer**. 13 of our
16 county pages also carry a **register of deeds** race. Voters may switch to an appointed treasurer; our
Cumberland, Androscoggin and Knox county pages carry no treasurer race, which
fits this.

*Me. Const. art. VI, § 6, art. IX, § 10; 30-A M.R.S. §§ 151, 156 · usual case · read in statute*

**District attorneys** are elected by prosecutorial district, not by county.
Avoid describing a DA race as a county race.

*30-A M.R.S. § 251(1) · statutory · read in statute*

### County charters

A county may adopt a **charter** by vote of its voters. The charter may set up
its own legislative body, choose how officers are selected, and abolish the
offices of commissioner, treasurer or register of deeds by handing their duties
to others. The Maine law on county charters has no election reservation, so
on a county page take the offices from race data, not from the default above.

*30-A M.R.S. §§ 1301, 1351(1), 1352(2) · varies by place · read in statute*

### Townships and plantations

Maine "townships" are mostly **unorganized territory** with no local
government. An unorganized township of 200 or more people may organize as a
**plantation**, which elects assessors, a clerk, a treasurer and other officers
much like a small town, and covers a single township. In Maine law,
"municipality" includes plantations as well as cities and towns.

*30-A M.R.S. §§ 7001, 7002; 1 M.R.S. § 72(13) · statutory · read in statute*

## City and town government

Maine has **cities, towns and plantations**. No Maine law read for this doc
sorts places into these types by population. Never infer a Maine place's type
from its size; use the type on the page.

*1 M.R.S. § 72(13) · statutory · read in statute*

**Towns** are governed by the town meeting as legislative body and an elected
**select board** of three, five or seven members. Each town decides the board
size and the terms. Our labels call the select board "city legislature"; in
copy, say select board on a town page.

*30-A M.R.S. §§ 2525, 2526(4) · varies by place · read in statute*

**Cities** have a charter-defined council, often with a mayor. Eleven of our 22
city pages carry a mayor race. Take each city's offices and terms from race
data.

### Home rule

Every Maine municipality has **constitutional home rule**: its people may
amend its charter on any local matter not barred by the constitution or
general law. Voters can force a charter commission by petition. There is no
population threshold.

*Me. Const. art. VIII, pt. 2, § 1; 30-A M.R.S. §§ 2101, 2102 · statutory · read in statute*

**Does home rule reach elections?** Yes. State election law governs municipal
voting "except as otherwise provided" by a charter or Title 30-A, and charters
can change the winning rule, party labels and timing. So election rules on a
Maine city or town page vary by place.

*30-A M.R.S. §§ 2501, 2553, 2555 · varies by place · read in statute*

## Metro and major-place exceptions

### Portland and the larger cities

`me/cumberland-county/portland`, `me/androscoggin-county/lewiston`,
`me/penobscot-county/bangor`

Cities are governed by their charters. Portland's charter elects its mayor, city
council and school board by ranked-choice voting, with an instant runoff when no
one has a majority of first choices. Their school boards sit on separate pages
filed at the state level, such as `me/portland-2309930`, not under the city.

*Portland City Charter art. II, § 3 · statutory · read in city charter*

### School pages

Our 171 Maine district pages are almost all school units, filed directly under
the state: school administrative districts (`me/school-administrative-district-61`),
regional school units (`me/regional-school-unit-04`), community school districts,
and single-town school departments named after the town (`me/york`, which is
separate from the town page `me/york-county/york-town`). Never treat a school
page as a town page, and never assign it a county from its name.

### Tribal school pages

`me/indian-island`, `me/indian-township`, `me/pleasant-point`

These pages are the school units of tribal communities. Avoid describing a town
government on them.

## Writing rules

These are the ways Maine copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never infer a county from a place name.** No Maine place page shares a
   name with a different county, but school pages carry no county at all.
   Always take the county from the page data.
2. **Never decide from the slug alone whether a page is a town or a school
   unit.** `me/york` is a school department; `me/york-county/york-town` is the
   town. Decide from the offices on the page.
3. **Do not give a town's election date.** Each town picks its own annual
   meeting. County races are in November of even-numbered years.
4. **Do not say a Maine local race uses ranked-choice voting, or that it does
   not,** unless race data or the city charter shows it. State law uses it only
   for state and federal offices, but Portland's charter uses it for mayor,
   council and school board.
5. Do not call Maine primaries closed or open without explaining that
   unenrolled voters may choose one party's primary.
6. Do not call city or town races partisan or nonpartisan unless race data says
   which. County races are partisan.
7. Say select board, not city council, on a town page. Do not describe a
   default three-member county commission for York County or a charter county.
8. Do not describe a district attorney race as a county race.
9. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
10. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
    contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.
