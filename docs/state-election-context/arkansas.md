# Arkansas: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Arkansas so you do not have to research the same
ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which
offices are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race
data, which always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean.

**The one thing to know first:** Arkansas splits local races between two
election days and two kinds of ballot. County, township and city offices are
elected in **November of even-numbered years**. School boards and judges are
elected months earlier, on the **March primary date**, as **nonpartisan**
races. County offices run in party primaries. City races in most places do
not, but a city council can ask the parties to hold them. Majorities matter
almost everywhere: most local races go to a runoff when nobody gets more than
half the vote.

## Legislative calendar

The General Assembly meets every year: a regular session in odd-numbered years
and a fiscal session in even-numbered years. The fiscal session considers only
appropriation bills unless two-thirds of each house agree otherwise. The next
regular session convenes on 11 January 2027 (the second Monday in January of
each odd-numbered year, Ark. Const. art. 5, § 5). It lasts 60 days unless
two-thirds of each house extend it (Ark. Const. art. 5, § 17).

During the session the governor has five days, Sundays excepted, to act on a
bill. For bills still pending when the session adjourns, the governor has 20
days after adjournment to veto, or they become law (Ark. Const. art. 6, § 15).
An act without an emergency clause usually waits out the 90-day referendum
window after final adjournment before it takes effect (Ark. Const. art. 5,
§ 1).

## How elections work in Arkansas

The timing and runoff rules below hold on any Arkansas page. Partisanship
splits by type of government: read that section before writing anything about
party labels.

### Election days

**November of even-numbered years** (the Tuesday after the first Monday) is
the general election.

*A.C.A. § 7-5-102 · statutory · read in statute*

- **County officers** (county judge, sheriff, clerks, assessor, treasurer,
  coroner, surveyor, collector) are elected at each **governor's election**,
  every four years, for four-year terms.
- **Justices of the peace** (the quorum court) and **constables** are elected
  every two years, at the same time as the legislature.

*A.C.A. § 14-14-1302 · statutory · read in statute*

- **City and town officials** are elected at the November general election.
  Their statutory cycles all fall in even years, and the odd-year elections
  that mayor-council cities of 50,000 or more once held have been abolished.

*A.C.A. §§ 14-42-201(a), 14-43-303(c) · usual case · read in statute*

Within a city, offices run on different four-year cycles:

- Mayors of second-class cities and incorporated towns, and of first-class
  mayor-council cities under 50,000, are elected in the **governor's election
  years** (the cycles that began in 1966 and 1970).
- In mayor-council cities of 50,000 or more, the mayor and city clerk are
  elected in **presidential years** (the cycle that began in 1960).
- Council members in most cities and towns are elected every two years.

*A.C.A. §§ 14-43-303(a), 14-43-305(a), 14-44-105, 14-45-104 · usual case · read in statute*

**The March primary date** (the first Tuesday after the first Monday in March)
carries the party preferential primaries. It is also the **general election**
for school boards and for every nonpartisan judicial and prosecuting attorney
race.

*A.C.A. §§ 7-7-203(b), 7-10-102(b)(1), 6-14-111(a)(2) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** a school board race is decided in **March**, not November. Only
a school board runoff moves to the November ballot. Never describe a school
board race as being "on the November ballot" unless race data shows a runoff.

*A.C.A. § 6-14-121(a)(3) · statutory · read in statute*

**Can a place move its own election date?** No general local option was
found. State law fixes the municipal, county and school election dates above.
A city council can choose some things (party primaries, ward elections, four-year
council terms), but not the day. Let race data supply any special election.

*A.C.A. §§ 14-42-201, 14-14-1302, 6-14-102 · usual case · read in statute*

### School and special districts

**School boards** are the third tier, and they are nonpartisan by statute.
Members are elected at the preferential primary election. In odd years the
annual school election falls on the same March date and decides money
questions.

*A.C.A. §§ 7-10-102(a), 6-14-111(a)(2), 6-14-102(a)(1) · statutory · read in statute*

Boards have five or seven members, or nine in districts with at least 20,000
students. Members serve four- or six-year staggered terms. A district elects
at large, from single-member zones, or a mix.

*A.C.A. §§ 6-13-634(a), 6-13-608(a) · usual case · read in statute*

School board elections changed in 2025 (Act 503): the board election moved to
the March primary date and the office joined the nonpartisan list. Copy written
from older sources that says "school elections are held in May" or "in
November" is out of date.

Special districts (water, fire, improvement districts and similar) mostly have
appointed or landowner-elected boards and few pages in our data. Describe only
what race data shows.

### Primary type

Arkansas has an **open primary** with no party registration. The voter names
the party primary they want at the polls, and the poll worker marks that choice
next to their name on the precinct list.

*A.C.A. § 7-7-308(b) · statutory · read in statute*

A voter who votes in one party's preferential primary may not then vote in a
**different** party's runoff primary.

*A.C.A. § 7-1-103(a)(18)(B) · statutory · read in statute*

Nominees need a **majority**. If no one has one, the top two meet in a general
primary (the party runoff) four weeks later.

*A.C.A. §§ 7-7-102(a), 7-7-202(a), 7-7-203(a) · statutory · read in statute*

For our audience: an independent-minded voter does not have to take a party
ballot at all on the March date. The nonpartisan races (school board, judges,
prosecuting attorney) are on every party's ballot and on a separate nonpartisan
ballot, and voting them does not require choosing a party.

*A.C.A. § 7-10-102(b)(2), (b)(3) · statutory · read in statute*

### Partisanship

This splits by type of government:

**County and township: partisan.** County, township and district offices are
filled through party primaries (independents can still run by petition, see
below). This covers the county judge, justices of the peace, constables and
every row officer.

*A.C.A. § 7-7-102(a) · statutory · read in statute*

**School boards, judges and prosecuting attorneys: nonpartisan.** Always, by
statute and, for judges, by the constitution.

*A.C.A. § 7-10-102(a); Ark. Const. amend. 80, §§ 17, 18 · statutory · read in statute*

**City manager and city administrator cities: nonpartisan.** The ballots show
no party designation.

*A.C.A. §§ 14-47-109(d)(1), 14-48-110(d)(1) · statutory · read in statute*

**Mayor-council cities and towns: varies by place.** By default, candidates
qualify by petition as independents. But the city or town council may ask the
county parties to hold party primaries for city offices, and that request stays
in force until the council revokes it. We do not hold which councils have done
this.

*A.C.A. § 14-42-206(a)(1), (b)(1) · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never write "Arkansas local elections are nonpartisan" or
"Arkansas local elections are partisan". County races are partisan, school
board races are nonpartisan, and city races depend on the city. On a city page,
only race data can say whether party labels appear.

### Winning

Majorities, not pluralities, are the default:

- **County and mayor-council city races** (justice of the peace included): if
  there are more than two candidates and no one wins a majority, the top two
  go to a runoff four weeks after the general election.
- **City manager cities** are left out of that rule.

*A.C.A. § 7-5-106(a), (f)(2) · usual case · read in statute*

- **Nonpartisan races** (school board, judges, prosecuting attorney) need a
  majority on the March date. A runoff goes onto the November general
  election ballot.

*A.C.A. § 7-10-102(c); Ark. Const. amend. 80, §§ 17, 18 · statutory · read in statute*

- **City manager cities** usually elect directors by plurality with a 40
  percent floor, and may set a winning threshold below 50 percent for a
  directly elected mayor.

*A.C.A. § 14-61-111(b)(2) · varies by place · read in statute*

Ranked choice voting is banned for every local, state and federal office.

*A.C.A. § 7-1-116(b) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not write "the candidate with the most votes wins" on an
Arkansas county, school board or mayor-council city page. Write that a
majority is needed and that a runoff may follow.

### Getting on the ballot as an independent

**County, township and district offices:** an independent files during the
party filing period and qualifies by petition. Every signer must be a
registered voter in the area the office serves.

*A.C.A. § 7-7-103(a)(1), (b)(2) · statutory · read in statute*

Someone who filed for a party's nomination cannot then run as an independent
for the same office at that election.

*A.C.A. § 7-7-204(a)(2) · statutory · read in statute*

**Mayor-council cities and towns:** independent is the normal route. The
candidate files a nominating petition with the county clerk. Ward council
candidates may collect signatures only from voters of their ward. A person
defeated in a party primary cannot file as an independent for that office.

*A.C.A. § 14-42-206(b) · statutory · read in statute*

**School board and nonpartisan judicial races:** every candidate gets on the
ballot the same way, so "independent" is not a separate path.

## County government

Our data has **75** county pages for Arkansas, and every Arkansas city, town and
township page sits under one of them.

*Our page data (pages-by-state.csv, 27 October 2026)*

### County judge and quorum court

The **county judge** is the county's chief executive, not a courtroom judge.
The office is elected countywide for four years. The county judge presides
over the quorum court without a vote but with a veto, runs the county roads and
hires county staff.

*Ark. Const. art. 7, § 29; amend. 55, § 3 · statutory · read in statute*

The **quorum court** is the county's legislature. All county legislative power
is vested in it. It has **9 to 15 justices of the peace**, each elected from a
single-member district for a two-year term. The districts are redrawn after
each census.

*Ark. Const. amend. 55, §§ 1, 2(a); art. 7, § 38; A.C.A. §§ 14-14-502(b)(1), 14-14-1302(b) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** a justice of the peace in Arkansas is a **county legislator**,
not a magistrate. Never describe the office as a court or say it hears cases.
Our position pages call the office "County Justice of the Peace", which is
right. Do not "correct" it to "county commissioner".

### Other county offices

Every county elects a **sheriff** (ex officio tax collector unless a separate
collector exists), an **assessor**, a **coroner**, a **treasurer**, a
**surveyor** and a **circuit clerk**, all for four years. Counties over 15,000
people also elect a separate **county clerk**. Counties with a separate tax
collector elect one.

*Ark. Const. art. 7, §§ 19, 46 · usual case · read in statute*

The quorum court can create, merge or abolish elective county offices, but
only with voter approval at a general election. So the list above is the
default, not a guarantee. Let race data supply the offices.

*Ark. Const. amend. 55, § 2(b) · statutory · read in statute*

### No county charters

Counties govern through the quorum court under Amendment 55, which lets a
county exercise local legislative authority not denied by the constitution or
by law. This research found no county charter option. The legislature is also
barred from passing local or special acts, so it cannot give one county its
own form of government. Elections are not reserved at the county tier by any
charter provision, because there is none: county election rules come straight
from the Election Code.

*Ark. Const. amend. 55, § 1(a); amend. 14 · statutory · read in statute*

### Townships

Arkansas **townships are not governments**. They are districts the county
court draws inside each county, and the office elected from them is the
**constable** (two-year term). Justices of the peace are elected from quorum
court districts instead, which are separate from townships.

*A.C.A. §§ 14-14-401(b), 14-14-604(2), (3); Ark. Const. art. 7, § 47 · statutory · read in statute*

Our data has 511 township pages (slugs ending in `-township`), and they carry
constable races only.

*Our page data (sitemap shard 4, 27 October 2026)*

**Watch out:** never describe an Arkansas township as having a board, a
trustee, a supervisor or taxes, and never borrow township language from
Illinois, Ohio or Indiana. On a township page, the only thing to describe is
the constable race. Keep the word "Township" in the name, because 27 Arkansas
townships share a name and a county with a city or town (see Writing rules).

`ar/sebastian-county`

The constitution lets Sebastian County have two districts and two county
seats.

*Ark. Const. art. 13, § 5 · statutory · read in statute*

## City and town government

Arkansas has three classes of municipality: **cities of the first class**,
**cities of the second class** and **incorporated towns**.

*Ark. Const. art. 12, § 3; A.C.A. § 14-37-102 · statutory · read in statute*

| Class | Population | Default elected offices |
| --- | --- | --- |
| City of the first class | over 2,500 | mayor, council members by ward, city clerk (or clerk-treasurer), treasurer, city attorney |
| City of the second class | 500 to 2,499 | mayor, two council members per ward, recorder-treasurer (or recorder and treasurer) |
| Incorporated town | under 500 | mayor, five council members, recorder-treasurer |

*A.C.A. §§ 14-37-103(a), 14-43-315, 14-43-316, 14-44-103, 14-44-115, 14-45-102, 14-45-108 · usual case · read in statute*

### Class mostly follows population, but only upward

A town or city that a census shows has grown past a threshold moves up
automatically. A city that shrinks below its threshold moves down only if its
council asks. A city of 1,500 or more may also choose first-class status by
ordinance, and a town under 500 that voted to become a second-class city
stays one.

*A.C.A. §§ 14-37-103(b), 14-37-104(b), 14-37-111(a) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never infer a place's class from its population. A small
first-class city or a second-class city under 500 is lawful. Our pages do not
record class, so avoid naming it unless race data makes it obvious.

### Mayor-council (the default)

**Mayor**: four-year term in every class.

*A.C.A. §§ 14-43-305(a), 14-44-105, 14-45-104 · statutory · read in statute*

**Council**: first-class cities under 50,000 and second-class cities elect two
council members per ward, and towns elect five council members. All serve
two-year terms unless the voters approve four-year staggered terms.

*A.C.A. §§ 14-43-312(a)(1), 14-44-103(a), 14-45-102(a) · usual case · read in statute*

First-class council members must live in their ward but are elected citywide,
unless the council has adopted ward-only voting by ordinance.

*A.C.A. § 14-43-307(a)(1) · usual case · read in statute*

**Clerk, treasurer, recorder and city attorney**: elected for four years by
default, but a council can make several of them appointed by ordinance (the
city attorney and treasurer in first-class cities, and the treasurer or
recorder-treasurer in smaller places).

*A.C.A. §§ 14-43-315(a), 14-43-316(a)(1), 14-44-115(a), 14-45-108(a) · usual case · read in statute*

### City manager and city administrator forms

A city of 2,500 or more may adopt the **city manager** form. It is governed by
a **board of directors** that hires a manager. Under the 1989 enabling act the
city picks how directors are elected (at large, by ward, by district or a mix).
The board chooses the mayor from among its members unless the voters have
chosen to elect the mayor directly.

*A.C.A. §§ 14-47-101, 14-47-109(a)(1), 14-61-107, 14-61-111(a) · varies by place · read in statute*

A first-class city may instead adopt the **city administrator** form, with a
directly elected mayor and a seven-member board of directors elected at the
November general election.

*A.C.A. §§ 14-48-101(a), 14-48-110(c)(1) · usual case · read in statute*

Both forms are nonpartisan (see Partisanship).

**Watch out:** in these cities the governing body is a **board of directors**,
not a city council, and its members are **directors**. Our position pages label
every Arkansas city governing body "City Council", including Little Rock and
Fort Smith. Describe the office in neutral words ("the city's governing
board") unless race data names it. This is listed as a data issue below.

### Home rule and charters

Arkansas cities and towns have broad power over **municipal affairs**, and the
old rule that cities have only the powers expressly granted (Dillon's Rule)
does not apply to them. "Municipal affairs" excludes a list of state affairs,
and elections are not on that list. But city elections are still run under the
general election laws above, which set their dates, petitions and runoffs.

*A.C.A. §§ 14-43-601(a)(1), 14-43-602 · statutory · read in statute*

A first- or second-class city may also adopt a **charter** written by an
appointed commission and approved by its voters. An approved charter overrides
conflicting state laws on the city's organization and government.

*A.C.A. §§ 14-42-302(a)(1), 14-42-305 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** we do not hold which cities have charters, and a charter could
change the offices or terms described above. On a city page, describe the
offices race data shows rather than a class template.

### Mayor-council cities of 50,000 or more

Their mayor and city clerk are elected in presidential years, while the city
attorney, treasurer and half the council are elected two years later. Do not
assume a big city's mayor is on the same ballot as the governor.

*A.C.A. § 14-43-303(a) · statutory · read in statute*

`ar/garland-county/hot-springs-township`. The city page is about city offices.
The township page is about the constable race. Never merge them.

## Data issues

For the data team, not for copy:

1. **"City Council" label on boards of directors.** Every Arkansas city
   governing body is labelled "City Council", including cities with the city
   manager or city administrator form, whose governing body is a board of
   directors under state law.
2. **"County District Court Judge" carries the county court description.** On
   62 county pages the `county-court-judge` position is titled "County District
   Court Judge" but its description is the constitution's description of the
   county court, which is the county judge's court. District judges are state
   judges elected nonpartisan in March. The county judge is a separate partisan
   office (`county-executive-head`).
3. **Fixed: sitemap listed seven constable URLs without the county.** The
   Atkins, Hector, London, Pea Ridge, Pocahontas, Pottsville and Russellville
   constable pages were listed without their county and only worked through a
   redirect. PR #517 fixed this, and as of 2 November 2026 the live sitemap lists
   each with its county (for example
   `ar/pope-county/atkins/position/county-constable`).

## Writing rules

These are the ways Arkansas copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never say Arkansas local elections are nonpartisan, or that they are
   partisan.** County and township races are partisan. School board, judicial
   and prosecuting attorney races are nonpartisan. City manager and
   administrator cities are nonpartisan. Mayor-council cities vary.
2. **Put school board races in March.** They are decided on the preferential
   primary date. Only a runoff moves to November.
3. **Avoid "most votes wins".** County, mayor-council city and nonpartisan
   races need a majority, and a runoff may follow.
4. **The county judge is not a judge in a courtroom**, and a justice of the
   peace is a member of the quorum court, not a magistrate.
5. **Never infer a county from a place name.** 14 Arkansas municipal pages are
   named after a county they are not in, for example Van Buren (Crawford
   County), Benton (Saline County), Conway (Faulkner County), Marion
   (Crittenden County) and Searcy (White County). Always take the county from
   the page data.
6. **Keep "Township" in township names, and describe only the constable race
   there.** 27 Arkansas townships share a name and a county with a city or
   town (El Dorado, Hot Springs, Lonoke, Dardanelle, Brinkley and others).
   Arkansas townships have no board, trustee or tax.
7. **Do not call a city's governing body a "city council" unless race data
   says so.** City manager and city administrator cities have a board of
   directors.
8. Avoid stating seat counts or term lengths as fixed for a city. Voters can
   adopt four-year council terms, councils can make clerks, treasurers and
   attorneys appointed, and charters can change more. Let race data win.
9. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
10. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
    contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.

### What search results commonly get wrong

- School board elections are often described as held in May or with the
  November election. Since 2025 (Act 503) they are held on the March primary
  date.
- Arkansas is sometimes called a closed-primary state. It has no party
  registration: voters pick a party ballot at the polls, and that choice is
  only recorded on the precinct list.
- "County judge" is often read as a judicial office and "justice of the peace"
  as a magistrate. Both are county government offices.
- Arkansas townships are sometimes described like Midwestern townships with
  trustees. They are only election districts for constables.
