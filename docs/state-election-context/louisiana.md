# Louisiana: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Louisiana so you do not have to research the same
ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which offices
are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race data, which
always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean. Every claim below has a verbatim quote
in [`research/louisiana.json`](./research/louisiana.json).

**The one thing to know first:** Louisiana has parishes, not counties, and runs
every local race through one open primary. All candidates for a local office,
whatever their party, run on one ballot and every voter can vote in it. A
candidate who wins a majority is elected outright; otherwise the top two meet in
a later "general election", which works as a runoff. Every candidate's party, or
"No Party", is printed next to their name, including school board and town races,
so no Louisiana race is nonpartisan on the ballot. Since 2026 a closed party
primary applies only to Congress, the state Supreme Court, the state board of
education (BESE) and the Public Service Commission. Elections are held on
Saturdays, except the November congressional-year primary.

## Legislative calendar

Next regular session: convenes 12 April 2027 (noon on the second Monday in April
in odd-numbered years, La. Const. art. III, § 2(A)(4)(a)). Odd-year sessions last
at most 45 legislative days and must end by the 60th calendar day, so by
11 June 2027. They are mainly fiscal, but each member may also prefile up to five
other bills, so election and local government laws can still change. Even-year
sessions are general and convene the second Monday in March. The governor can
call an extraordinary session (La. Const. art. III, § 2(A)(3)-(4), (B)).

A bill delivered to the governor after the session has ended becomes law unless
vetoed within twenty days of delivery (La. Const. art. III, § 18(A)), so the last
bills are settled about three weeks after adjournment. New laws from a regular
session take effect on 1 August unless the bill sets another date (La. Const.
art. III, § 19).

## How elections work in Louisiana

Safe on any Louisiana page, at the tier named.

### The open primary

For every office except a party primary office, all voters may vote in the
primary whatever their party affiliation, and all candidates run in it whatever
theirs. The general election is open the same way.

*La. R.S. 18:401(B)(1), (C) · statutory · read in statute*

A candidate who wins a majority of the votes in the primary is elected and there
is no general election for that seat. Otherwise the top two (twice the number of
seats still to fill) go on to the general election, even if both are from the
same party. A candidate with no opponent is declared elected and does not appear
on the ballot.

*La. R.S. 18:511(A), (B), 18:481, 18:482 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** in Louisiana the "general election" for a local office is the
second round, not the main event. Never write that the primary only narrows the
field or picks a party's nominee: it often decides the race.

### Closed party primaries (federal and some state offices only)

Since 2026, the party primary offices are U.S. Senate and House, the Louisiana
Supreme Court, BESE and the Public Service Commission. No local office is a
party primary office.

*La. R.S. 18:2(9) · statutory · read in statute*

For these offices, each major party holds its own primary on the spring election
dates, and a voter may vote only for candidates of their own party. A voter not
affiliated with a party may pick one major party's ballot, and must stay with
that party for its second primary. A primary winner needs a majority, or the top
two of that party go to a second party primary. Candidates who are not affiliated
with a major party skip the primary and reach the general election by petition,
which only voters not affiliated with a major party may sign.

*La. R.S. 18:410.3, 18:410.7(A)-(B), 18:410.8, 18:410.10 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** "Louisiana ended its jungle primary" is wrong for every page we
have. Governor, legislature, parish, city and school board races still use the
open primary. Never describe a local race as a party primary.

### Election days

Louisiana fixes its election dates in a four-year cycle of rules, and most of
them are Saturdays.

- **Spring (every year):** the primary for municipal and ward officers is the
  third Saturday in April, or the last Saturday in March in presidential years.
  The general is the sixth Saturday after.
- **Fall of the governor's year (odd years):** the primary is the third to last
  Saturday in October, for the governor and officers elected with the governor.
  The general is six Saturdays later.
- **Fall of congressional years (even years):** the primary is the November
  federal election day, the Tuesday after the first Monday. The general is the
  sixth Saturday after it, in December.
- **Fall of other odd years:** only a parish containing a municipality of
  300,000 or more holds a fall election for parish and city offices, on the
  second Saturday in October. That is Orleans Parish.

*La. R.S. 18:402(B)-(D) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** on a school board, justice of the peace or constable page, the
November ballot in an even year is the first round, and a December runoff may
follow. Do not call November the general election there.

### Which offices vote when

**Parish offices:** police jurors are elected at the state general election for
four-year terms, and take office on the second Monday in January after it. Each
parish also elects a sheriff, clerk of court, assessor and coroner for four-year
terms. Home rule parishes set their council's terms in their charter.

*La. R.S. 33:1221(A), 33:1226(A); La. Const. art. V, §§ 27-29, art. VII, § 24(A) · usual case · read in statute*

**Municipalities:** officers under the Lawrason Act (most towns and villages)
serve four-year terms and take office on 1 July. Municipal elections are held in
the spring, but which year depends on the municipality. A municipality may also,
by an irrevocable plan, move its elections to the fall congressional or
gubernatorial elections, so some municipalities will vote in the fall from 2031.
Take the election date from race data.

*La. R.S. 33:383(A); 18:402(B)(1), (C)(1), (D)(1) · varies by place · read in statute*

**School boards:** parish school board members are elected at the congressional
elections, in November of even years with a December runoff if needed, for
four-year terms beginning 1 January. Some boards have planned a move to the
fall gubernatorial election, so they will change cycle from 2031. Take the
election date from race data.

*La. R.S. 17:52(A) · usual case · read in statute*

**Justices of the peace and constables** are elected by ward at the
congressional election for six-year terms. Each justice of the peace court has
one constable. City court marshals are also elected at the congressional election
for six years, except Baton Rouge's city constable, who is elected under the
city's charter and plan of government.

*La. R.S. 13:2582(B), 13:2583(A)-(B), 13:1879 · statutory · read in statute*

Our Louisiana data has only one special district race (an airport district
board). Do not describe special district elections in general terms.

### Can a place change its own election rules?

No, except where the Election Code allows it. State law bars parishes and
municipalities from adopting any ordinance or resolution about elections unless
the Election Code specifically authorizes it. The municipal and school board
"plan" options above are such authorizations.

*La. R.S. 18:1(B) · statutory · read in statute*

### Party labels

**Every candidate in every race carries a party label on the ballot.** A
candidate registered with a recognized party has that party printed next to
their name; a candidate registered with an unrecognized party is shown as
"Other"; a candidate with no affiliation is shown as "No Party". This applies to
primary and general ballots for parish, municipal, school board and judicial
offices alike, and no charter can change it.

*La. R.S. 18:551(D)(1), 18:463(A)(1)(a), 18:1(B) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** this is a label, not a nomination. Parties do not choose
candidates for local office, and two candidates of the same party can meet in
the general election. Do not call a Louisiana local race nonpartisan, and do not call it a
party contest either. "Every candidate runs on one ballot, with their party
listed" is the safe phrasing.

### Running as an independent

There is no separate independent route for local office. Any candidate qualifies
for the open primary by filing a notice of candidacy with either the qualifying
fee or a nominating petition, and appears as "No Party" if not affiliated. Voters
who sign a petition must be registered to vote on that office and cannot withdraw
their signature. A person under an order of imprisonment for a felony conviction
cannot qualify.

*La. R.S. 18:461(A), 18:465(B) · statutory · read in statute*

No party may register under the name "Independent", so the ballot word is "No
Party", not "Independent".

*La. R.S. 18:441(B)(4) · statutory · read in statute*

## Parish government

Louisiana's counties are called **parishes**. Our data has 64 parish pages, all
with slugs ending in `-parish`. Parishes and their boundaries are fixed by the
constitution.

*La. Const. art. VI, § 1(A) · statutory · read in statute*

### Police juries

A parish without a home rule charter is governed by a **police jury**. It sets
its own size by ordinance, between five and fifteen members (as few as three in a
parish of 10,000 people or fewer), elected from wards or districts for four-year
terms. The jurors elect a president from among themselves; there is no elected
parish executive.

*La. R.S. 33:1221(A), 33:1224, 33:1226(A) · usual case · read in statute*

### Home rule charter parishes

Any parish may adopt a **home rule charter** by a majority vote of its residents,
whatever its size. The charter sets the government's structure, so its governing
body is usually a **parish council** and many have an elected **parish
president**. The legislature cannot change the structure of a charter
government. Take the body's name, and whether the parish elects a president,
from race data.

*La. Const. art. VI, §§ 5(A), (C), (E), 6, 44(1) · varies by place · read in statute*

Charters and plans of government that existed in 1974 remain in force, and two
or more governments in one parish may adopt one joint charter. That is how the
consolidated city-parish governments exist.

*La. Const. art. VI, §§ 4, 5(D) · statutory · read in statute*

### Elected parish officers

Every parish elects a **sheriff, clerk of court, assessor and coroner**, each for
four years. No charter may change these offices or the school board in a way
that conflicts with state law. The constitution's sheriff section does not apply
to Orleans Parish, and Orleans elects its assessor with the New Orleans city
officers.

*La. Const. art. V, §§ 27-29; art. VI, § 5(G); art. VII, § 24 · statutory · read in statute*

**Does home rule reach elections?** No. The home rule article has no election
clause of its own, but the Election Code bars every parish and municipality from
making its own election rules unless the code allows it. Timing, the open
primary and party labels are flat-assertable for every parish.

*La. Const. art. VI, § 5; La. R.S. 18:1(B) · statutory · read in statute*

### Wards, not townships

Below the parish, the units are wards, not townships. Police jurors,
justices of the peace and constables are elected by ward or district. Justice of
the peace and constable races sit on our parish pages.

*La. R.S. 33:1224, 13:2582-2583 · statutory · read in statute*

## City, town and village government

Louisiana municipalities come in three classes, by population: **cities** (5,000
or more), **towns** (1,001 to 4,999) and **villages** (1,000 or fewer).

*La. R.S. 33:341; La. Const. art. VI, § 44(3) · statutory · read in statute*

**Class does not change automatically.** A municipality changes class only when
its board asks and the governor issues a proclamation, and a village that grows
by fewer than 200 people past 1,000 may choose to stay a village. Never infer a
place's class from its population: use the word in its name.

*La. R.S. 33:342 · statutory · read in statute*

### What municipalities elect

The default is the **Lawrason Act**, which governs every
municipality without a home rule charter or a pre-1974 special legislative
charter. A Lawrason municipality elects:

- a **mayor**, at large;
- a **board of aldermen**: three in a village (at large), five in a town, five to
  nine in a city, from districts, at large or both. A board may call itself a
  village, town or city council and its members council members;
- a **chief of police**, at large, in some places called the **marshal**.

All serve four-year terms.

*La. R.S. 33:321, 33:343, 33:381(A)-(B), 33:382, 33:383(A)(1) · usual case · read in statute*

Some municipalities appoint their chief of police instead, under laws written for
them by name or a vote of the town's residents. Never write that every Louisiana
town elects a police chief. Take it from race data.

*La. R.S. 33:381(B)-(C), 33:381.2 · varies by place · read in statute*

Municipalities with a home rule charter or a special
legislative charter set their own structure, often a mayor and city council. The
Lawrason Act fills only the gaps in a special legislative charter.

*La. R.S. 33:321, 33:481; La. Const. art. VI, §§ 2, 5 · varies by place · read in statute*

### Home rule

Any municipality may adopt a home rule charter by vote of its residents. There
is no population threshold. As with parishes, the charter sets the structure but
not the election rules: timing, the open primary and party labels stay state
law.

*La. Const. art. VI, §§ 5, 44(1); La. R.S. 33:1395(A), 18:1(B) · statutory · read in statute*

## Metro and major-place exceptions

Four places in our data have a consolidated city-parish government. On each, the
**parish page** carries the shared government (a mayor-president or mayor and a
council), and the **city page** has no mayor race, only city court offices or a
city council. Never describe a separate mayor on these city pages.

### New Orleans and Orleans Parish

`la/orleans-parish`, `la/orleans-parish/new-orleans-city`

The city and parish are one government: the New Orleans city council is the
parish governing authority. The mayor and council races sit on our Orleans
Parish page, and the city page (slug `new-orleans-city`) has only a traffic
court race. Orleans Parish is the one parish with a fall election in odd years
without a governor's race, and its assessor is elected with the city officers.
The constitution's sheriff section does not apply to Orleans.

*La. R.S. 18:2(7), 18:402(D)(2)(a); La. Const. art. V, § 27, art. VII, § 24(B) · statutory · read in statute*

### Baton Rouge and East Baton Rouge Parish

`la/east-baton-rouge-parish`, `la/east-baton-rouge-parish/baton-rouge`

Baton Rouge and the parish share a plan of government with a Metropolitan
Council, which is what our parish page lists. Baton Rouge elects a city
constable under its charter rather than the general city court law. Baker,
Central and Zachary are separate cities with their own mayors and their own
school districts (`baker-city-school-district`,
`central-community-school-district`, `zachary-community-school-district`). The
East Baton Rouge Parish School Board has its own membership law.

*La. R.S. 13:1879(B)(1), 17:58; La. Const. art. VI, § 4 · statutory · read in statute*

### Lafayette and Lafayette Parish

`la/lafayette-parish`, `la/lafayette-parish/lafayette`

Lafayette and its parish run under a unified city-parish plan kept in force as a
local act. Our parish page lists a parish council and the city page a city
council and city marshal, with no mayor. Take both from race data. Broussard,
Carencro, Duson, Scott and Youngsville are separate municipalities.

*La. R.S. 33:1391 (note) · varies by place · read in statute*

### Houma and Terrebonne Parish

`la/terrebonne-parish`, `la/terrebonne-parish/houma`

Terrebonne Parish's plan of government, also kept as a local act, governs the
parish and Houma together. Our Houma page has only city court races; the council
and parish president sit on the parish page.

*La. R.S. 33:1392 (note) · varies by place · read in statute*

### City school districts

`la/monroe-city-school-district` and the three East Baton Rouge cities above.
Monroe City and Ouachita Parish have separate school boards with separate
voters: Monroe City residents vote only for the Monroe City board. A
municipality holding over half a parish's people is represented on the parish
school board in proportion to its population.

*La. Const. art. VIII, § 10(A)-(B); La. R.S. 17:57 · statutory · read in statute*

### Cities across parish lines

Each of our pages files a municipality under one parish. Do not write that a
place lies wholly within the parish on its page.

## Writing rules

These are the ways Louisiana copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Say parish, never county.** Louisiana's equivalent of a county is the
   parish, and every one of our parish slugs ends in `-parish`.
2. **Never infer a parish from a place name.** Three of our Louisiana place pages
   share a name with a different parish: the town of Jackson is in East
   Feliciana Parish, Washington is in St. Landry Parish and Franklin is in
   St. Mary Parish. The full list is in
   [`research/places-named-after-another-county.json`](./research/places-named-after-another-county.json).
   Always take the parish from the page data.
3. **Do not call a local race nonpartisan or a party primary.** Every candidate
   runs on one ballot with their party, "Other" or "No Party" printed by their
   name, and parties do not nominate for local office.
4. **Do not call the November election "the general election" for a school
   board, justice of the peace or constable race.** In even years, November is
   the first round and December the runoff.
5. **Do not say Louisiana has closed primaries** without naming the four kinds of
   office they cover. Never say the jungle primary has ended.
6. **Say "No Party", not "independent"** when describing how an unaffiliated
   candidate appears on the ballot.
7. **Do not describe a separate mayor** for New Orleans, Baton Rouge, Lafayette
   or Houma on the city page. Their mayor-level office sits on the parish page.
8. **Do not infer a municipality's class from its population.** Use the word in
   its name (city, town, village).
9. Do not say every parish has a police jury, or every town elects a police
   chief. Take both from race data.
10. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
11. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
    contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.
