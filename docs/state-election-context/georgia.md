# Georgia: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Georgia so you do not have to research the same
ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which
offices are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race
data, which always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean. Every claim below has a verbatim quote
in [`research/georgia.json`](./research/georgia.json).

**The one thing to know first:** Georgia splits local races three ways. County
offices are partisan, with a party primary in May and the general election in
November of even-numbered years. Judges, and any office a local act has made
nonpartisan (often school boards and consolidated city-county governments), are
elected on the May primary ballot with no party label. Cities vote in November of
odd-numbered years under their own charters. And almost every Georgia race needs
a **majority** to win, so runoffs are common.

## Legislative calendar

Next regular session: convenes 11 January 2027 (the second Monday in January of
each year, Ga. Const. art. III, § IV, ¶ I). The session may last no more than 40
legislative days, counted in the aggregate, so it has no fixed end date. The
governor has six days to act on a bill during the session, and 40 days after the
session adjourns for good; a bill not vetoed in that time becomes law (Ga. Const.
art. III, § V, ¶ XIII). General acts signed from January to June take effect on
1 July unless they say otherwise (O.C.G.A. § 1-3-4(a)). **Local acts**, which
create cities and decide most local election rules, take effect as soon as the governor signs them (§ 1-3-4(b)).

## How elections work in Georgia

Safe on any Georgia page, at the tier named.

### Election days

**County offices** are elected at the November election, held on the Tuesday
after the first Monday in November of even-numbered years. Each office is
elected in the November election before its term runs out.

*O.C.G.A. §§ 21-2-2(15), 21-2-9(a) · statutory · read in statute*

**The general primary** is held on the Tuesday of the 24th week before the
November election, in even-numbered years. That puts it in May.

*O.C.G.A. § 21-2-150 · statutory · read in statute*

**Nonpartisan offices** (judges, and the local offices described under
Partisanship) are elected on the same day as the general primary, on a separate
nonpartisan part of the ballot. There is no earlier primary for them.

*O.C.G.A. §§ 21-2-9(b), 21-2-138, 21-2-139(a) · statutory · read in statute*

**Cities** hold their general elections on the Tuesday after the first Monday in
November of **odd-numbered years**. A local act of the General Assembly can move
a city to even-year November elections, give it two-year terms, or stagger its
terms. City offices default to four-year terms.

*O.C.G.A. §§ 21-2-9(c), 21-2-541.1, 21-2-541.2 · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not say a city votes in an odd year, or an even year, unless
race data gives the date. Say "in the city election".

**School boards.** Every school system is run by an elected board of education,
"elected as provided by law", which in Georgia usually means a local act for that
school system. Whether a board is elected on the May nonpartisan ballot or as a
partisan office depends on that local act.

*Ga. Const. art. VIII, § V, ¶ II; O.C.G.A. §§ 21-2-9(b), 21-2-139(a) · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not state when a school board is elected, or whether it is
partisan, unless race data shows the date and whether candidates carry a party.

**Can a place move its own election date?** Not by itself. A county's home rule
power does not reach elected county offices or the election of its governing
authority, and a city's home rule power does not reach how its council is
elected. Those changes need a local act of the General Assembly, or something
Georgia's Election Code itself lets a charter decide.

*Ga. Const. art. IX, § II, ¶ I(c)(1)-(2); O.C.G.A. § 36-35-6(a)(1) · statutory · read in statute*

### Primary type

Georgia has no party registration. At a primary, a voter picks one party's
ballot, or a ballot with only the nonpartisan races. A voter who votes in one
party's primary cannot vote in another party's primary for the same election, or
in another party's runoff.

*O.C.G.A. §§ 21-2-224(d), 21-2-152(c), 21-2-285.1 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not call Georgia's primary simply "open" or "closed". Say
voters choose a party's ballot, or the nonpartisan ballot, at the polls.

### Partisanship, by type of local government

**County offices: partisan.** Parties nominate in the May primary, and the
general election is in November. This covers the county commission, sheriff,
clerk of superior court, tax commissioner, coroner and other elected county
offices, unless a local act has made one nonpartisan.

*O.C.G.A. §§ 21-2-9(a), 21-2-151(a) · usual case · read in statute*

**Judges: nonpartisan.** Superior court judges always run on the nonpartisan
ballot. So do state court, probate and magistrate judges wherever the law
provides nonpartisan elections for that county office. A nonpartisan judicial
candidate cannot be nominated by a party or run as an independent.

*O.C.G.A. § 21-2-138 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Probate judges, school boards and consolidated governments: nonpartisan only
if a local act says so.** The General Assembly may make any of these offices
nonpartisan by local act. Nonpartisan elections for a consolidated government
count as county elections, so they are held in May of even years, not in the
city election.

*O.C.G.A. § 21-2-139(a) · varies by place · read in statute*

**Pending: probate judges.** A 2025 law takes probate judges out of the list
above, contingent on a constitutional amendment on the November 2026 ballot that
would authorize nonpartisan probate judge elections. Re-check after the vote.

*O.C.G.A. § 21-2-139, note to Ga. L. 2025, p. 780 · statutory · read in statute*

**Coming in 2028: some counties go nonpartisan.** From 1 January 2028, in any
county that has abolished its elected coroner, every elected county office except
the sheriff becomes nonpartisan, elected in May with no primary. It does not
apply to a county governing authority set up by a local constitutional amendment.
The law does not name the counties.

*O.C.G.A. § 21-2-140 (Ga. L. 2026, p. 956) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** until 2028, do not describe any county office as nonpartisan
because of this law. After that, do not apply it to a county unless race data
shows its county offices without party labels.

**Cities: set by charter.** A party may hold a municipal primary, a city may
bar party primaries by charter or ordinance, and a city may call its own
nonpartisan primary. Nonpartisan city elections run on the dates in the charter.

*O.C.G.A. §§ 21-2-139(b)-(c), 21-2-157 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not call a city's races partisan or nonpartisan unless race
data shows whether candidates carry a party.

### Winning an election

**Majority, with runoffs.** A candidate needs a majority of the votes cast to win
a primary or an election. If nobody gets one, the top two meet in a runoff 28
days later. The runoff is a continuation of the first election: only voters
entitled to vote in the first round may vote in it.

*O.C.G.A. § 21-2-501(a)(1); Ga. Const. art. II, § II, ¶ II · statutory · read in statute*

**Cities can choose plurality,** if their charter or ordinances say a plurality
wins. That is not allowed in cities of more than 100,000 people, which must
require a majority.

*O.C.G.A. § 21-2-501(b), (e) · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never write that the candidate with the most votes wins in
Georgia. Say a candidate needs a majority, or a runoff decides it, unless race
data shows a plurality city.

### Running as an independent

For county and other non-city offices, an independent gets on the ballot by
nomination petition and is listed in the independent column. Signers must be
registered voters entitled to vote for that office, and nobody may sign the same
petition twice. The law does not bar people who voted in a primary from signing.

*O.C.G.A. § 21-2-170(a), (c), (g) · statutory · read in statute*

**City offices are different.** A city candidate can be nominated by petition
only if the city's charter or an ordinance allows it. In a nonpartisan city
election, every candidate files with the city, so "independent" has no meaning
there.

*O.C.G.A. §§ 21-2-170(a), (h), 21-2-132(c)(3) · statutory · read in statute*

## County government

Georgia has **159 counties**, the most the constitution allows, and our data has a
page for each one. Each county's government is set by local acts of the General
Assembly, so it varies more from county to county than in most states.

*Ga. Const. art. IX, § I, ¶¶ I-II · statutory · read in statute*

**The governing authority** is one of three things: a **board of
commissioners**, a **sole commissioner** (one elected person who is the whole
county government), or the governing body of a **consolidated government**. Board
size, districts, terms and whether the chair is elected separately are all set by
the county's own local act.

*O.C.G.A. § 1-3-3(7); Ga. Const. art. IX, § I, ¶ I · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never assume a county has a board. Take the governing body from
race data. A sole commissioner county has one commissioner race and no board.

**Elected county officers.** Every county elects a **clerk of the superior
court**, **judge of the probate court** and **sheriff**, and either a **tax
commissioner** or a separate tax receiver and tax collector, each for four
years. **Coroners** are elected the same way, also for four years, except where
the office has been abolished.

*Ga. Const. art. IX, § I, ¶ III(a); O.C.G.A. § 45-16-1(a) · statutory · read in statute*

**No county charters, and home rule stops at elections.** A county's home rule
power cannot touch any elected county office or the composition, form or
election of the county governing authority. Those are changed only by general
law or a local act.

*Ga. Const. art. IX, § II, ¶ I(c) · statutory · read in statute*

## City government

### One kind of municipality

In Georgia law, "city", "town", "municipality" and "village" all mean the same
thing: an incorporated municipality. There are no classes. Each one is governed
by its own charter, which is a local act of the General Assembly.

*O.C.G.A. § 36-30-1 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** a place called a "town" in its name is legally the same as a city.
Never imply a town has fewer powers or a different form of government.

**New cities are created only by local act** of the General Assembly (or a method
set by general law), and need at least 200 residents. The same goes for
dissolving or merging a city.

*O.C.G.A. §§ 36-35-2(a), 36-31-3 · statutory · read in statute*

**What a city elects** is set by its charter, usually a mayor and a council. Terms default to four years unless a
local act sets two.

*O.C.G.A. §§ 21-2-541.1, 21-2-541.2 · varies by place · read in statute*

### Home rule

A city may amend its own charter by ordinance, but its home rule does not reach
the composition and form of its governing body or how its members are elected.
Those stay with local acts, except for what the Election Code itself lets a
charter or ordinance decide: barring party primaries, allowing petition
nominations, and choosing plurality wins.

*O.C.G.A. §§ 36-35-3(b), 36-35-6(a)(1), 21-2-139(c), 21-2-170(h), 21-2-501(b) · statutory · read in statute*

## School and special districts

School board elections are covered in "How elections work" above. The
constitution keeps Georgia's existing county school systems and its existing
**independent (city) school systems**, each with its own board, and forbids new
independent systems. Our data has 176 school district pages.

*Ga. Const. art. VIII, § V, ¶¶ I-II · statutory · read in statute*

Soil and water conservation district supervisors appear on many of our county
pages. The Election Code's definition of "public office" leaves them out.

*O.C.G.A. § 21-2-2(30) · statutory · read in statute*

## Metro and major-place exceptions

### Consolidated city-county governments

A city and its county can merge into one government only if voters in the county
and in the city each approve. A county with no city inside it can also declare
itself a consolidated government. Every consolidation is set up by its own local
act, so each one is different.

*Ga. Const. art. IX, § III, ¶ II(a); O.C.G.A. § 36-68-4 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Athens-Clarke County** `ga/clarke-county`. A unified government. Our data puts
the mayor on `ga/clarke-county/athens-clarke-county-unified-government` and the
commission on the county page. Winterville, inside Clarke County, has its own
page with its own mayor and council races (`ga/clarke-county/winterville`).

*Athens-Clarke County Unified Government, About Us · varies by place · local government publication*

**Columbus** `ga/muscogee-county/columbus`. A consolidated government. Our data
puts the mayor and council on the Columbus page and the constitutional county
officers on `ga/muscogee-county`.

*Columbus Consolidated Government home page · varies by place · local government publication*

**Augusta** `ga/richmond-county` and **Macon** `ga/bibb-county`. Our data has no
separate city page with a mayor for either. The mayor is filed as the county
executive and the commission as the county board, on the county page. Hephzibah
and Blythe (Richmond County) keep their own pages and city races. Do not call
Augusta or Macon consolidated governments in copy until that is confirmed in
their charters; describe the offices on the page instead.

**Watch out:** on a consolidated government's page, do not describe a separate
city council and county commission. Do not call its races partisan or
nonpartisan unless race data shows party labels. If a local act made them
nonpartisan they are on the May ballot.

### Atlanta

`ga/fulton-county/atlanta`

Our data files Atlanta under Fulton County only. Copy must not say Atlanta lies
wholly within Fulton County. Follow the general city rules above and take the
election date and any party label from race data.

### Big counties and cities across county lines

Each of our pages files a city under one county, even where it extends into
others. Do not write that a city lies wholly within the county on its page.

## Writing rules

These are the ways Georgia copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never infer a county from a place name.** 24 of our Georgia municipal pages
   share a name with a different Georgia county. Decatur is in DeKalb County,
   Jackson is in Butts County, Calhoun is in Gordon County, Forsyth is in Monroe
   County, Clayton is in Rabun County, Monroe is in Walton County and Lumpkin is in
   Stewart County. The full list is in
   [`research/places-named-after-another-county.json`](./research/places-named-after-another-county.json).
   Always take the county from the page data.
2. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
3. **Never say the most votes wins.** Georgia requires a majority and holds a
   runoff 28 days later, unless race data shows a plurality city.
4. **Do not call local races nonpartisan, or partisan, as a group.** County offices
   are partisan, judges are nonpartisan, and school boards, consolidated
   governments and cities depend on local law.
5. **Never assume a county has a board of commissioners.** Some have a single
   sole commissioner, and some are consolidated with a city.
6. Do not call Georgia's primary simply open or closed.
7. Do not state whether a city votes in an odd or even year unless race data gives
   the date.
8. **A "town" is a city** in Georgia law. Do not imply a different form of
   government. Slugs ending in `-city` (`peachtree-city`, `union-city`,
   `garden-city`) are part of the place's name, not its type.
9. Do not describe the 2028 nonpartisan change for counties without a coroner, or
   the pending probate judge change, as already in effect.
10. Do not describe a consolidated government as a separate city and county.
11. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
    contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.
