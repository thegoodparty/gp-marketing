# South Carolina: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across South Carolina so you do not have to research the same
ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which offices
are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race data, which
always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean. Every claim below has a verbatim quote
in [`research/south-carolina.json`](./research/south-carolina.json).

**The one thing to know first:** South Carolina runs two calendars. County offices
are partisan and on the November ballot in even-numbered years, after a June party
primary that needs a majority and can go to a runoff. Cities and towns vote on
their own dates, mostly nonpartisan, and a 2026 law moves almost all of them to
odd-numbered years from 1 January 2027. The state constitution bans laws for a
single county or city, so county and municipal rules are the same statewide,
but school boards are set up district by district by local act.

## How elections work in South Carolina

Safe on any South Carolina page, at the tier named.

### Election days

**County offices** are elected at the general election on the first Tuesday after
the first Monday in November of even-numbered years. County council members serve
two- or four-year terms as the General Assembly set for each county. Sheriffs,
coroners, clerks of court and the remaining elected county supervisors and
superintendents of education are elected every four years, counting from 1960;
probate judges every four years counting from 1958.

*S.C. Code §§ 7-13-10, 7-13-20, 7-13-30, 4-9-90 · statutory · read in statute*

**Cities and towns: the 2027 change.** From 1 January 2027 every municipality must
hold its general elections in odd-numbered years, on the first Tuesday after the
first Monday in either April or November. A municipality that already votes in
November of odd years must stay there; one that does not choose defaults to
November of odd years. The exception: a municipality whose last elections were in
April or November of an even-numbered year may keep that even-year date. Terms
running now are lengthened or shortened so they end at the new dates.

*S.C. Code §§ 5-15-45, 5-15-50 (both effective 1 January 2027) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** before 2027 each council set its own election date by ordinance,
so dates were all over the calendar. Never state a city's election date or year
unless race data gives it, and never describe the old ordinance rule as current.

*S.C. Code § 5-15-50 (effective until 1 January 2027) · statutory · read in statute*

Mayors and council members serve two- or four-year terms. Four-year terms are
staggered so no more than half the council and mayor are elected at once, unless
an ordinance says otherwise. Two-year terms are not staggered.

*S.C. Code § 5-15-40 · varies by place · read in statute*

**School boards and special purpose districts.** The default in general law is a
three-member board of trustees appointed for three-year terms, "unless it be
specially provided otherwise by law", and many districts have their own local
acts deciding whether the board is elected, when, and with or without party
nomination. Where parties nominate, school board
and special district candidates are in the state-run June primary. Some public
service districts elect commissioners at the November general election.

*S.C. Code §§ 59-19-20, 7-13-15(B)(2), 7-11-71 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Can a place move its own election date?** Counties cannot: their dates are in
state law. From 2027 a city or town may only choose between April and November of
odd years (or keep an existing even-year date).

*S.C. Code §§ 7-13-10, 5-15-50 · statutory · read in statute*

### Primary type

No one may vote in more than one party's primary on the same day. A party may
nominate by primary or by convention, and anyone may qualify by petition instead.
Party primaries are held on the second Tuesday in June of general election years.

*S.C. Code §§ 7-11-10(A), 7-13-40, 7-13-1040 · statutory · read in statute*

**Majority and runoffs.** A first primary needs a majority. If no one has one, a
second primary between the top two is held two weeks later, and its leader is
nominated with or without a majority.

*S.C. Code §§ 7-17-600, 7-13-50 · statutory · read in statute*

A candidate who loses a party primary or convention may not then appear on the
general election ballot for that office.

*S.C. Code § 7-11-10(A) · statutory · read in statute*

### Partisanship

- **County races are partisan.** County council, sheriff, probate judge, coroner,
  clerk of court and other county offices are nominated in party primaries, and
  the November ballot groups candidates under their parties, with separate columns
  for petition candidates.
- **City and town races are usually nonpartisan.** Each municipality adopts one of
  three nonpartisan methods by ordinance. A municipality that does not adopt one
  may use party nominations and must pay for its own primary. From 2027 party
  nominations are allowed only in municipalities under 39,000 people; larger
  places that used them must switch to a nonpartisan method by 1 April 2027.
- **School boards and special districts** depend on each district's local act.

*S.C. Code §§ 7-13-15, 7-13-330, 5-15-60 (both versions), 59-19-20 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never call a city race partisan or nonpartisan, or a school board
race either, unless race data shows it (candidates' party labels are the tell).

### Winning an election

The majority rule above applies in party primaries. Municipalities choose one of
three methods for their nonpartisan elections:

1. **Nonpartisan plurality:** most votes wins.
2. **Nonpartisan election and runoff:** a majority is needed; if no one gets one,
   a runoff between the top two is held two weeks later.
3. **Nonpartisan primary and general election.**

*S.C. Code §§ 5-15-60(A), 5-15-61, 5-15-62 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not say a city race "may go to a runoff" unless race data shows
the city uses the runoff method.

### Running as a petition candidate

A candidate who is not a party nominee gets on the ballot by a nominating petition
signed by qualified registered electors of the office's area, filed with the
county board of voter registration and elections for county and district offices,
or with the municipal clerk for city and town offices. Someone who lost the party
primary for the office cannot then file as a petition candidate.

*S.C. Code §§ 7-11-10(A), 7-11-70 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** South Carolina ballots call these "petition" candidates. Say
"petition candidate" rather than "independent" when describing ballot access.

### Who runs town elections (from 2027)

From 1 January 2027 municipalities under 10,000 people no longer have their own
election commissions; the county board of voter registration and elections runs
their elections.

*S.C. Code § 5-15-175 (effective 1 January 2027) · statutory · read in statute*

## County government

Our data has a page for each of South Carolina's 46 counties; the constitution
allows no more than 46. There is county home rule, but on uniform terms: the
General Assembly may not pass a law for one county, and every county uses one of
five forms set by general law. Home rule cannot set aside general law on election
and suffrage qualifications.

*S.C. Const. art. VIII, §§ 3, 7, 14; S.C. Code § 4-9-25 · statutory · read in statute*

The governing body is a **county council**. The five forms are council,
council-supervisor, council-administrator, council-manager and board of
commissioners. Councils usually have 3 to 12 members elected from single-member
districts. The council picks its own chair unless the chair is a separate elected
office; our data shows a separately elected chair in Aiken, Clarendon, Horry,
Kershaw, Saluda and Spartanburg counties. In council-supervisor counties the
supervisor is elected at large, chairs the council and votes only to break ties;
our data has that race in Berkeley, Chester, Union and Williamsburg counties.

*S.C. Code §§ 4-9-20, 4-9-90, 4-9-110, 4-9-410, 4-9-610 · varies by place · read in statute*

Every county elects a **sheriff**, a **coroner** and a **clerk of court**, each for
four years, and a **probate judge**. The **solicitor** (the local prosecutor) is
elected by judicial circuit, not by county. Our data also shows an elected auditor
and treasurer in most counties and a register of deeds in six; those vary by
county.

*S.C. Const. art. V, § 24; S.C. Code §§ 7-13-20, 7-13-30 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** say "solicitor", never "district attorney", and do not write that
a county elects one: solicitors are elected by circuit.

### Townships

South Carolina has no township governments in our data. The law still lets the
General Assembly create a "township" that counts as a municipality, but our data
has no such pages.

*S.C. Code § 5-1-20(1) · statutory · read in statute*

## City and town government

A municipality is a city or town incorporated by certificate. "City" and "town"
are names, not classes with different powers, and the constitution bans laws for
a single municipality. Each picks one of three forms by ordinance: mayor-council,
council, or council-manager.

*S.C. Const. art. VIII, §§ 9, 10; S.C. Code §§ 5-1-20, 5-5-10 · statutory · read in statute*

Every municipality elects a **mayor**, always at large, and a **council**. The
council decides by ordinance whether members are elected at large, by ward, or a
mix. Council or voters by petition can call a referendum to change the council's
size or how it is elected.

*S.C. Code §§ 5-15-20, 5-15-30 · varies by place · read in statute*

Our data has 240 municipal pages; almost all have a mayor and council race. Eleven
have an elected commissioner of public works.

**Watch out:** do not infer size or powers from "city" or "town" in a name.

### School districts

Our data has 76 school district pages. Many counties have several districts (for
example numbered districts in Lexington, Richland and Spartanburg counties). Each
board's size, terms, election date and partisanship come from its own local act;
take them from race data.

### Cities across county lines

Each of our pages files a city under one county, even where it extends into
others. Do not write that a city lies wholly within the county on its page.

## Writing rules

1. **Never infer a county from a place name.** Our one South Carolina collision:
   Kershaw is in Lancaster County, not Kershaw County. Always take the county from
   the page data.
2. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
3. **County races are partisan; city and school races depend on the place.** Do not
   call a city or school board race partisan or nonpartisan unless race data shows it.
4. **Do not state a city's election date or year** unless race data gives it. The
   2027 law moves most to odd years, in April or November.
5. Say **solicitor**, not district attorney, and remember it is elected by circuit.
6. Say **petition candidate** when describing how a non-party candidate gets on
   the ballot.
7. A primary runoff is part of a party primary, not of the general election.
8. Do not infer size or powers from "city" or "town".
9. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
   contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.
