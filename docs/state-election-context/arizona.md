# Arizona: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Arizona so you do not have to research the same
ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which
offices are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race
data, which always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean. Every claim below has a verbatim quote
in [`research/arizona.json`](./research/arizona.json).

**The one thing to know first:** Arizona puts nearly every local candidate race
in even-numbered years, on two dates: a first election (the primary) on the
second to last Tuesday in July, and the general election in November. County
races are partisan. School board races are nonpartisan by law, and city and town
ballots may not show a party. Tucson still holds city elections in odd years, so
take city election dates from race data. Two-thirds of our Arizona location
pages are school districts.

## Legislative calendar

Next regular session: convenes 11 January 2027 (noon on the second Monday of
January, every year, A.R.S. 41-1101(A)). Arizona sets no fixed session length;
the session runs until both houses adjourn. The governor has five days (Sundays
excepted) to act on a bill during the session, and ten days (Sundays excepted)
after final adjournment, or the bill becomes law (Ariz. Const. art. V, § 7).

New laws usually take effect ninety days after the session closes, unless they
are emergency measures (Ariz. Const. art. IV, pt. 1, § 1(3)).

Re-check this doc: August 2027, or a month after the session's final adjournment
if it runs later. Last researched: October 2026.

## How elections work in Arizona

Safe on any Arizona page, at the tier named.

### Election days

**State and county** primaries are on the second to last Tuesday in July, and the
general election is the first Tuesday after the first Monday in November of
even-numbered years. County officers (sheriff, county attorney, recorder,
treasurer, assessor, school superintendent and supervisors) serve four-year
terms, and supervisors are elected in presidential election years.

*A.R.S. 16-201, 16-211, 11-211(A); Ariz. Const. art. XII, § 3 · statutory · read in statute*

**Cities, towns, school districts and community college districts**: since 2014,
state law allows candidate elections for these only in even-numbered years, on
the same two dates. Where a place holds two rounds, the first is on the July
date, whatever the place calls it, and the second in November.

*A.R.S. 16-204(E), (E)(1), (H) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** the even-year rule is not the whole story for charter cities.
After a court ruling in a case brought by Tucson, the legislature instead
requires a place to move to the statewide dates only after its off-cycle
elections show a significant drop in turnout, and terms are lengthened when it
moves. Our Tucson page shows city races in November of an odd year. Never state a
city's election year from the statute. Take it from race data.

*A.R.S. 16-204.01(A), (B), 16-204.02(A) · varies by place · read in statute*

**School boards** are elected at the November general election, usually for
four-year terms. A school district board has three members unless enlarged, and
a union high school district board has five.

*A.R.S. 15-424(A), (B), 15-421(A), (B)(2) · usual case · read in statute*

**Fire districts** have boards of three or five members, depending on size.

*A.R.S. 48-803(A) · usual case · read in statute*

### Primary type

Arizona's party primaries are semi-open. A voter registered with a recognized
party gets only that party's ballot. A voter registered as independent, with no
party preference, or with an unrecognized party chooses the ballot of one
recognized party. Nominees are those with the most votes.

*A.R.S. 16-467(B), 16-645(A) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not call Arizona primaries open or closed. Party members cannot
cross over. Independents pick one party's ballot.

### Partisanship

**County races are partisan.** Supervisors are nominated at the party primary
and run on the partisan November ballot.

*A.R.S. 11-213(A) · statutory · read in statute*

**School board races are nonpartisan.** Candidates appear without any party or
other designation, in the nonpartisan section of the ballot.

*A.R.S. 15-422(B), 16-502 · statutory · read in statute*

**City and town races are nonpartisan in state law**, which bars any sign on the
ballot of a candidate's party. The legislature's own findings cite a court
ruling in a case brought by Tucson over charter city elections, so avoid stating
as law that a charter city's races are nonpartisan. Defer to race data.

*A.R.S. 9-821.01(B), 16-204.01(A) · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** "local races are nonpartisan" is wrong on every Arizona county
page. Name the tier.

### Winning an election

The candidate with the most votes wins, and ties are decided by lot. A city may
provide by ordinance that a mayor or council candidate who wins a majority at the
July election is elected outright, with no November race. Otherwise the top
finishers go on to November. Council members elected by district are voted on
only by that district's voters.

*A.R.S. 16-647, 15-426(B), 16-649(A), 9-821.01(C), (D) · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not say a city race "goes to a runoff" or "was decided in the
primary" without race data. Each city sets this.

### Running as an independent

A voter who is not registered with a recognized party may be nominated by
petition instead of the primary. The petition may be signed only by voters who
have not signed a primary petition for the same office. A candidate who failed to
make the primary ballot for lack of valid signatures cannot switch to an
independent petition.

*A.R.S. 16-341(A)-(C) · statutory · read in statute*

For school board, city and town office there is no party nomination: every
candidate files nomination papers and a petition for the nonpartisan ballot.

*A.R.S. 16-311(B), 15-422(A) · statutory · read in statute*

## County government

Arizona has 15 counties, and our data has a page for each.

Each county is governed by a **board of supervisors**. Counties of 175,000 or
more have five supervisors. Smaller counties have three, unless their voters
choose to change. Each supervisor is nominated and elected only by the voters of
their own supervisorial district, for a four-year term.

*A.R.S. 11-211(A), 11-212, 11-213(A); Ariz. Const. art. XII, § 3 · usual case · read in statute*

Counties also elect a **sheriff, county attorney, recorder, treasurer, assessor
and school superintendent**, each for four years. The treasurer is also the tax
collector. Our pages also show clerk of the superior court, justice of the peace
and constable races. Take those from race data.

*Ariz. Const. art. XII, § 3; A.R.S. 11-401 · statutory · read in statute*

### Charters

A county of more than 500,000 people may elect a charter committee and adopt a
charter. Avoid describing any Arizona county as a charter county. Take county
offices from race data.

*Ariz. Const. art. XII, § 5 · varies by place · read in statute*

### Townships

No township government appears in the Arizona county or municipal law read for
this doc, and our data has no township pages. Below the county, the elected
units are cities and towns, school districts and special districts.

## City and town government

Arizona has cities and towns. A community of 1,500 or more may incorporate as
either.

*A.R.S. 9-101(A) · statutory · read in statute*

**What a town elects by default:** a council of five or seven members, depending
on population at incorporation, serving two-year terms. The council chooses the
mayor from among its members. Voters may switch to a district system, with
council members elected by district and a mayor elected directly.

*A.R.S. 9-231(B), 9-232(B), (C), 9-232.04 · usual case · read in statute*

**Type does not follow population automatically.** A town of 3,000 or more
becomes a city only if its council proposes it and its voters approve. Never
infer whether an Arizona place is a city or a town from its population.

*A.R.S. 9-271(A) · statutory · read in statute*

### Charter cities

A city of more than 3,500 may adopt a charter. The charter must be consistent
with state law, but it prevails over conflicting state laws about cities. A
charter sets the city's own offices, terms and whether the mayor is elected
directly.

*Ariz. Const. art. XIII, § 2; A.R.S. 9-284(A) · varies by place · read in statute*

**Does home rule reach elections?** Partly. The state sets the election dates
and the nonpartisan ballot by statute, and the legislature declares both matters
of statewide concern. Its findings also respond to a court ruling for Tucson on
election dates. Treat each charter city's dates, offices and party labels as
unknown until race data shows them.

*A.R.S. 16-204(A), 16-204.01(A), 9-821.01(A) · varies by place · read in statute*

### Tucson

`az/pima-county/tucson`

Still holds city elections in odd-numbered years, as our page shows. Do not

race data.

*A.R.S. 16-204.01(A) · varies by place · read in statute*

### Cities across county lines

Some Arizona cities extend into more than one county. Our pages file each city
under one county, so do not write that a city lies wholly within the county on
its page.

## Writing rules

These are the ways Arizona copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never infer a county from a place name.** Of the 3 Arizona place pages that
   share a name with an Arizona county, 2 sit in a different county: the town of
   Pima is in Graham County, and the city of Maricopa is in Pinal County. The
   full list is in
   [`research/places-named-after-another-county.json`](./research/places-named-a
fter-another-county.json).
   Always take the county from the page data.
2. **Name the tier before saying partisan or nonpartisan.** County races are
   partisan. School board races are not. City races follow state law and, for
   charter cities, race data.
3. **Do not state a city's election year from the even-year rule.** Some charter
   cities still vote in odd years.
4. Call the July election the primary only for county and state races. A city
   may call its July election something else and may elect a candidate outright
   there.
5. Do not call Arizona primaries open or closed.
6. Do not infer whether a place is a city or a town, or a charter city, from its
   population.
7. Supervisors are elected by district voters only. Do not say the whole county
   votes for every supervisor.
8. Do not call any Arizona place a township or village.
9. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
10. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
    contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.

### What search results commonly get wrong

- That Arizona primaries are in August. Since 2026 the primary is on the second
  to last Tuesday in July.
- That Arizona primaries are open to everyone. Party members are limited to
  their own party's ballot.
- That all Arizona city elections are in even years. Some charter cities still
  vote in odd years.
