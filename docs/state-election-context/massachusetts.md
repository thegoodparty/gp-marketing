# Massachusetts: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Massachusetts so you do not have to research the same
ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which
offices are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race
data, which always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean. Every claim below has a verbatim quote
in [`research/massachusetts.json`](./research/massachusetts.json).

**The one thing to know first:** in Massachusetts, local government means the
**city or town**, and they elect almost everything local. Counties barely exist as governments: seven of the 14
have no county government at all, and the county offices that survive (sheriff,
register of deeds, register of probate, clerk of courts) are partisan offices
elected at the November state election. City and town races, by contrast,
usually carry no party label. And the word "town" in a place's name does not
tell you its form of government: some places officially called towns are run
by a mayor or a council.

## Legislative calendar

year on the first Wednesday in January, Mass. Const. amend. art. LXIV, § 3; an
earlier switch to biennial sessions was annulled by amend. art. LXXV). The
General Court meets year-round, and the governor can also call it into session.

The governor has 10 days to act on a bill once it is laid before them. A bill
not returned in time becomes law, unless the General Court adjourns within those
10 days and so prevents its return, in which case it dies (Mass. Const. pt. 2,
c. 1, § 1, art. II and amend. art. I, both as amended by amend. art. XC). New
laws take effect 90 days after they become law, unless declared emergency laws,
which take effect at once (Mass. Const. amend. art. XLVIII, The Referendum, pt.
I; M.G.L. c. 4, § 1).

## How elections work in Massachusetts

Safe on any Massachusetts page, at the tier named.

### Election days

**State election:** the Tuesday after the first Monday in November, in
even-numbered years. Apart from state and federal offices, it elects every
county office (sheriff, register of deeds, register of probate, clerk of courts,
and county commissioners and treasurer where a county still has them), district
attorneys, and regional school committee members elected district-wide.

*M.G.L. c. 50, § 1; c. 54, § 62 · statutory · read in statute*

**State primary:** the seventh Tuesday before the state election. A city or town
primary, where one is held, is on the 28th day before the local election.

*M.G.L. c. 53, § 28 · statutory · read in statute*

**Town elections:** each town elects its officers at its **annual town meeting**,
which by default falls between February and June. A town can hold the ballot
election separately from the business meeting, as long as both stay in that
February-to-June window. A special law or the town's charter can set another
time.

*M.G.L. c. 39, §§ 9, 9A · usual case · read in statute*

**City elections:** the date is set by each city's charter or special law. The
standard charter plans in state law hold city elections in odd-numbered years
(Plan E cities on the Tuesday after the first Monday in November), and a city
that votes every year or in even years votes on the third Tuesday of December.
In practice the date varies by city.

*M.G.L. c. 43, §§ 15, 109; c. 54, § 103A · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never say a Massachusetts city or town votes "every November" or
"every spring". Say "at the [city/town]'s local election" and take the date
from race data. Town elections are usually in spring; city elections are
usually in the fall; both have exceptions.

### Primary type

**State primary:** semi-closed. A voter enrolled in a party may receive only
that party's ballot. An **unenrolled** voter (Massachusetts's word for a voter
with no party) chooses any party's ballot at the polls and **stays unenrolled**
afterwards. Voters registered in a "political designation" that is not a
qualified party count as unenrolled for this purpose. Enrollment changes made in
the 20 days before a primary do not count for it.

*M.G.L. c. 53, §§ 37, 38 · statutory · read in statute*

**Local primaries and preliminaries:** cities under the standard charter plans
hold no party primary for local office. Many hold a nonpartisan **preliminary
election** instead, by default on the fourth Tuesday before the city election,
which narrows each race to two candidates per seat. The mayor and council (or
the select board) can fix its day.

*M.G.L. c. 43, §§ 16, 44A, 44F; c. 54, § 103P · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not call Massachusetts primaries open or closed without
qualification, and never say an unenrolled voter "joins" a party by voting in
its primary. Use "unenrolled", not "independent", for a voter with no party.

### Partisanship

Massachusetts splits by **type of office**, not by place.

**County offices and district attorneys are partisan.** Sheriff, register of
deeds, register of probate, clerk of courts, county commissioner, county
treasurer and district attorney are nominated at the state party primary and
appear on the November ballot with a party or political designation (or
"Unenrolled").

*M.G.L. c. 53, § 48; c. 54, § 41 · statutory · read in statute*

**City offices usually carry no party label.** A city ballot shows party
designations only where the city election follows a party primary. Under the
standard charter plans, no city primary or caucus is held, and Plan A and Plan E
ballots are barred from showing any party mark. Only the rarely used **Plan F**
nominates mayor and council in party primaries.

*M.G.L. c. 43, §§ 1, 16, 49, 112; c. 54, § 41 · usual case · read in statute*

**Town offices usually carry no party label, but the law still allows one.** A
town ballot shows a political designation only where the town election follows
a party primary or party caucus. Where a town has not adopted municipal
primaries, a party that wants to nominate for town office does so by caucus. A
town can also opt for a nonparty town caucus, which shuts out party caucuses.
Nomination papers for city and town office never need a party designation.

*M.G.L. c. 53, §§ 8, 71, 121; c. 54, § 41 · varies by place · read in statute*

**School committees:** city and town school committees follow the city or town
ballot. Regional school committee members elected district-wide are chosen at
the November state election with **no** political designation on the ballot.

*M.G.L. c. 54, §§ 42, 162 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** "Massachusetts local elections are nonpartisan" is wrong for the
sheriff, register of deeds and the other county offices, and not guaranteed for
towns. Name the office: "City council races here carry no party label" is
usually safe; "town races are nonpartisan by law" is not.

### Winning an election

Plurality wins: the candidate with the most votes is elected, and in a
multi-seat race the top vote-getters fill the seats. Massachusetts has no runoffs.
Where a city holds a preliminary election, the top two per seat go on to the
final ballot, and no preliminary is held when no more than twice the number of
seats file.

*M.G.L. c. 43, §§ 44F, 44G; c. 50, § 2 · statutory · read in statute*

**Exception:** Plan E elects the city council and school committee at large by
**proportional representation**: voters rank candidates with numbers. Cambridge
elects this way (see below). A place's own charter or special law can set
another method, so never describe the counting method for a city or
town unless race data or that place's charter says so.

*M.G.L. c. 43, §§ 1, 112 · varies by place · read in statute*

### Running as an independent

For **county offices and district attorney**, a candidate not running as a
party nominee files nomination papers and appears as "Unenrolled" or with a
political designation of up to three words that is not a party's name. An
unenrolled candidate must obtain a certificate that they are not enrolled in
any party, and must not have been enrolled in a party after the cutoff that
applies to party candidates.

*M.G.L. c. 53, §§ 6, 8; c. 54, § 41 · statutory · read in statute*

For **city and town offices**, every candidate files nomination papers unless a
charter or special law sets another route, and most local ballots carry no
party label, so there is no separate independent route to describe.

*M.G.L. c. 53, § 6; c. 43, § 16 · usual case · read in statute*

**Who may sign:** registered voters of the city or town and of the district for
the office, each signing in person as registered. Only their signatures count.

*M.G.L. c. 53, § 7 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not use "Independent" as a party name in Massachusetts copy.
The state's term for a candidate or voter with no party is "unenrolled", and
"Independent" cannot be used to form a political designation.

*M.G.L. c. 50, § 1 · statutory · read in statute*

## County government

Massachusetts has 14 counties, and our data has one county page for each. **Seven
have no county government:**

- **Middlesex, Hampden, Worcester, Hampshire, Essex and Berkshire** county
  governments were abolished by state law, and their functions moved to the
  Commonwealth. **Franklin** County's government was also abolished.
- **Suffolk** has no county commissioners: the Boston, Chelsea, Revere and
  Winthrop governments perform their duties.

Abolishing a county government did not change its boundaries, so these counties
still exist as geography, court districts and election districts.

*M.G.L. c. 34, § 4; c. 34B, §§ 1, 2, 12 · statutory · read in statute*

**What every county still elects** (at the partisan November election, in
abolished counties too):

- **Sheriff**, every six years. In abolished counties the sheriff became a
  state employee but stayed an elected office.
- **Register of deeds**, every six years, by registry district. Some counties
  are split into more than one registry district, each with its own register.
- **Register of probate**, every six years.
- **Clerk of courts**, every six years. Suffolk elects several court clerks.
- **District attorney**, every four years, by district.

*M.G.L. c. 34B, §§ 10, 12; c. 54, §§ 154-157, 159 · statutory · read in statute*

**Counties that still have a county government** (Barnstable, Bristol, Dukes,
Norfolk, Plymouth and Nantucket) differ:

- **County commissioners:** by default three, elected at the state election for
  four-year terms, no two from the same city or town. Bristol, Norfolk and
  Plymouth follow this pattern in our data.
- **County treasurer:** elected every six years, except in Suffolk and
  Nantucket. The office expired in the abolished counties.
- **Nantucket:** the town and county are the same place, and the town's select
  board acts as the county commissioners.
- **Barnstable:** governed under its own county charter, not the general county
  charter law. Our race data shows an elected Barnstable County Assembly of
  Delegates seat on each Cape Cod town page, alongside the county's commissioners.
- **Dukes:** our race data shows a county commission seat on its page.

*M.G.L. c. 34, § 4; c. 34A, § 2; c. 34B, § 2; c. 54, §§ 158, 160 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never say a Massachusetts county "governs" a city or town, sets
local policy, or has a county executive. On a page in Middlesex, Worcester,
Essex, Hampden, Hampshire, Berkshire, Franklin or Suffolk County, do not mention
a county government at all. The sheriff and registers are fine to mention.

**County charters:** every county except Barnstable may adopt a charter under
the County Charter Procedures Act. This is a structural power and gives no
control over how county officers are elected.

*M.G.L. c. 34A, § 2 · statutory · read in statute*

**Below the county:** our data has no township pages: every municipal page is a
city or a town. Special-purpose districts (fire, water, sewer, light, improvement) sit
inside or across towns and some elect their own boards.

*M.G.L. c. 41, § 1A · statutory · read in statute*

## City and town government

Massachusetts has two kinds of municipality: **cities** and **towns**. Our data
has 353 municipal pages: 41 cities with a plain slug, 17 with a `-city`
slug and 295 with a `-town` slug. In state statutes, "town" includes city.

*M.G.L. c. 4, § 7, cl. Thirty-fourth · statutory · read in statute*

### Towns

A town's legislature is its **town meeting**: either an **open town meeting** of
all registered voters or, in towns of at least 6,000 people that choose it, a
**representative town meeting** of members elected by precinct. The town's
executive is an elected **select board** (the statutes still say "board of
selectmen").

What a town elects, by default:

- **Select board:** three or more members, terms of up to three years.
- **School committee:** three or more members, terms of up to three years.
- **Moderator**, who presides at town meeting, where the town elects one.
- Often a town clerk, treasurer, assessors, board of health, constables, tree
  warden and others. Any of these except the select board and school committee
  can be made appointed by town meeting vote plus a ballot vote, and many towns
  have done so. Anything not elected is appointed by the select board.
- **Town meeting members**, in towns with representative town meeting. Under
  the standard form they serve three-year terms, a third elected each year, and
  their nomination papers carry no political designation.

Multi-member boards elect about a third of their seats each year. No town office
can have a term longer than five years.

*Mass. Const. amend. art. LXXXIX, § 2; M.G.L. c. 39, § 14; c. 41, §§ 1, 1B; c. 43A, §§ 4, 6 · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** the list of elected town offices differs in every town. Never
say a town elects a particular office (clerk, treasurer, assessors, constables)
unless race data shows it.

### Cities

A city has a **mayor** (or a city manager hired by the council) and a **city
council**, and usually an elected **school committee**. There are no fixed
classes: each city's structure comes from its own charter or special laws.
State law offers six standard plans (A to F), which Boston cannot adopt:

- **Plan A:** mayor and a council elected at large.
- **Plan B:** mayor and a council elected partly at large and partly by ward.
- **Plan D and Plan E:** council-manager. Plan E elects the council and school
  committee by proportional representation, and the council picks a mayor from
  among its members.
- **Plan F:** mayor and council nominated in party primaries.

Under Plans A and F the mayor's term is two years by default; voters can adopt a
four-year term by ballot question. Cities of 150,000 or more (other than Plan E)
must elect council and school committee seats from equal-population districts.

*M.G.L. c. 43, §§ 1, 2, 17C, 48, 119 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never state a Massachusetts mayor's term or council size unless
race data gives it. They differ city by city.

### "Town" in the name does not mean town government

A town of at least 12,000 people may adopt a city form of government (a town
under 6,000 cannot adopt representative town meeting). Many towns that did so
kept "Town of" in their legal name; state law itself refers to "the city known
as the town of Agawam". In our data, **22 pages with a `-town` slug carry a
mayor or council seat** rather than a select board (a few of those labels look
wrong; see the data issues below), among them Agawam, Amesbury,
Barnstable, Braintree, Bridgewater, Easthampton, Franklin, Methuen, Randolph,
Watertown, West Springfield, Weymouth and Winthrop.

*Mass. Const. amend. art. LXXXIX, § 2; M.G.L. c. 34B, § 6(d)(1) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** decide whether a place has a mayor, a council, a select board or
a town meeting from the offices on the page, never from the slug or the name.
Refer to the place by its name ("Braintree"), not "Braintree Town", and do not
call it a city or a town in copy unless the office list makes that clear.

### Home rule, charters and special acts

Every city and town has home rule under the constitution's Home Rule Amendment.
It can adopt, revise or amend its own charter by local vote, and pass local
ordinances or by-laws on any matter not denied to it by state law or its
charter.

*Mass. Const. amend. art. LXXXIX, §§ 2, 6; M.G.L. c. 43B, §§ 2, 13 · statutory · read in statute*

**Does home rule reach elections?** Only through the charter itself. Home rule
grants **no general power to regulate elections**, but a charter adopted or
revised by local vote can set the structure of government, which offices are
elected, terms, and the mode of election. Changes to the makeup, mode of
election or terms of the council, mayor, manager or select board need a full
charter revision. Two offices must always be elected wherever they exist: the
**select board** and the **school committee**.

*Mass. Const. amend. art. LXXXIX, §§ 4, 7; M.G.L. c. 43B, §§ 18, 20 · statutory · read in statute*

**Special acts** are the other route, and a very common one. The General Court
can pass a law for one city or town on that place's own petition (a "home rule
petition"). Such special laws have the force of a charter. Much of what makes
one Massachusetts town different from the next (an appointed clerk, a town
council, a different election day) comes from a special
act, not from the general laws.

*Mass. Const. amend. art. LXXXIX, §§ 8, 9; M.G.L. c. 4, § 7, cl. Fifth · statutory · read in statute*

So for every Massachusetts city and town, election timing, terms, board sizes
and even the counting method are `varies by place`. Only the county tier and the
rule that select boards and school committees are elected are flat-assertable.

### School committees and districts

Every city and town with its own schools has an elected **school committee**
(the Massachusetts word for a school board). Many towns instead or also belong
to a **regional school district**. Each regional district picks, in its own
agreement, how its committee is chosen: by voters in each member town, by
district-wide election at the November state election (terms set by the
district, up to four years), with weighted votes, or by appointment by local
officials.

*M.G.L. c. 43B, § 20; c. 54, § 162; c. 71, § 14E · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** call it a "school committee", not a "school board", in
Massachusetts copy. Our data has 229 school and other district pages; a
regional district's seats may be elected town by town, so the seat on one town's
page may not be voted on by the whole district.

## Metro and major-place exceptions

### Boston

`ma/suffolk-county/boston`

Boston has its own charter by special law and cannot adopt the standard plans.
Its city council has four at-large and nine district councilors, elected every
two years. Boston, with Chelsea, Revere and
Winthrop, also performs the duties of Suffolk County's commissioners.

*M.G.L. c. 34, § 4; c. 43, § 2; City of Boston, City Council · usual case · local government publication*

### Cambridge

`ma/middlesex-county/cambridge`

Elects by proportional representation under Plan E. Voters rank candidates for nine city councillors and six school committee members,
all elected at large for two-year terms, and the council then chooses one of
its members as mayor. Cambridge voted to adopt a new charter in 2025, so check
race data before describing its structure.

*M.G.L. c. 43, §§ 1, 112; Cambridge Election Commission · usual case · local government publication*

### Nantucket

`ma/nantucket-county` and `ma/nantucket-county/nantucket-town`

Town and county are the same place. The town's select board acts as the county
commissioners, and there is no elected county treasurer.

*M.G.L. c. 34, § 4; c. 54, § 160 · statutory · read in statute*

### Barnstable County and Cape Cod

`ma/barnstable-county` and its town pages

Barnstable County kept a county government under its own charter. Each Cape
Cod town page in our data carries a Barnstable County Assembly of Delegates
seat and, in some years, a county Charter Commission seat. In our data the
Town of Barnstable has a town council, not a select board.

*M.G.L. c. 34A, § 2 · usual case · read in statute*

### Big cities with two pages

`ma/essex-county/lawrence` and `ma/essex-county/lawrence-city`, and ten others
(Chelsea, Haverhill, Malden, Melrose, Newburyport, Northampton, Peabody, Revere,
Woburn, and Framingham with `framingham-town`)

The plain page holds the mayor and council. The `-city` or `-town` twin holds
only a school committee or a district seat for the same place. They are one
government, not two. Treat them as the same city.

## Writing rules

These are the ways Massachusetts copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never infer a county from a place name.** None of our Massachusetts pages
   sits outside the county it is named after, but the Town of Franklin is in
   Norfolk County, not Franklin County. Always take the county from the page
   data.
2. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
3. **Never infer a form of government from the slug or the word "Town".** About
   20 of our `-town` pages are mayor-council or council-manager places. Use the
   offices on the page.
4. **Never mention county government on a page in an abolished county**
   (Middlesex, Worcester, Essex, Hampden, Hampshire, Berkshire, Franklin), or in
   Suffolk. Sheriffs, registers of deeds and probate, and clerks of courts are
   still elected there.
5. **Do not call local races nonpartisan without naming the office.** County
   offices and district attorneys run with party labels. Town races may carry
   party labels where a town uses party caucuses.
6. Say "select board" (or the board's name as shown in race data), "school
   committee", "town meeting" and "unenrolled". Do not say "township", "school
   board", "county executive" or "independent voter".
7. Do not describe a runoff. Massachusetts has none; cities may hold a
   preliminary election instead.
8. Do not state a term length, board size, election month or counting method
   for a city or town unless race data gives it. Charters and special acts vary
   them place by place.
9. Do not call our "City Assessor", "City Clerk" or "City Legislature" labels a
   city office on a town page. Use the office name from race data.
10. Do not treat a `-city` twin page as a separate government (see "Big cities
    with two pages").
11. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
    contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.

## Data issues for the data team

Note these; never write copy around them.

- **Duplicate city pages.** 11 cities have a plain page and a `-city` or
  `-town` twin holding only school committee or district seats (listed above).
- **Position labels misname town offices.** Select boards are filed under
  `city-legislature` (208 pages titled "Town Select Board"); assessors, clerks
  and constables on town pages are labelled "City". Some council titles read
  "Town City Council" (Braintree, Franklin, Palmer, Southbridge, Weymouth).
- **Council labels on select-board towns.** Bourne, Holliston, Leicester and
  South Hadley pages show a "City Council" or "Town Council" seat, though these
  towns are generally governed by a select board. Worth checking upstream.
- **Barnstable has two pages:** `ma/barnstable-town` (no county segment) and
  `ma/barnstable-county/barnstable-town`, plus a position filed under
  `barnstable-county/barnstable-town-city`.
