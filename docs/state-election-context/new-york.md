# New York: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across New York so you do not have to research the same
ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which
offices are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race
data, which always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean. Every claim below has a verbatim quote
in [`research/new-york.json`](./research/new-york.json).

**The one thing to know first:** in New York, local races are partisan almost
everywhere, but the election year depends on the type of government. Cities vote
in odd-numbered years. Most town and county offices now vote in even-numbered
years. Villages pick their own day, and school boards vote every May. A village
sits inside a town, so one street can have a village race and a town race in
different years. Copy that gives one "local election year" for a New York place
will be wrong on a large share of pages.

## Legislative calendar

Next regular session: convenes 6 January 2027 (the Legislature assembles every
year on the first Wednesday after the first Monday in January, N.Y. Const. art.
XIII, § 4). The Legislature meets year-round. Most bills pass by June, but they
reach the governor in batches through the rest of the year (both practice, not
law). The governor has ten days, Sundays excepted, to act on a bill once it is
presented. After the Legislature's final adjournment, a bill becomes law only if
the governor approves it within thirty days (N.Y. Const. art. IV, § 7). A new law
takes effect on the twentieth day after it becomes law unless it sets its own date
(N.Y. Legis. Law § 43).

Re-check this doc: July 2027, then January 2028 to catch bills signed late in
2027. Last researched: October 2026.

## How elections work in New York

Safe on any New York page, at the tier named.

### Election days

The general election is held every year on the Tuesday after the first Monday in
November. The primary is held on the fourth Tuesday in June.

*N.Y. Elec. Law § 8-100(1) · statutory · read in statute*

No local law can move the general election date.

*N.Y. Mun. Home Rule Law § 11(1)(f) · statutory · read in statute*

Which November a local office is on the ballot depends on the type of government:

**Cities** elect their officers in odd-numbered years. This is in the state
constitution, so no city charter can change it. Judges are the exception.

*N.Y. Const. art. XIII, § 8 · statutory · read in statute*

**Towns** elect most officers in even-numbered years, and state law overrides any
town charter or local law on this. Town justices, and town offices that had
three-year terms before 2025, are outside the rule. A town that shares its
boundary exactly with a village, and that voted in odd years before 2025, keeps
odd-year elections.

*N.Y. Town Law § 80 · statutory · read in statute*

**Counties** elect most county officials in even-numbered years, and a county
charter cannot change this. The sheriff, county clerk, district attorney, county
judges and any office that had a three-year term before 2025 are outside the
rule, so they can fall in either kind of year.

*N.Y. County Law § 400(8); N.Y. Mun. Home Rule Law § 34(3)(h) · statutory · read in statute*

The five counties inside New York City are different again: their county
officers are elected in odd-numbered years, with the city.

*N.Y. Const. art. XIII, § 8 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** many sources, including older official ones, say New York town and
county elections are held in odd-numbered years. That is out of date: the
current text of Town Law § 80 and County Law § 400, in force since January 2025,
moves most of them to even years. Never state which year a town or county race
falls in unless race data gives the date.

**Villages** hold their general election on the third Tuesday in March by
default, but a village can choose another date. A village whose elections are
run by the county board of elections can move them to the November general
election. A change of date only applies to an election more than 120 days later.

*N.Y. Elec. Law § 15-104(1)-(2) · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never say a village votes in March, or in November, unless race
data gives the date.

**School districts** hold their annual meeting and board election on the third
Tuesday of May every year. The large city school districts follow their own
rules, and New York City's school board is appointed, not elected.

*N.Y. Educ. Law §§ 2002(1), 2502(2), 2590-b · usual case · read in statute*

### Primary type

Closed. To vote in a party's primary, a voter must be enrolled in that party.
State party rules can allow otherwise, but the default is closed. Party
nominations for offices filled at the general election are made at the primary.

*N.Y. Elec. Law §§ 5-210, 6-110 · statutory · read in statute*

### Fusion voting

New York allows **fusion voting**, also called cross-endorsement: more than one
party can nominate the same candidate. That candidate's name then appears on each
party's line on the ballot. A candidate who is also nominated by an independent
body appears on that body's line too.

*N.Y. Elec. Law § 7-104 · statutory · read in statute*

A party can nominate someone who is not one of its enrolled members only if the
party's committee authorizes it. The candidate must then file a signed acceptance
of each nomination other than their own party's, or that nomination is void.

*N.Y. Elec. Law §§ 6-120, 6-146(1) · statutory · read in statute*

A voter marks one line. If a voter marks the same candidate on two or more lines,
only one vote counts. Official results show a fused candidate's votes on each
line separately, as well as the candidate's total.

*N.Y. Elec. Law §§ 9-112(4), 9-210 · statutory · read in statute*

**Why the separate counts matter.** A political organization keeps its status as
a party, and its automatic place on the ballot, only if its candidate for governor
or president wins a minimum share of the vote. Only the votes cast on that party's
own line count toward it. So the line a voter chooses can matter even when it does
not change who wins.

*N.Y. Elec. Law § 1-104(3) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not call New York primaries open, and do not describe a
candidate as belonging to a single party because one party nominated them. A
candidate may hold several lines. If copy mentions a candidate's party, take every
line from race data rather than picking one.

### Partisanship

**County, city and town races are partisan.** Party candidates are nominated at
the primary and run on party lines in November. Home rule does not change this: a
local law cannot be inconsistent with state law, and a county charter cannot
override the Election Law. We found no route in state law for a county, city or
town to make its races nonpartisan.

*N.Y. Elec. Law §§ 6-110, 7-104; N.Y. Mun. Home Rule Law §§ 10(1), 34(3)(g) · usual case · read in statute*

**Village races run on party or independent lines.** Parties nominate village
candidates at a caucus, or at a primary if the county party's rules say so. Only
party members who live in the village take part. Anyone else runs by independent
petition under the name of an independent body.

*N.Y. Elec. Law § 15-108(2), (4) · statutory · read in statute*

**School board races are nonpartisan.** Candidates are nominated by petition,
not by parties.

*N.Y. Educ. Law § 2018(a) · usual case · read in statute*

### Winning an election

Plurality: the candidate with the most votes wins county, city, town and village
offices. A village election goes to a run-off only to break a tie.

*N.Y. Elec. Law §§ 9-212, 15-104(1)(d) · statutory · read in statute*

**New York City is the exception.** Primaries for mayor, public advocate,
comptroller, borough president and council member use ranked choice voting. So
does any election for those offices where every candidate was nominated by
independent petition. No run-off is held for those offices. State law still
mentions a New York City run-off primary, but the city charter's ranked choice
rules replace it for these offices.

*N.Y.C. Charter § 1057-g; N.Y. Elec. Law § 6-162 · statutory · read in statute*

### Running as an independent

An independent candidate is nominated by a petition signed by registered voters
of the area the office covers. A signature does not count if the signer has also
signed another petition, party or independent, for the same office. So a voter
who signs a party candidate's petition cannot also sign an independent's petition
for that office.

*N.Y. Elec. Law § 6-138(1) · statutory · read in statute*

Village independents also run by petition, and school board candidates all do.

*N.Y. Elec. Law § 15-108(4); N.Y. Educ. Law § 2018(a) · statutory · read in statute*

## County government

Our data has 62 New York county pages. Five of those counties lie entirely
inside New York City and have no county government of their own (see
[New York City](#new-york-city) below).

*N.Y. Elec. Law § 3-200(1); N.Y. Const. art. IX, § 1(a) · statutory · read in statute*

**The governing body's name varies.** State law calls it a board of supervisors
by default, made up of the supervisors of the county's towns and cities. The law
also recognizes it under other names, such as county legislature, and counties
differ in how its members are elected. Never guess the name. Use the office names
in race data.

*N.Y. County Law §§ 150, 214(1) · varies by place · read in statute*

Every county outside New York City elects a **sheriff**, **county clerk**,
**district attorney** and **county treasurer**, each for four years. Some counties
still elect coroners, and a county may replace them with an appointed medical
examiner.

*N.Y. County Law § 400(1), (1-a), (2) · statutory · read in statute*

**County charters.** A county outside New York City may adopt a charter that
sets its own form of government. Some have an elected county executive; race data
shows which.

*N.Y. Const. art. IX, § 1(h)(1) · varies by place · read in statute*

**Does a charter reach elections?** Only partly. A charter cannot override the
Election Law or the even-year rule for county elections. A local law that changes
how an officer is nominated or elected, or changes a term, must go to the voters.

*N.Y. Mun. Home Rule Law §§ 23(2)(e), 34(3)(g)-(h) · statutory · read in statute*

**Below the county:** cities and towns, with villages inside towns. New York has
no townships. A New York town is a full municipal government in its own right.

*N.Y. Town Law § 2; N.Y. Village Law § 2-250 · statutory · read in statute*

## City, town and village government

New York has three kinds of municipality: cities, towns and villages. They elect
different offices, in different years, under different laws.

### Cities

Each city is governed by its own charter, which sets its offices, council size
and form of government. City races are partisan and held in odd-numbered years.
A new city charter must be approved by the city's voters.

*N.Y. Mun. Home Rule Law § 23(2)(a); N.Y. Const. art. XIII, § 8 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** "City" in a place's name does not make it a city. Our Garden City
and Johnson City pages are separate places whose names happen to end in "City".
Take the type of government from the offices on the page.

### Towns

A town is a municipal corporation, not an unincorporated area and not a township.
Towns are first or second class, mostly by population: towns of 10,000 or more
are usually first class. The law writes in exceptions. Every Westchester town is
first class, and every Suffolk and Broome town is second class whatever its size.
A second class town that reaches 10,000 at the census becomes first class.

*N.Y. Town Law §§ 2, 10, 11(1) · statutory · read in statute*

The **town board** is the **town supervisor** plus the **town council members**.
A first class town has four council members by default, which can be changed to
six or two. It also elects a town clerk, town justices, a superintendent of
highways and a receiver of taxes. Second class towns have a smaller default
board and elect a similar list of offices.

*N.Y. Town Law §§ 20(1), 60(1) · usual case · read in statute*

Most town offices, including supervisor, have two-year terms by default. Council
members serve staggered four-year terms, and town justices serve four years.

*N.Y. Town Law § 24 · usual case · read in statute*

### Villages

A village sits inside one or more towns, so village residents are also town
residents and vote in both. A village cannot include part of a city.

*N.Y. Village Law §§ 2-200(1), 2-250 · statutory · read in statute*

Every village elects a **mayor** and a **board of trustees**, four trustees by
default. The board can change the number of trustees, subject to a referendum if
voters petition for one. Mayor and trustees serve two-year terms by default.

*N.Y. Village Law §§ 3-301(1), 3-302(3), 3-304 · usual case · read in statute*

### Home rule and terms

New York has no home rule population threshold. Every county, city, town and
village can pass local laws on how its officers are chosen and how long they
serve, as long as the local law is consistent with state law. Changing how an
office is elected, changing a term, abolishing an elected office or changing the
size of a city, town or village board needs approval at a referendum.

*N.Y. Mun. Home Rule Law §§ 10(1), 23(2)(b), 23(2)(e) · statutory · read in statute*

**Does home rule reach elections?** Partly. Terms and board size can change
locally with voter approval. Election years cannot: they are fixed by the
constitution for cities and by state law for towns and counties. Partisanship
cannot either.

## Metro and major-place exceptions

### New York City

`ny/queens-county/new-york`

New York City spans five counties, one for each borough. Copy must never tie
the city to one county. Our page is filed under Queens County, which is a data
error (see below), not a fact about the city.

*N.Y. Elec. Law § 3-200(1) · statutory · read in statute*

The city elects a mayor, public advocate, comptroller, borough presidents and
the city council, in odd-numbered years, with ranked choice voting in the
primaries.

*N.Y.C. Charter § 1057-g; N.Y. Const. art. XIII, § 8 · statutory · read in statute*

### New York City's five counties

`ny/new-york-county`, `ny/kings-county`, `ny/queens-county`, `ny/bronx-county`,
`ny/richmond-county`

These counties have no county legislature and no county executive. Their county
powers belong to the city. Each county elects a district attorney, but its county
clerk is appointed. These pages carry only judicial and district attorney races.
Never describe a county government, county legislature or county executive on
them.

*N.Y. Const. art. IX, §§ 1(a), 2(e); art. XIII, § 13(a) · statutory · read in statute*

### Big upstate and suburban cities

`ny/erie-county/buffalo`, `ny/monroe-county/rochester`,
`ny/westchester-county/yonkers`, `ny/onondaga-county/syracuse`,
`ny/albany-county/albany`

Each has its own charter, so council structure and citywide offices vary. All
vote in odd-numbered years and on party lines. Rochester also has a second
page, `ny/monroe-county/rochester-city`, which holds only its city court race
(see data issues).

*N.Y. Const. art. XIII, § 8 · statutory · read in statute*

### A town and a village or city with the same name

176 of our New York municipal pages share a name with another page in the same
county. In each pair, the page ending in `-town` is the town, and the other page
is a village or city with the same name. For example,
`ny/livingston-county/livonia` and `ny/livingston-county/livonia-town`, or
`ny/steuben-county/corning` and `ny/steuben-county/corning-town`. These are
separate governments with separate elections, often in different years. Never
merge them, and never call one part of the other unless the place is a village
inside its town.

## Data issues for the data team

Do not write copy around these. They are listed so the data can be fixed.

1. **New York City is filed under Queens County.** `ny/queens-county/new-york`
   holds the mayor, comptroller, public advocate and council races. The city spans
   five counties, so the page should not sit under any one of them.
2. **Fourteen cities have two pages.** Ithaca, Norwich, Rochester, Mechanicville,
   Canandaigua, Elmira, Ogdensburg, Johnstown, Hudson, Glen Cove, Long Beach,
   Plattsburgh, Auburn and Gloversville each have a plain page and a `-city` page,
   and their offices are split between the two. Most `-city` pages hold only a
   city court judge. Some hold a county legislature seat or a receiver of taxes,
   which do not belong to the city.
3. **Town offices are labelled as township offices.** Town supervisor, town
   council, town clerk, highway superintendent and receiver of taxes all use
   `township-*` labels, and council seats use "township trustee". New York has
   no townships and no town trustees.
4. **Village offices are labelled as city offices.** Village mayor and trustee
   seats use `city-executive-mayor` and `city-legislature`, so neither the slug
   nor the labels tell a village from a city. The village of New York Mills
   (`ny/oneida-county/new-york-mills`) is one example.

## Writing rules

These are the ways New York copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never infer a county from a place name.** 7 of our New York municipal pages
   share a name with a different New York county: Allegany (Cattaraugus County),
   Clinton (Dutchess County), Hamilton and Oneida (Madison County), Fulton
   (Oswego County), Nassau (Rensselaer County) and New York (filed under Queens,
   see data issues). The full list is in
   [`research/places-named-after-another-county.json`](./research/places-named-after-another-county.json).
   Always take the county from the page data.
2. **Never tie New York City to one county**, and never describe a county
   government in its five counties.
3. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
4. **Say "town", never "township"**, even though our position labels say
   township. Do not call a town council member a trustee.
5. **Do not call local races nonpartisan**, except school board races. County,
   city, town and village races run on party or independent lines.
6. **Do not give one election year for a place.** Cities vote in odd years, most
   town and county offices in even years, villages on their own date, school
   boards every May. Do not state the year or month unless race data gives it.
7. Do not infer whether a page is a city or a village from its slug or its
   position labels. A `-town` slug is a town. A plain slug can be either.
8. Do not merge a town with the village or city of the same name.
9. Do not call New York primaries open, and do not tie a candidate to one party
   when fusion voting may give them several party lines.
10. Do not assume a run-off. Plurality wins, except New York City's ranked choice
    primaries.
11. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
    contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.
