# Ohio: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Ohio so you do not have to research the same ground
on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which offices are
actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race data, which
always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean.

**The one thing to know first:** Ohio's local races are partisan at one tier and
nonpartisan at the next. County offices carry party labels. Township and school
board races never do by default. City and village races depend on how that one
place nominated its candidates back in 1989, so they cannot be described from
state law at all.

## Legislative calendar

The General Assembly meets year-round, in two-year assemblies, so a change can
pass in any month. It next convenes on 4 January 2027 (the first Monday of
January, in first regular session in odd-numbered years and second regular
session the following year, Ohio Const. art. II, § 8). A law usually takes
effect ninety days after the governor files it with the secretary of state
(Ohio Const. art. II, § 1c).

Re-check this doc: January and July each year. Last researched: October 2026. Reviewed by Emily: 27 October 2026.

## How elections work in Ohio

The timing rules below hold on any Ohio page. Partisanship does not: read that
section before writing anything about party labels.

### Election days

The constitution splits local elections by year. **County** officers are elected
in November of **even-numbered** years, on the same ballot as state and federal
races. **Municipal and township** officers and **school board** members are
elected in November of **odd-numbered** years, at the regular municipal election
(the first Tuesday after the first Monday in November).

*Ohio Const. art. XVII, § 1; R.C. 3501.01(B), 3501.02(C), (D) · statutory · read in statute*

So a county page and a city page in the same county are almost never about the
same election year. Avoid putting county offices and city offices on one ballot,
but let our own election data decide: if race data shows them together, follow it.

The primary is the first Tuesday after the first Monday in **May**. In a
presidential primary year it moves to the third Tuesday after the first Monday
in **March**.

*R.C. 3501.01(E) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** a municipal or county **charter** may set its own primary day,
and special elections on local questions may run on other days where a law or
charter allows. Say "the May primary" only where race data agrees. The
November general election rule itself is constitutional and holds everywhere.

*R.C. 3501.01(D), (E)(2), 3501.02 · statutory · read in statute*

These sections were amended in 2025 and again with effect from June 2026 (House
Bill 96, Senate Bills 63 and 293 of the 136th General Assembly). The rules above
are the text in force when this was researched.

### Primary type

A voter picks a party's ballot at the primary, and the primaries they vote in
become their party record. A voter who asks for a party's
ballot can be challenged if they voted in another party's primary in the
current or previous two calendar years, and they can still vote by signing a
statement that they want to affiliate with the party whose ballot they asked
for.

*R.C. 3513.19 · statutory · read in statute*

For our audience: an unaffiliated Ohioan can take a party ballot, but doing so
puts them on record with that party. Many local offices in Ohio have no party
primary at all (see below), so this matters mainly on county pages and in
partisan cities.

### Partisanship

This is the part of Ohio that is easiest to get wrong. It runs by tier.

**County: partisan.** County offices are filled through party primaries and
elected in even years.

*R.C. 3513.01(A), 3513.04 · statutory · read in statute*

The two charter counties keep this: their charters say the executive and
council are nominated and elected the way general law provides for county
officers.

*Summit County Charter §§ 2.01, 3.01.1; Cuyahoga County Charter § 2.01 · varies by place · read in county charter*

**Township: nonpartisan by default.** Township trustees and fiscal officers are
nominated only by petition and appear on the nonpartisan ballot, unless a
majority of the township's electors petition for party primaries.

*R.C. 3513.01(C), 3513.253, 3505.04 · usual case · read in statute*

**School board: always nonpartisan.** Candidates for city, local and exempted
village school boards, and for educational service center boards, appear on the
nonpartisan ballot.

*R.C. 3513.254, 3513.255 · statutory · read in statute*

**Villages and cities under 2,000: nonpartisan by default.** No party primary is
held unless a majority of electors petition for one.

*R.C. 3513.01(C) · usual case · read in statute*

**Cities and villages of 2,000 or more: varies by place.** They nominate the way
each one did in **1989**, unless their voters have changed it since by
referendum, in either direction. Charter municipalities may also put their
offices on a separate nonpartisan ballot.

*R.C. 3513.01(B), (D), (E), 3505.04 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never write "local elections in Ohio are nonpartisan" or "Ohio
city races are partisan". Neither holds statewide. On a city or village page,
only race data can say whether party labels appear. Township and school board
pages can safely say their races carry no party label.

### Winning

The candidate with the most votes wins, and ties are resolved by lot. State law
has no general runoff. Ranked choice and instant runoff voting are banned,
except as permitted under the county and municipal home rule articles of the
constitution.

*R.C. 3505.33, 3505.011 · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** charter cities can run their own nominating systems (for example
a nonpartisan primary that narrows the field), so do not write "no runoff" or
"one round" on a charter city page. The ranked choice ban took its current form
in June 2026.

### Getting on the ballot as an independent

An independent candidate is one who claims no party affiliation and files a
statement of candidacy and a nominating petition. For an office that has party
primaries, the filing is due the day before the primary. Signers must be
voters qualified to vote on that office.

*R.C. 3501.01(I), 3513.257, 3501.38(A) · statutory · read in statute*

A person who ran in a party primary cannot then run as an independent at the
following general election. School board and township trustee races are
exempt from this rule.

*R.C. 3513.04 · statutory · read in statute*

For nonpartisan township and school board races, every candidate gets on the
ballot by petition, so "independent" is not a separate path there.

## County government

Our data has **88** county pages for Ohio, and every Ohio municipal and township
page sits under one of them.

*Our page data (pages-by-state.csv, 27 October 2026)*

### Board of county commissioners

A board of **three county commissioners**, elected on staggered four-year
terms: two seats in one even year and the third seat two years later. Counties also elect a prosecuting attorney, auditor, treasurer,
clerk of the court of common pleas, sheriff, recorder, engineer and coroner.

*R.C. 305.01, 305.02(A) · usual case · read in statute*

The legislature may offer alternative forms of county government, which apply
only where county voters adopt one.

*Ohio Const. art. X, § 1 · statutory · read in statute*

### Charter counties

Any county may adopt a charter, and the charter decides the form of government
and **which officers are elected and how**. Elections are not reserved to state
law at the county tier.

*Ohio Const. art. X, § 3 · statutory · read in statute*

Two counties have done so. Both have an elected **county executive** and an
**eleven-member county council** in place of the three commissioners.

- **Summit County** (`oh/summit-county`): executive and eleven council members,
  nominated and elected as general law provides for county officers.
  *Summit County Charter §§ 2.01, 3.01.1 · varies by place · read in county charter*
- **Cuyahoga County** (`oh/cuyahoga-county`): executive and eleven council
  members elected by district to four-year terms.
  *Cuyahoga County Charter §§ 2.01, 3.01, 3.02 · varies by place · read in county charter*

Never write "three commissioners" on either page.

### Townships

Townships are Ohio's main sub-county layer and have their own pages in our data,
with slugs ending in `-township` (`oh/butler-county/west-chester-township`).
Every township has a board of **three trustees** on staggered four-year terms
and an elected **fiscal officer**. Their races are nonpartisan by default (see
above).

*Ohio Const. art. X, § 2; R.C. 505.01, 3513.253 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** a city or village and a township can share a name and a county
(`oh/butler-county/oxford` and `oh/butler-county/oxford-township`). They are
different governments with different offices and different partisanship rules.
Take the type from the slug and never drop the word "Township".

## City and village government

Ohio has two classes of municipality: **cities** (5,000 or more people) and
**villages** (everyone else). Class follows the federal census in both
directions, so a village that grows past 5,000 becomes a city and a city that
falls below it becomes a village.

*Ohio Const. art. XVIII, § 1 · statutory · read in statute*

### Village

A **mayor** and a **six-member council** elected at large, all on four-year
terms. The mayor presides over council.

*R.C. 731.09(A), 733.24 · usual case · read in statute*

### City (statutory plan)

The default for a city without a charter or optional plan. Elects a **mayor**
(four years), a **president of council** (two years), an **auditor**, a
**treasurer** and a **director of law** (four years each), and a **council** of
at least seven, four by ward and three at large, growing with population to at
most seventeen. Council members serve **two-year** terms.

*R.C. 731.01(A), 731.03(A), 733.02, 733.09(A), 733.10, 733.42, 733.49 · usual case · read in statute*

Voters can change several of these by referendum: council size to anything
from five to seventeen, and council terms from two years to four.

*R.C. 731.01(B), 731.03(B) · usual case · read in statute*

### Optional plans

State law also offers optional forms (commission, city manager and federal
plans) that a municipality's voters may adopt. They change which offices are
elected, so let race data supply the offices.

*Ohio Const. art. XVIII, § 2 · varies by place · read in statute*

### Charter municipalities (home rule)

**Any** municipality, city or village, may adopt a charter and exercise all
powers of local self-government. The only election limit the home rule article
imposes is the constitution's voter qualification rule. A charter can set the
offices, terms, council size, primary date and whether races are nonpartisan.

*Ohio Const. art. XVIII, §§ 3, 7 · statutory · read in statute*

The Election Code confirms this in several places: charters may set their own
primary day, put municipal offices on a separate nonpartisan ballot, and are
the one route left for ranked choice voting.

*R.C. 3501.01(D), 3505.04, 3505.011 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** none of the city or village templates above can be asserted for a
charter municipality, and we do not hold which places have charters. On city
pages, describe the offices race data
shows, not a template.

## Metro and major-place exceptions

### Cuyahoga County and Summit County

`oh/cuyahoga-county`, `oh/summit-county`

The two charter counties, covered above. County executive and eleven-member
council, partisan, elected in even years.

### Columbus

`oh/franklin-county/columbus`

Our data files it under Franklin County only; the
Delaware County and Fairfield County versions of the URL redirect there. Do not
write that Columbus lies entirely in Franklin County, and do not infer anything
from the redirect.

*Our page data, checked 27 October 2026*

### Cleveland schools

`oh/cuyahoga-county/cleveland`

State law gives a school district that has been under federal court supervision
a nine-member board **appointed by the mayor**, not elected. The Cleveland
Metropolitan School District is run this way. Never describe Cleveland's school
board as elected or write that Cleveland voters choose it.

*R.C. 3311.71; City of Cleveland, CMSD Board of Education Appointment Information · varies by place · local government publication*

### Other large cities

`oh/hamilton-county/cincinnati`, `oh/lucas-county/toledo`,
`oh/summit-county/akron`, `oh/montgomery-county/dayton`

The big-city differences come from city charters, which this doc did not read.
Assume a charter,
describe only what race data shows, and do not assume partisan or nonpartisan
races either way.

## Writing rules

These are the ways Ohio copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never say Ohio local elections are nonpartisan, or that they are
   partisan.** County races are partisan, township and school board races are
   nonpartisan, and city and village races vary by place.
2. **Avoid putting county and city offices in the same election year.** Counties
   vote in even years, cities, villages, townships and school boards in odd
   years. Our own election data still comes first.
3. **Never infer a county from a place name.** 26 Ohio municipal pages are named
   after a county they are not in, for example Hamilton (Butler County),
   Franklin (Warren County), Warren (Trumbull County) and Lucas (Richland
   County). Always take the county from the page data.
4. **Keep "Township" in township names.** 114 Ohio pages share a name with
   another place in the same county, usually a city or village and a township.
   Take the government type from the slug.
5. **Never write "three commissioners" for Summit or Cuyahoga County.**
6. **Never call the Cleveland school board elected.**
7. Do not state seat counts or term lengths as fixed for a city. Charters,
   optional plans and referendums can all change them. Village and township
   numbers are safer, but still let race data win.
8. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
9. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
   contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.

### What search results commonly get wrong

- Ohio is often called an "open primary" state. It is closer to "pick a party at
  the polls": the ballot a voter takes becomes their party record and can be
  challenged at the next primary.
- Summaries often say Ohio municipal elections are nonpartisan. Many cities of
  2,000 or more still nominate in party primaries under the 1989 rule.
- Township trustees are sometimes listed with party labels. By default they are
  nominated by petition and elected on the nonpartisan ballot.

**Bulk-access recipe.** Whole chapters download as one page:
`https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/chapter-<n>` (e.g. `chapter-3513`),
and whole constitution articles as `https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-constitution/article-<n>`
with an Arabic numeral (`article-18`). Single sections are
`.../ohio-revised-code/section-<n>`; single constitution sections redirect.
Each section on a chapter page shows its effective date and latest act, which is
the quickest amendment check. The site rate-limits after about a dozen quick
requests (HTTP 429), so download one at a time with a pause, and keep the
ledger on a dozen or fewer distinct codes.ohio.gov pages so `verify-ledger.ts`
does not trip it. The charter PDFs extract with the zlib fallback.

Aggregator sites were not used and must not appear in published copy. Statutes
may be cited by name and number.
