# Illinois: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Illinois so you do not have to research the same
ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which
offices are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race
data, which always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean.

**The one thing to know first:** Illinois runs local elections on two separate
cycles. County offices are elected in November of even-numbered years, on the
same ballot as state and federal races. Almost everything else that is local
(cities, villages, townships, school boards, park, library and fire districts)
is elected in April of odd-numbered years, at what Illinois calls the
**consolidated election**. Copy that puts a city or school board race in
November is wrong almost everywhere in the state.

## Legislative calendar

The General Assembly is a continuous body that can meet in any month, but most
bills pass in the spring session. It next convenes on 13 January 2027 (the
second Wednesday of January each year, Ill. Const. art. IV, § 5). A bill passed
after 31 May cannot take effect before the next 1 June without a three-fifths
vote (Ill. Const. art. IV, § 10). A bill passed before 1 June with no date of its
own takes effect on 1 January of the following year (5 ILCS 75/1). A bill goes to
the Governor within 30 days of passage and the Governor has 60 days to act (Ill.
Const. art. IV, § 9), so spring bills are settled by about the end of August.

## How elections work in Illinois

Safe on any Illinois page, at any tier.

### Election days

Illinois law fixes one statewide schedule, and no election may be held on any
other date. This applies even to home rule places, because the Election Code
governs "notwithstanding the provisions of any other statute or municipal
charter".

*10 ILCS 5/2A-1 · statutory · read in statute*

**Even-numbered years.** The general primary is the third Tuesday in March. The
general election is the first Tuesday after the first Monday in November.
County offices are filled here: county board members or commissioners, the
sheriff, clerk, treasurer, state's attorney, circuit court clerk and the other
elected county offices. Trustees of the Metropolitan Water Reclamation District
of Greater Chicago and of other sanitary districts are also elected in November.

*10 ILCS 5/2A-1.1(a), 2A-1.2(a)(5), 2A-1.2(a)(10) · statutory · read in statute*

**Odd-numbered years.** The consolidated primary is the last Tuesday in
February. The consolidated election is the first Tuesday in April (pushed back
a week when it falls during Passover). Filled here: municipal officers, township
officers, highway commissioners, school boards outside Chicago, community college
boards, and park, library and fire protection district boards.

*10 ILCS 5/2A-1.1(b), 2A-1.1a, 2A-1.2(c) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** publish the rule, not the date. "Illinois elects its village
boards and school boards in April of odd-numbered years" is safe. A specific
calendar date is not.

**Watch out:** a city or village with annual elections may fill some offices on
the even-year March primary date instead (10 ILCS 5/2A-1.2(b)(3)). This is
rare. Let race data override the April rule when it shows a March municipal
race.

### Can a place move its own election date?

No. The schedule is set by state law and there is no local opt-out, by
ordinance, referendum or home rule. The narrow exceptions are statutory:
Passover, the even-year March date for places with annual elections, emergency
referenda approved by a court, and votes at the annual township meeting.

*10 ILCS 5/2A-1(b) · statutory · read in statute*

### School and special districts

School boards (outside Chicago), community college boards and fire protection
district trustees are filled at the April consolidated election and are left out
of party nominations at the consolidated primary. Most school districts are run
by a seven-member board of education with four-year terms.

*10 ILCS 5/2A-1.2(c)(12)-(14), 2A-1.2(d); 105 ILCS 5/10-10 · statutory · read in statute*

Chicago is different: its Board of Education members are elected at the
November general election in even-numbered years, on a nonpartisan basis.

*10 ILCS 5/2A-1.2(a)(12) · statutory · read in statute*

Park districts, library districts and other special districts also elect in
April. Whether a given district is partisan depends on the law that created it,
so do not label a special district race partisan or nonpartisan unless race data
says so.

*10 ILCS 5/2A-1.2(c) · varies by place · read in statute*

### Primary type

Illinois has **no party registration**. To vote in a primary, a voter tells the
election judges which party's ballot they want and receives only that ballot. A
voter who will not name a party cannot vote a party primary.

*10 ILCS 5/7-43(a), 7-44 · statutory · read in statute*

What it commits the voter to: someone who votes a party's ballot at the March
general primary **cannot then run as an independent** (or for another party) at
the November general election that follows. The choice of ballot is not a
lasting registration: it is made again at each primary.

*10 ILCS 5/7-43 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** search results call Illinois both "open" and "closed". Neither
word is quite right. The accurate phrasing is that Illinois voters do not
register by party and choose a party ballot at each primary. That choice is
public, and it matters for a voter thinking of running as an independent.

The primary is decided by plurality. There is no primary runoff.

*10 ILCS 5/7-59(a) · statutory · read in statute*

### Partisanship

**County offices are partisan.** Candidates are nominated at the March party
primary and appear with a party label in November.

*10 ILCS 5/7-1(a), 2A-1.2(a)(5) · statutory · read in statute*

**School boards are nonpartisan.** School elections sit outside the party
primary entirely.

*10 ILCS 5/7-1(b), 2A-1.2(d) · statutory · read in statute*

**Municipal partisanship varies by place, and is decided in state law plus
local votes, not by a charter.**

- A village may elect in nonpartisan primaries and general elections, and its
  voters can switch either way by referendum. Villages that were partisan before
  1992 may simply stay partisan.
- A city or village under the commission form (Article 4) or the managerial form
  (Article 5) elects on a nonpartisan basis.
- In a town of 5,000 or fewer, parties nominate by caucus or petition rather
  than by primary, unless the town has opted into a primary.
- Candidates may also run under a party that exists only in that one town.

*65 ILCS 5/3.1-25-20, 3.1-25-60, 3.1-25-65; 10 ILCS 5/10-3.1, 7-1(b), 10-1, 7-44 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never write "Illinois city elections are nonpartisan" or "are
partisan". Both are wrong for hundreds of places. Small-town ballots often show
a local party name that is not a state party. Describe what the race data shows.

**Township offices are usually partisan.** Most townships nominate by a party
caucus in December; larger townships may switch to a party primary.

*60 ILCS 1/45-10, 45-55 · usual case · read in statute*

### Plurality or majority

Plurality is the default. Whoever gets the most votes wins, with no runoff.
This is fixed in law for county offices and party primaries.

*10 ILCS 5/22-2, 7-59 · statutory · read in statute*

The exceptions are places whose own statute or form-of-government ordinance
provides a runoff. Chicago is the main one (see below).

*10 ILCS 5/2A-1.2(c)(1) · varies by place · read in statute*

### Independent candidates

An independent gets on the ballot by filing nomination papers signed by voters
of the district. Nonpartisan candidates for municipal and district offices also
file petitions, under the rules for that office.

*10 ILCS 5/10-3, 10-3.1 · statutory · read in statute*

Who is barred: a person who circulates petitions may not circulate for an
independent and a party candidate in the same election. A voter who signs a
party's primary petition may not sign another party's. We found no rule that
stops a primary voter from signing an independent's petition, so do not assert
one either way. The main bar falls on the candidate: voting a party ballot at
the March primary rules out running as an independent that November.

*10 ILCS 5/10-4, 7-10, 7-43 · statutory · read in statute*

## County government

102 counties (our page count). There are no independent cities and no
consolidated city-county governments: Chicago is a city inside Cook County.

Every county elects a **county board**. In most counties the board has between 5
and 29 members, elected at large or from districts, as the board decides at each
redistricting. Seats are usually four-year terms, with one two-year term in each
seat's rotation after a redistricting. A board may let voters elect its chair.

*Ill. Const. art. VII, § 3(a); 55 ILCS 5/2-3002, 2-3007, 2-3009 · usual case · read in statute*

Every county elects a sheriff, a county clerk and a treasurer. Counties may also
elect a coroner, recorder, assessor, auditor and other officers, and voters
elect a state's attorney and circuit court clerk at the same November election.
Elected county officers serve four-year terms.

*Ill. Const. art. VII, § 4(c); 10 ILCS 5/2A-1.2(a)(5)-(6) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** which optional offices a county elects (coroner, recorder,
assessor, auditor) varies. The constitution lets each county elect or appoint
them, and lets voters eliminate them. Never list them as if every county
elects them.

### Commission counties

A county without townships may instead be run by **three county commissioners**
elected at large to six-year terms, one elected each general election. Use
"board of commissioners" there, not "county board members".

*55 ILCS 5/2-4006 · usual case · read in statute*

### Home rule

County home rule does not come from a charter. A county is home rule when it
has a chief executive elected by its voters. Cook County is one. A home rule county can
change its officers only in the ways the constitution allows, mostly by
countywide referendum, and it cannot move its election dates (see above).

*Ill. Const. art. VII, §§ 4, 6(a), 6(f) · statutory · read in statute*

### Townships

Many Illinois counties are divided into **townships**, a full layer of
government between the county and the town. Voters in each county decided by
referendum whether to have them.

*Ill. Const. art. VII, § 5 · statutory · read in statute*

Each township elects, to four-year terms in April of odd years:

- a **supervisor**, who chairs the township board and runs general assistance
- a **clerk**
- an **assessor** (or a multi-township assessor shared with neighbors)
- a **collector** in some townships
- **four trustees**, who sit on the township board with the supervisor
- a **highway commissioner** for the township's road district

Townships also hold an annual town meeting each April where voters vote
directly on some township business.

*60 ILCS 1/50-5, 80-5, 30-5; 10 ILCS 5/2A-1.2(c)(7)-(8) · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** a township is **not** the town of the same name. Taylorville
and Taylorville Township are two different governments, each with its own
officers and its own races, even though they share a name. Our data has 464 of
these same-name pairs in Illinois. The township's page always ends in
`-township` (`il/christian-county/taylorville-township` next to
`il/christian-county/taylorville`), so you can always tell which one a page is
about from its slug. Never describe township offices on a city or
village page, or city offices on a township page.

## City, village and town government

Illinois has three kinds of municipality: **cities**, **villages** and
**incorporated towns**. School districts are not municipalities.

*Ill. Const. art. VII, § 1 · statutory · read in statute*

### City

Elects a **mayor**, **alderpersons** (the council), a **city clerk** and a
**city treasurer**. A city of 10,000 or fewer may make the treasurer an
appointed post. The number of alderpersons is set by population, from 6 in the
smallest cities to 20, with two per ward. The mayor serves four years.

*65 ILCS 5/3.1-15-5, 3.1-15-10, 3.1-20-5, 3.1-20-10, 3.1-20-15 · usual case · read in statute*

### Village

Elects a **village president**, six **trustees** and a
**village clerk**. Trustees serve four years. The president may also be called
mayor.

*65 ILCS 5/3.1-15-5, 3.1-15-10, 3.1-25-5 · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** a village has a **board of trustees**, not a city council and not
aldermen. Do not call a village a city, however large it is. A village keeps
being a village as it grows (see below).

### Incorporated town

Elects a president, trustees and a clerk, like a village.

*65 ILCS 5/3.1-15-5 · usual case · read in statute*

### Commission and managerial forms

A city or village can adopt a different form of government by referendum. The
commission form (Article 4) and the managerial form (Article 5) both elect on a
nonpartisan basis, and their offices differ from the templates above. If race
data shows commissioners instead of alderpersons or trustees, this is why.

*10 ILCS 5/10-3.1; Ill. Const. art. VII, §§ 6(f), 7 · varies by place · read in statute*

### Class does not follow population

A village keeps its class when it grows. A village or town of 2,500 or more
**may** choose to become a city by referendum, but nothing forces it to. Never
work out whether a place is a city or a village from its population. Take it
from our data.

*65 ILCS 5/2-2-4 · statutory · read in statute*

### Home rule

Home rule does not come from adopting a charter. A municipality
over 25,000 people is home rule automatically, and a smaller one can become home
rule by referendum. Any home rule unit can also vote to give it up.

A few places are still organized under special charters, and their
officers are not nominated through the party primary.

*Ill. Const. art. VII, § 6(a)-(b); 10 ILCS 5/7-1(b) · statutory · read in statute*

Home rule does **not** reach elections freely. A home rule municipality can
change its officers, how they are chosen and their terms only by referendum or
as state law allows, and the Election Code still sets the dates and conduct of
the election.

*Ill. Const. art. VII, § 6(f); 10 ILCS 5/2A-1 · statutory · read in statute*

### Changing terms and council size

All of these happen by local referendum, within limits set by state law:

- A municipality under 500,000 can cut terms from four years to two.
- A city under 100,000 can halve its council to one alderperson per ward.
- A village under 5,000 can cut its board from six trustees to four.

*65 ILCS 5/3.1-10-65, 3.1-20-20, 3.1-25-10 · varies by place · read in statute*

So do not state a term length or seat count as fixed for a specific place.

## Metro and major-place exceptions

Illinois has 3,627 location pages, the most of any state: 102 county pages, 835
school and special district pages, and 2,689 city, village and township pages
(our 27 October 2026 snapshot).

### Chicago

`il/cook-county/chicago`

Chicago runs on its own statute and breaks most of the rules above.

- The **mayor, city clerk and city treasurer** are elected in a **nonpartisan**
  election. A candidate who wins a majority at the **February** consolidated
  primary is elected outright. Otherwise the top two meet in a runoff at the
  April consolidated election.
- The **City Council** has **50 alderpersons**, one per ward, serving four-year
  terms. Each also needs a majority, with an April runoff if nobody gets one.
- The elected members of the **Board of Education** are chosen in November of
  even-numbered years, not in April (see school districts above).

*65 ILCS 20/21-5, 21-12, 21-22, 21-25, 21-26, 21-36; 10 ILCS 5/2A-1.2(a)(12) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** in Chicago the February date can be the deciding election, not
a primary in the usual sense. Do not write "Chicago's mayoral primary".

### Cook County

`il/cook-county`

A **home rule** county, because its board president is
elected countywide and serves as the county's chief executive. The board has 17
commissioners elected every four years. Cook can change how its board is elected
in ways no other county can. Describe each commissioner by the district in race
data, not as a "Chicago" or "suburban" seat.

*Ill. Const. art. VII, §§ 3(b)-(c), 4(b), 6(a); 55 ILCS 5/2-6001 · statutory · read in statute*

Townships in Cook County follow slightly different rules on which officers they
elect.

*60 ILCS 1/50-10 · statutory · read in statute*

### Large suburbs

A large suburb may be a village rather than a city. A village over 25,000 is
home rule, but it still elects a village president (who may be titled mayor)
and a board of trustees. Check the municipality type in our
data before writing "city", and never infer it from size.

*Ill. Const. art. VII, § 6(a); 65 ILCS 5/2-2-4, 3.1-15-10 · statutory · read in statute*

### Cities that cross county lines

Our pages file every city under one county, but a city can extend into others.
Avoid phrasing that ties a city to only one county unless the page is about
that county.

### Any place with commissioners, a city manager or a runoff

If race data shows city commissioners, a managerial form or a runoff, that place
has adopted a form of government by referendum and the city or village template
does not apply. Let race data override structure, never timing.

## Writing rules

These are the ways Illinois copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never put a city, village, township or school board election in
   November.** They are in April of odd-numbered years. In November it is
   county offices, sanitary district trustees and Chicago's school board.
2. **Never infer a county from a place name.** 37 Illinois places share a name
   with an Illinois county and 21 of them sit in a *different* county. Marion is
   in Williamson County, and Williamson is in Madison County. Kane is in Greene
   County, and Montgomery is in Kane County. Always take the county from the page
   data.
3. **Never mix up a town and its township.** Our data has 464 same-name pairs.
   The page ending in `-township` is the township.
4. **Never call a village a city**, or its trustees a city council. Check the
   municipality type.
5. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
6. Do not call Illinois primaries "open" or "closed". Say voters choose a party
   ballot at each primary, without registering by party.
7. Do not say local elections here are nonpartisan, or partisan, as a rule.
   County races are partisan, school boards are nonpartisan, and towns vary.
8. Do not state a term length or seat count as fixed for a town. Both can be
   changed by local referendum.
9. Do not say a place became home rule by adopting a charter. Illinois home
   rule comes from population or a referendum.
10. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
    contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.
