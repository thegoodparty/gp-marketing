# Missouri: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Missouri so you do not have to research the same
ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which
offices are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race
data, which always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean.

## How elections work in Missouri

Safe on any Missouri page, at any tier.

### Election days

City, town, village and other local officers are elected on general municipal
election day, the first Tuesday after the first Monday in April. This happens
**every year**, not only in odd years, because most local terms are staggered
two-year terms, so part of a board stands each spring.

*§ 115.121.3 RSMo · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** a city with its own charter may set a different day (§ 115.123
RSMo), and about 40 Missouri cities have charters. Phrase this as how Missouri
works rather than as a fact about the specific city, unless race data gives you
the real date.

County and state offices are nominated at the primary, the first Tuesday after
the first Monday in August of even-numbered years, and elected at the general
election, the first Tuesday after the first Monday in November of even-numbered
years.

*§§ 115.121.1, 115.121.2 RSMo · statutory · read in statute*

### Primary type

Open. A voter picks one party's ballot at the polls and does not have to be
registered with that party. A voter who wants no party ballot can still vote on
ballot questions and nonpartisan races.

*§ 115.397 RSMo · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** since 2022 the registration form has an *optional* party
affiliation field and voters default to unaffiliated (§ 115.155 RSMo). This
makes search results claim Missouri closed its primaries. It did not. Those
results describe bills that never passed.

### Partisanship

County elections in Missouri are **partisan**. Candidates file on a party ticket,
are nominated at the August primary, and appear with a party label in November.

*§§ 115.339, 115.341, 115.349 RSMo · statutory · read in statute*

Municipal offices are not nominated through the state party primary, and in
practice city elections are nonpartisan.

*usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not say "nonpartisan" as a flat statement of law. Charter
cities can provide otherwise, and third and fourth class cities may hold their
own primaries by ordinance. "Local races here are typically nonpartisan" is
safe; "Missouri city elections are nonpartisan" is not.

## County government

114 counties, plus St. Louis City, which is not in any county.

Most Missouri counties are run by a three-member **county commission**: a
presiding commissioner elected countywide, and two commissioners each elected
from one of the county's two districts. All serve four-year terms. Counties also
elect a clerk, assessor, collector, treasurer, recorder, sheriff, coroner and
prosecuting attorney, all partisan.

*§§ 49.010, 49.020 RSMo; Mo. Const. art. VI, § 7 · usual case · read in statute*

### Charter counties

A county over 85,000 people may adopt its own charter. A charter county sits
outside the ordinary class system with whatever government its charter sets up.
These are the state's big suburban counties and they do **not** have a
three-member commission. See the metro exceptions below.

*Mo. Const. art. VI, § 18(a) · statutory · read in statute*

### Township counties

Some smaller counties have adopted "township organization", which adds elected
township trustees, clerks and board members alongside the county commission. If
race data shows township offices on a county's ballot, this is why.

*§§ 65.010, 65.110 RSMo · usual case · read in statute*

## City, town and village government

Missouri sorts municipalities into a small number of classes. Roughly 930
municipalities statewide: about 60% fourth class, about 27% villages, then third
class, home rule charter, and seven special charter cities.

### Village

Under about 500 people. Governed by a **board of trustees**, normally five
members, or nine in a village over 2,500. Trustees serve two-year terms.

A village has no mayor and no aldermen, so use "board of trustees" and not city
vocabulary.

*§ 80.040 RSMo · usual case · read in statute*

### Fourth class city

Roughly 500 to 3,000 people, and this is the most common class. Elects a mayor
and a **board of aldermen**. The city is divided into at least two wards with two
aldermen from each, so the smallest board is four. A city of 1,000 or fewer may
instead elect aldermen at large. Many also elect a marshal or collector, or
appoint them instead after a vote.

*§§ 79.050, 79.060 RSMo · usual case · read in statute*

### Third class city

Roughly 3,000 to 30,000 people. Elects a mayor and a **city council** by ward,
with at least four wards and normally two council members per ward. Some third
class cities instead run a council-manager or commission form, and many employ a
city administrator.

*§§ 77.030, 77.040 RSMo; ch. 78 RSMo · usual case · read in statute*

### Constitutional charter city (home rule)

Over 5,000 people, and about 40 cities have done this. Elects whatever the city's
own charter sets up. There is no statutory default, so council size, whether
seats are by district or at large, and which offices are elected are all local
choices.

*Mo. Const. art. VI, §§ 19, 19(a) · statutory · read in statute*

### Special charter city

Seven cities only, all chartered before 1875: Augusta, Carrollton, Chillicothe,
La Grange, Liberty, Miami and Pleasant Hill. Each is governed as set out in its
own charter.

*statutory · Secretary of State publication*

### Class does not follow population

A Missouri city keeps its class when it grows. Ballwin has over 31,000 residents
and is still a fourth class city. Never work out a city's class from its
population, and never say which class a city is unless we have been told.

*statutory · read in statute*

## Metro and major-place exceptions

These are the places where the general rules above are wrong. This is the most
useful section for high-traffic pages.

### St. Louis City

`mo/st-louis-city`

St. Louis is an **independent city**. It is both a city and a county, and it
belongs to **no** county. There is no county commission above it and no county
government separate from the city. It runs under its own charter and elects a
mayor, a Board of Aldermen, a comptroller and the usual county-type offices such
as sheriff, collector and recorder.

*Mo. Const. art. VI, § 31 · statutory · read in statute*

**Never** write about a county government sitting above St. Louis City, and never
confuse it with St. Louis County, which is a separate place with its own
government and 89 municipalities inside it.

### St. Louis County

`mo/st-louis-county`

A charter county, not a commission county. Led by a county executive and a county
council rather than a presiding commissioner and two commissioners. It is the
most municipality-dense county in the state, containing 89 of our Missouri city
pages, so county-level copy here should not imply it governs those cities
directly.

*statutory · Secretary of State publication*

### Jackson County

`mo/jackson-county`

A charter county led by a county executive and a county legislature, not a
three-member commission. Contains Kansas City and Independence.

*statutory · Secretary of State publication*

### Clay County and St. Charles County

`mo/clay-county`, `mo/st-charles-county`

Both charter counties. The Secretary of State's manual lists Clay's
classification as "Charter" and St. Charles as "first class charter".

*statutory · Secretary of State publication*

### Kansas City

`mo/jackson-county/kansas-city`

A home rule charter city, so its structure is set locally: a mayor and a City
Council with members elected both by district and at large. It physically spans
Cass, Clay, Jackson and Platte counties, although our page files it under Jackson
County. Avoid phrasing that ties it to a single county.

*statutory · Secretary of State publication*

### Other home rule cities

Springfield, Columbia, Independence, Lee's Summit, O'Fallon, St. Joseph and
Florissant are all home rule charter cities. Each sets its own council structure,
whether it uses a city manager, and which offices are elected. Do not apply the
third or fourth class templates to any of them.

*statutory · Secretary of State publication*

Note: our Lee's Summit page is filed under Washington County, which is wrong.
Lee's Summit is in Jackson County. That is a data issue, not something copy
should work around.

### Any county with a county executive

If race data shows a county executive, county council or county legislature, that
county is a charter county and the three-member commission description does not
apply. Let the race data override this file.

## Writing rules

These are the ways Missouri copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never infer a county from a place name.** 30 Missouri cities share a name
   with a county and 20 of them sit in a *different* county. Butler is in Bates
   County. Jackson is in Cape Girardeau County. Clinton is in Henry County.
   Oregon is in Holt County, and Holt is in Clay County. Always take the county
   from the page data.
2. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
3. Do not label a city's form of government (mayor-council, council-manager,
   commission). Missouri does not publish which form a city uses. Describe the
   offices on the ballot instead.
4. Do not state a term length as fixed. Missouri cities can extend two-year terms
   to four by a vote of the people.
5. Do not state which class a city is unless it is in our data, and never guess
   it from population.
6. Do not give a county's classification number. The thresholds are now indexed
   to inflation annually and a county's class can change between copy
   regenerations.
7. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
   contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.
