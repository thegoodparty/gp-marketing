# Montana: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Montana so you do not have to research the same ground
on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which offices are
actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race data, which
always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean. Every claim below has a verbatim quote
in [`research/montana.json`](./research/montana.json).

**The one thing to know first:** Montana runs three election calendars. County
offices are elected in November of even-numbered years, with the statewide primary
in June. City and town offices are elected in November of odd-numbered years, with a
September primary only when one is needed. School boards and most special districts
vote every year in May. And no statewide rule says whether a local race is partisan:
each county, city and town has a form of government, adopted or kept by its own
voters, that settles it. More than half of our Montana pages are school districts,
so the May calendar is the right one for most pages.

## Legislative calendar

Next regular session: convenes 4 January 2027 (noon on the first Monday of January
in odd-numbered years, or the first Wednesday when 1 January is a Monday, MCA
5-2-103). The Legislature meets in regular session only in odd-numbered years, for
no more than 90 legislative days (Mont. Const. art. V, § 6). There is no regular
session in 2028. The governor or a majority of legislators can call a special
session. A bill the governor neither signs nor vetoes within 10 days after it is
delivered becomes law (Mont. Const. art. VI, § 10(1)), so the session's last bills
are settled a few weeks after it adjourns.

New laws usually take effect on 1 October after passage, appropriations on 1 July,
and special session laws on passage, unless the act sets another date
(MCA 1-2-201).

## How elections work in Montana

Safe on any Montana page, at the tier named.

### Election days

**County** offices are on the even-year cycle: the statewide primary on the first
Tuesday after the first Monday in June, and the general election on the first
Tuesday after the first Monday in November.

*MCA 13-1-104(1), (2)(b), 13-1-107(1) · statutory · read in statute*

**Cities and towns** elect their officers on general election day in
odd-numbered years. The odd-year primary, held only if required, is the Tuesday
after the second Monday in September.

*MCA 13-1-104(3)(a), 13-1-107(2) · statutory · read in statute*

Any county, city, town or consolidated government election must fall on the
statewide primary or general election day, except an election about funding. A
local government cannot pick its own election day.

*MCA 13-1-402(2), 13-1-405(1) · statutory · read in statute*

**School boards** are elected every year on the regular school election day, the
first Tuesday after the first Monday in May. Trustees usually serve three-year
terms, staggered so that a majority of a board is never up at once.

*MCA 20-20-105(1), 20-3-301(1)-(2), 20-3-302(1) · statutory · read in statute*

**Special purpose districts** (fire, water and sewer, irrigation, hospital and
similar boards) fill their offices on the same May school election day.
Conservation districts are the exception and vote on a June primary or November
general election day. A few districts may elect at an annual meeting where a law
specifically allows it.

*MCA 13-1-504(1)-(3) · statutory · read in statute*

**Uncontested races are often not on the ballot.** When candidates do not
outnumber seats, a county, city, school board or special district may cancel the
election and declare the candidates elected by acclamation. So a seat with one
candidate may never appear on a ballot.

*MCA 13-1-403(4)-(5), 7-5-4410(1), 20-3-313(3) · statutory · read in statute*

**Local government review.** Every ten years each county, city and town must ask
its voters whether to review its form of government. If they say yes, a study
commission is elected at a November general election, on a nonpartisan ballot,
and no elected official of that government may serve on it. Some of our pages
carry "study commissioner" races for this reason.

*Mont. Const. art. XI, § 9(2); MCA 7-3-174, 7-3-176(2)-(3) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** Montana school board elections are in May, not November, and they
happen every year. Never put a Montana city election in an even year or a county
election in an odd year.

### Primary type

Montana's party primaries are open. Each voter receives every qualified party's
ballot, plus the nonpartisan and ballot issue choices, and votes only one party's
ballot. A candidate may not seek more than one party's nomination. Parties whose
statewide candidates reached 5% of the vote for governor nominate by primary.

*MCA 13-10-209(1), (7), 13-10-301(2), 13-10-201(5), 13-10-601(1) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not call Montana primaries closed, and do not say a voter must
belong to a party to vote in one. Do not say the voter's choice of party ballot
is recorded as a registration either; the statutes read for this doc say nothing
about that, so leave it out.

### Partisanship

There is no statewide rule. Each county, city and town runs its own elections
**on a partisan or nonpartisan basis as set by its form of government**, and every
local government has adopted one of the forms in state law or a charter. Each
statutory form offers both options, and a charter must say which one applies.

*MCA 7-3-102, 7-3-219, 7-3-314, 7-3-413, 7-3-704(1) · varies by place · read in statute*

Defaults for governments that kept their old statutory form:

- **Counties under the traditional elected county official form** may switch
  between partisan and nonpartisan elections by a vote of residents, so
  never assume either for a given county.
- **Cities under the old council-mayor form** are tied to the partisan option.
- **Cities under the commission-manager form, and counties under the county
  manager form,** are tied to the nonpartisan option.

*MCA 7-3-103(4), 7-3-113(1)(k), 7-3-114(1)(h), 7-3-112(1)(g) · varies by place · read in statute*

These are only defaults. Any city or county may have voted in a new form or a
charter since, so never call a Montana city or county race partisan or
nonpartisan unless race data says which.

**Always nonpartisan:**

- **Justices of the peace**, on the nonpartisan judicial ballot.
- **School trustees.** Their ballot lists candidate names only, and nonpartisan
  candidates may not state a party.
- **Local government study commissions.**

*MCA 3-10-201(2), 20-3-306(2), 13-14-112(2), 7-3-176(2) · statutory · read in statute*

A local nonpartisan race goes to a primary only when it is crowded (roughly, more
than three candidates per seat). Otherwise every candidate goes straight to the
general election, unless the governing body requires a primary anyway.

*MCA 13-14-115(2)-(3) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** "local races in Montana are nonpartisan" is wrong. It is right for
school boards and justices of the peace, and wrong or unknown for any one county,
city or town.

### Winning an election

Plurality: the candidate with the most valid votes is elected or nominated. There
is no runoff. Ranked-choice voting is banned for local, state and federal offices.
School elections follow the same general election rules.

*MCA 13-1-103, 13-1-125(1), 20-20-102 · statutory · read in statute*

### Running as an independent

For a **partisan** race, an independent files a declaration, pays the filing fee
and circulates a nomination petition. Signers must be active electors living in
the district or political subdivision of the office, and each petition sheet may
carry signers from only one county. An independent may not have run as a
partisan candidate or held office with a party label in Montana during the year
before filing. A candidate who loses a primary may not run for the same office as
an independent or write-in that year.

*MCA 13-10-501(1), (5)-(6), 13-10-502(1), 13-10-507(1)-(2) · statutory · read in statute*

For a **nonpartisan** race (school board, justice of the peace, and any city or
county that votes nonpartisan), there is no independent route because there are
no party nominees: every candidate files the same way, with no party.

*MCA 13-10-505, 13-14-112(2) · statutory · read in statute*

## County government

Montana's counties are fixed by the constitution, and our data has 56 county
pages. Two of them are consolidated city-county governments (see the exceptions
below). Montana has no townships in our data; do not describe a township layer.

*Mont. Const. art. XI, § 2 · statutory · read in statute*

The traditional form, which the constitution names, elects
a **board of three county commissioners**. Each commissioner must live in a
commissioner district, but the whole county votes on every seat unless the
county's plan of government or a court order says otherwise. Commissioners serve
six-year terms.

*Mont. Const. art. XI, § 3(2); MCA 7-3-111(2), 7-4-2101, 7-4-2104(1), 7-4-2105(1) · usual case · read in statute*

A county may adopt another form by vote, with a commission of a different size,
different terms and different election methods. Under the commission form,
elected **community councils** may advise each commissioner; our Fergus County
page has such a race.

*Mont. Const. art. XI, § 3(1); MCA 7-3-417 · varies by place · read in statute*

Counties may elect or appoint a **county attorney, clerk of district court, county
clerk, sheriff, treasurer, superintendent of schools,
surveyor, assessor, coroner, public administrator** and at least one **justice of
the peace**. Elected county officers serve four-year terms.

*MCA 7-4-2203(1), 7-4-2205(1) · usual case · read in statute*

**Combined offices are normal in Montana.** County commissioners may consolidate
any two or more county offices (except justice of the peace), and counties may
share a joint office. Many of our county pages show joint offices such as
"sheriff and coroner" or "clerk and recorder and superintendent of schools". Take
each county's offices from race data, and never list a standard set of separate
officers.

*Mont. Const. art. XI, § 3(2); MCA 7-4-2301 · varies by place · read in statute*

A county superintendent of schools is elected in every county that has not
adopted a county manager form.

*MCA 20-3-201(1) · usual case · read in statute*

### Home rule and charters

Any Montana county, city or town may adopt a **self-government charter** by a vote
of its residents. There is no population threshold. A charter government may
exercise any power not prohibited by law, and charter provisions on its structure
(the size of the governing body, terms, districts, which offices are elected)
override statutes.

*Mont. Const. art. XI, §§ 5(1), (3), 6 · statutory · read in statute*

**Does home rule reach elections?** A self-government unit stays subject to all
laws regulating the election of local officials, so election days, plurality and
nomination rules hold everywhere. But the statutes themselves leave partisanship
to each government's form or charter, and a charter sets the structure. So timing
is flat-assertable for every county, city and town; partisanship and offices are
not.

*MCA 7-1-114(1)(d), (2), 7-3-704(1) · statutory · read in statute*

## City and town government

Montana has four classes, set by population. Places of 1,000 or more are
**cities**; smaller ones are **towns**. There are no villages or boroughs.

*MCA 7-1-4111 · statutory · read in statute*

- **First class city:** 10,000 or more.
- **Second class city:** 5,001 to 9,999.
- **Third class city:** 1,001 to 4,999.
- **Town:** 301 to 999. A new municipality needs at least 300 inhabitants.

*MCA 7-1-4111(1)-(4), 7-2-4103(1)(a) · statutory · read in statute*

**Class mostly follows population.** After a census the council must move the
place up a class when it qualifies, and must move a first or second class city
down when it falls short. Places in overlap bands (for example 1,001 to 2,499)
may choose between two classes. Never infer a place's class from a population figure.

*MCA 7-1-4112, 7-1-4114, 7-1-4118(1) · statutory · read in statute*

### What cities and towns elect

The statutory default is the same in every class: a **mayor, two council
members per ward, and a city judge**. Mayors and council members serve four-year
terms, with one council seat per ward up at each election. Towns and third class
cities may make the city judge appointed instead of elected, and city judges
serve four years.

*MCA 7-4-4101(1), 7-4-4102(3), 7-4-4103(2), 7-4-4302, 7-4-4402, 3-11-201(2) · usual case · read in statute*

Ward counts depend on class: first class cities have 4 to 10 wards, and towns two
or three (a town may go down to one).

*MCA 7-5-4401(2) · usual case · read in statute*

These defaults rarely hold as written. Every Montana city and town has adopted
one of six forms (commission-executive, commission-manager, commission,
commission-presiding officer, town meeting, or charter), and each form lets the
place choose its council size, terms, at-large or ward elections, and whether
the mayor is elected. The old council-mayor form alone allows a council of up to
20. Take each place's offices and terms from race data.

*MCA 7-3-102, 7-3-113(2), 7-3-201, 7-3-218 · varies by place · read in statute*

## Metro and major-place exceptions

### Butte-Silver Bow

`mt/silver-bow-county`, `mt/silver-bow-county/butte-silver-bow`

One consolidated city-county government, led by an elected **chief executive**
and a council of commissioners elected by district. A consolidated government has
the status of both a county and a city and replaces both. Do not describe a
separate city of Butte with a mayor and city council alongside a county
commission. Our data shows the same government twice (see data issues), so write
both pages as one government.

*MCA 7-3-1101(1), 7-3-1103(1); Butte-Silver Bow home page · statutory · local government publication*

### Anaconda-Deer Lodge County

`mt/deer-lodge-county`

Also a consolidated city-county government, headed by a **chief executive**
elected on a nonpartisan ballot for four years, by plurality. Our data has no
separate Anaconda city page, which fits.

*MCA 7-3-1103(1); Anaconda-Deer Lodge County chief executive page · varies by place · local government publication*

**Watch out:** the city of **Deer Lodge** (`mt/powell-county/deer-lodge`) is in
Powell County. It is not in Deer Lodge County and has nothing to do with this
consolidated government.

### Consolidated governments in general

A city or town joins a consolidation only if its own study commission takes part
and approves, so a small town inside a consolidated county can stay separate.
Do not write that a consolidated government covers every place in its county.

*MCA 7-3-1101(3) · statutory · read in statute*

### Big Sky

`mt/gallatin-county/big-sky`

Our only race here is a resort area district board, and our data has no city or
town offices for Big Sky. Do not describe a mayor, council or city government on
this page.

### The largest cities

Billings (`mt/yellowstone-county/billings`), Missoula
(`mt/missoula-county/missoula`), Great Falls (`mt/cascade-county/great-falls`),
Bozeman (`mt/gallatin-county/bozeman`), Helena
(`mt/lewis-and-clark-county/helena`) and Kalispell
(`mt/flathead-county/kalispell`) each run under their own adopted form or
charter. Their council size, terms, wards and partisanship were not read for this
doc. Take all of it from race data.

### School district pages

229 of our 401 Montana location pages are school districts: 126 elementary, 43
high school, 59 K-12 and one other. Montana keeps elementary and high school
districts separate even in the same town, and a high school district's board is
usually the elementary board where the high school stands, plus extra trustees
for the outlying area. A K-12 district is an elementary district attached to a
high school district. Districts ignore county lines.

*MCA 20-6-101(1)-(2), 20-3-351(1) · statutory · read in statute*

A first class elementary district has seven trustees and a third class one has
three (or five by board vote). A county high school has seven. Boards may also
create single-member trustee districts.

*MCA 20-3-341(1), (3), 20-3-351(2), 20-3-337(1) · usual case · read in statute*

## Writing rules

These are the ways Montana copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never infer a county from a place name.** Two of our Montana place pages
   share a name with a different county: **Deer Lodge** is in Powell County, not
   Deer Lodge County, and **Sheridan** is in Madison County, not Sheridan County.
   The full list is in
   [`research/places-named-after-another-county.json`](./research/places-named-after-another-county.json).
   Always take the county from the page data.
2. **Use the right calendar for the tier.** Counties: November of even years,
   June primary. Cities and towns: November of odd years. School boards and most
   special districts: May, every year.
3. **Do not call a county, city or town race partisan or nonpartisan without race
   data.** Montana has no statewide rule. School board and justice of the peace
   races are always nonpartisan.
4. Do not call Montana primaries closed, or say a voter must join a party.
5. Do not mention runoffs or ranked-choice voting. Plurality wins.
6. **Do not list a standard set of county officers.** Many counties combine
   offices, and some have changed their commission. Take the offices from race
   data.
7. Butte-Silver Bow and Anaconda-Deer Lodge are each one government with a chief
   executive. Never describe a separate mayor and county commission there.
8. **An elementary district and a high school district with the same town name
   are separate districts** with overlapping boards. Do not merge them or treat
   one as part of the other.
9. Do not infer a place's class from its population, and do not call a Montana
   place a village or borough.
10. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
11. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
    contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.
