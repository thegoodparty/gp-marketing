# North Carolina: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across North Carolina so you do not have to research the same
ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which offices
are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race data, which
always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean. Every claim below has a verbatim quote
in [`research/north-carolina.json`](./research/north-carolina.json).

**The one thing to know first:** North Carolina runs two election calendars and
two kinds of local race. County offices are partisan and elected in November of
even-numbered years, after a March party primary that can go to a second primary.
Cities, towns and villages vote in their own municipal elections, usually in odd
years, and each one picks one of four election methods in its charter, only one
of them partisan. School boards are nonpartisan by default, but a
large and growing number of counties have made theirs partisan by local act.
There is no home rule: almost every local exception is a law the General Assembly
passed for that one place.

## Legislative calendar

Next regular session: convenes 13 January 2027 (the second Wednesday in January
after the election, N.C. Gen. Stat. § 120-11.1), meeting that day only to organize,
then reconvenes 27 January 2027 (the third Wednesday after the second Monday in
January, § 120-11.1). The Constitution has the General Assembly meet every two
years on the day set by law (N.C. Const. art. II, § 11(1)). The session has no
fixed end date. The governor has 10 days to act on a bill presented during the
session; after the General Assembly adjourns sine die or for more than 30 days,
the governor has 30 days to return a bill with a veto (art. II, § 22(7)). Bills
that apply in fewer than 15 counties, which includes most bills about one city,
county or school board, never go to the governor at all (art. II, § 22(6)). New
laws take effect 60 days after the session adjourns unless the act sets its own
date (N.C. Gen. Stat. § 120-20), and most acts do set one.

## How elections work in North Carolina

Safe on any North Carolina page, at the tier named.

### Election days

**County offices** are elected at the November general election in even-numbered
years, the same election as the General Assembly. The party primary is held on
the Tuesday after the first Monday in March of that year.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. §§ 163-1(a)-(b), 153A-62 · statutory · read in statute*

**Cities, towns, villages and special districts** hold their elections every two
or four years, as each charter provides, counting from 1973. That puts the default
municipal election in odd-numbered years. The day depends on the city's election
method (below), but the final election is in November except under the
election-and-runoff method, where the first round is four Tuesdays earlier and a
runoff, if any, is in November.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. § 163-279(a) · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** some cities have moved to even years by local act. Asheville votes
in even years since 2020. High Point moved to even years in 2006 and back to odd
years from 2017. A city with districts can also push one election into the
following even year after a census if its new districts are not ready. Never state
a city's election year unless race data gives the date.

*S.L. 2018-123; S.L. 2006-171, 2013-261; N.C. Gen. Stat. § 160A-23.1 · varies by place · General Assembly charter amendment table, read in statute*

**School boards** elected under the general law are chosen at the time of the
March primary in even-numbered years, on a nonpartisan ballot, with about half the
seats up every two years. Many counties have changed this by local act: the
timing, the board's size and terms, the party label, or all of them.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. § 115C-37(a) · usual case · read in statute*

**Soil and water conservation district** supervisors (three elected in each county)
are chosen at the November county election, on a nonpartisan ballot with no
primary.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. § 139-6 · statutory · read in statute*

**Can a place move its own election date?** A city can change its election method,
council terms and structure by ordinance (below), but the year it votes is set in
its charter, which is mostly a set of local acts. Counties have no power of
their own over when they vote. In practice most changes to when or how a place votes come from
a local act of the General Assembly, which the governor cannot veto.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. §§ 160A-1(1), 160A-102, 163-290(b); N.C. Const. art. II, § 22(6), art. VII, § 1 · statutory · read in statute*

### Primary type

Party primaries are semi-closed. A voter registered with a party votes in that
party's primary. An unaffiliated voter chooses one party's primary ballot at the
polls. No one may vote in more than one party's primary.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. §§ 163-59, 163-119, 163-166.7(b) · statutory · read in statute*

**The second primary.** A primary candidate needs more than 30 percent of the vote
(the law calls this a "substantial plurality"). If no one gets there, the leader is
nominated anyway unless the second-place candidate asks for a second primary. The
second primary is held 10 weeks after the first, between the top two, and there is
never a third. Runner-ups for county office ask the county board of elections.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. § 163-111(a), (b), (c)(2), (e), (f) · statutory · read in statute*

Partisan city elections follow the same rules, with the city primary on the second
Tuesday after Labor Day and any second primary four Tuesdays before the November
election.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. §§ 163-279(a)(2), 163-291 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not call the second primary a runoff of the general election.
It is a second round of a party primary and only happens if the runner-up asks
for it.

### Partisanship

**County races are partisan.** County commissioners, sheriff, clerk of superior
court, register of deeds and the other county offices are nominated in party
primaries.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. §§ 163-1(b), 163-106(a) · statutory · read in statute*

**City, town and village races depend on the charter.** Every municipality uses
one of four methods, and only the first is partisan:

1. **Partisan primary and election**: party primaries in the fall, a second
   primary if needed, then a November election with party labels.
2. **Nonpartisan primary and election**: a nonpartisan primary narrows the field
   to two candidates per seat, then the November election.
3. **Nonpartisan plurality**: one November election, most votes wins.
4. **Nonpartisan election and runoff**: a first round needing a majority, and a
   November runoff between the top two if the runner-up asks for it.

A city changes method by charter amendment, which the council can make by
ordinance, or by local act. Which method a given city uses is not something this
doc can tell you.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. §§ 163-290, 163-291, 163-292, 163-293, 163-294, 160A-101(7), 160A-102 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not write that a city's elections are nonpartisan, or that they
use a runoff or a primary, unless race data shows it. Candidates' party labels in
race data are the tell. Raleigh, for example, adopted the nonpartisan primary method
and four-year staggered terms by ordinance in 2024.

*Raleigh Ord. No. 627 (2024) · varies by place · General Assembly charter amendment table*

**School boards are nonpartisan by default and partisan in many counties.** The
general law prints school board candidates with no party. But local acts have made
many county boards partisan: a statute on filling school board vacancies lists more
than 40 counties whose boards are elected on a partisan basis, and the list grows
most sessions (Pitt County joins in December 2026, Currituck and Moore in December
2028). City school boards (Asheboro, Hickory and others) are appointed or elected
as their own local laws say.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. §§ 115C-37(a), 115C-37(c), 115C-37.1(a), (d) · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never call a North Carolina school board race nonpartisan or partisan
from this doc. Take it from race data.

**Soil and water supervisors** are always nonpartisan.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. § 139-6 · statutory · read in statute*

### Winning an election

At the general election, the candidate with the most votes wins. There is no
general election runoff. Majority rules apply only in party primaries (the 30
percent threshold above) and in cities that use the election-and-runoff method.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. §§ 163-182.15, 163-292, 163-293(a)-(b) · statutory · read in statute*

### Running as an independent

North Carolina's word is **unaffiliated**. An unaffiliated candidate for county
office gets on the November ballot by filing petitions with the county board of
elections, signed by registered voters of the county (or of the district, for a
district seat). In a partisan city the same petition route applies. Anyone whose
name was on a primary ballot cannot then run as unaffiliated for the same office
that year, and every party candidate signs a pledge not to run as a write-in if
they lose the primary.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. §§ 163-122(a)(3), 163-122(b), 163-296, 163-106(a) · statutory · read in statute*

In a nonpartisan city there is no party route and no petition: every candidate
files a notice of candidacy and must be registered to vote in the municipality.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. § 163-294.2(b) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** in North Carolina "independent" is not a ballot label. Say
"unaffiliated candidate" when describing how someone gets on a county ballot.

### Recent changes to election administration (2023-2024)

The General Assembly rewrote how elections are run in 2023 and 2024. As the law now
reads:

- The **State Auditor** appoints the five-member State Board of Elections from
  lists submitted by the two largest parties, and no more than three members may
  share a party.
- Each **county board of elections** has five members: four appointed by the State
  Board, two from each of the two largest parties, and a chair appointed by the
  State Auditor.
- Mailed **absentee ballots** must reach the county board by 7:30 p.m. on election
  day.
- Voters who vote in person show **photo ID**. A constitutional amendment on photo
  ID is on the 3 November 2026 ballot.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. §§ 163-19(b), 163-30(a), 163-231(b)(1), 163-166.16(a); N.C. Const. art. VI, § 2 note · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not characterize these changes or their politics. If copy needs it at
all, describe what the law says now, and nothing about voting procedures that might
change after the November 2026 amendment vote.

## County government

Our data has a page for each of North Carolina's 100 counties. There are no county
charters and no county home rule: the General Assembly organizes every county, and
changes for one county come by local act.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. § 153A-10; N.C. Const. art. VII, § 1 · statutory · read in statute*

Every county is governed by a **board of commissioners**. Its size, terms and
districts are whatever they were in 1974 unless changed since, so they differ
county to county:

- at least three members, with no upper limit,
- two-year, four-year or overlapping four-year terms (the general law's default
  is two years),
- elected at large, from districts, or a mix, with no more than half at large.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. §§ 153A-34, 153A-58(1)-(3), 163-1(a) · varies by place · read in statute*

The board normally picks its own **chair** each year. A county can instead make the
chair a separate office elected countywide; our data shows this in Buncombe,
Jackson and Swain counties. A county changes its board's structure by resolution
and a countywide referendum.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. §§ 153A-58(4), 153A-60(4) · varies by place · read in statute*

Every county also elects:

- a **sheriff**, for four years,
- a **clerk of superior court**, for four years (an officer of the state court
  system, elected by county),
- a **register of deeds**,
- three **soil and water conservation district supervisors**, nonpartisan.

*N.C. Const. art. VII, § 2; art. IV, § 9(3); N.C. Gen. Stat. §§ 161-1, 139-6 · statutory · read in statute*

The general law also provides an elected **coroner** in each county, but our data
shows a coroner race only in Avery and Yadkin counties. Take it from race data.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. § 152-1 · usual case · read in statute*

**Consolidation.** The Constitution allows merged city-county governments, but a
consolidation needs an act of the General Assembly, and our data has none. A county
with no municipality inside it holds a city's powers as a "unified government";
Camden County has no municipal pages in our data.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. §§ 153A-405(d), 153A-471(a) · statutory · read in statute*

### Townships

North Carolina townships are **not governments**. They are lines a county draws by
resolution, used for things like precincts and reported to the Census Bureau. They
have no elected officers of their own.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. § 153A-19(a), (c) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** our data has 20 township pages (Gaston, Pamlico, Polk, Hyde,
Currituck, Cumberland and Wayne counties). They hold school board seats elected
from township-based districts, or sanitary district boards. Copy for those pages
must describe the school board seat or the district, never a township government,
and never a township trustee or mayor.

## City, town and village government

North Carolina law has one kind of municipality. "City", "town" and "village" are
interchangeable labels for the same thing, and so are "council", "board of
aldermen" and "board of commissioners" for its governing board. A place's style is
a choice it makes, not a class. There are no population classes.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. §§ 160A-1(2), 160A-1(3), 160A-101(2) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not infer size or powers from "town" or "village" in a name. Several places carry "City" in their name (Elizabeth
City, Morehead City, Siler City, Bryson City); that is the name, not a class.

### What a municipality elects

The default, where a charter says nothing, is a **mayor** and a **three-member
council** elected at large for two-year terms. Almost every charter changes this.
Within the law's options:

- the council has 3 to 12 members, serving two- or four-year terms (not all terms
  need be the same length or end in the same year),
- members are elected at large, from districts, or a mix, with at most half at
  large,
- the mayor is elected by the voters for two to four years, or chosen by the
  council from its own members.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. §§ 160A-66, 160A-101(4)-(6), (8) · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** if a municipal page has no mayor race, the council may choose the
mayor. Do not write that voters elect a mayor unless race data has one.

### Changing the charter

There is no home rule threshold. A council can adopt any of the optional forms
above (name, style, council size, terms, districts, election method, how the mayor
is chosen) by ordinance after a public hearing. A referendum is needed only if the
council calls one or voters petition for one. Voters can also start a charter amendment by initiative petition.
Anything outside those options, including the year a city votes, takes a local act.

*N.C. Gen. Stat. §§ 160A-101, 160A-102, 160A-104, 160A-1(5) · statutory · read in statute*

### School districts

Most of our 115 district pages are county school systems (`nc/wake-county-schools`,
`nc/charlotte-mecklenburg-schools`). Some are city systems
(`nc/asheville-city-schools`, `nc/hickory-city-schools`) or merged city-county
systems (`nc/alamance-burlington-schools`, `nc/rowan-salisbury-schools`). Size,
terms, timing and partisanship all vary by local act.

### Cities across county lines

Each of our pages files a city under one county, even where it extends into
others. Do not write that a city lies wholly within the
county on its page.

## Writing rules

These are the ways North Carolina copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never infer a county from a place name.** 16 of our North Carolina municipal
   pages share a name with a different North Carolina county. Davidson is in
   Mecklenburg County, Henderson in Vance County, Graham in Alamance County, Lenoir
   in Caldwell County, Washington in Beaufort County, Beaufort in Carteret County,
   and Gaston and Jackson in Northampton County. The full list is in
   [`research/places-named-after-another-county.json`](./research/places-named-after-another-county.json).
   Always take the county from the page data.
2. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
3. **Do not call city or school board races nonpartisan, or partisan,** unless
   race data shows it. County races are partisan; soil and water supervisors are
   not.
4. **Do not state the year a city votes** unless race data gives the date. Odd
   years are only the default.
5. **A township is not a government.** On a township page, describe the school
   board seat or district on it.
6. **Do not treat city, town and village as classes,** or infer size or powers
   from the word.
7. Say "unaffiliated", not "independent", for candidates who petition onto a
   county or partisan city ballot.
8. The second primary is part of a party primary, not a general election runoff,
   and happens only on request.
9. Do not describe the politics of the 2023-2024 election board changes.
10. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
    contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.
