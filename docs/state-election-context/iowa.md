# Iowa: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across Iowa so you do not have to research the same ground
on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which offices are
actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race data, which
always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean. Every claim below has a verbatim quote
in [`research/iowa.json`](./research/iowa.json).

**The one thing to know first:** Iowa runs two local election calendars.
Cities, school boards and community college boards vote together in November
of **odd-numbered** years, without party labels. Counties and townships vote at
the November general election in **even-numbered** years: county offices are
partisan, township offices are not. Iowa has exactly one class of municipality,
the "city", so there are no villages, towns or boroughs to sort out.

## Legislative calendar

Next regular session: convenes 11 January 2027 (the second Monday in January,
every year, Iowa Code § 2.1; Iowa Const. art. III, § 2). There is no fixed end
date, but lawmakers' daily expense pay stops after 110 days in the first year of
a General Assembly and 100 days in the second, which in practice ends the
session around late April or May (Iowa Code § 2.10, `usual case`).

The governor has three days, Sundays excepted, to act on a bill during the
session. A bill presented in the last three days of the session is acted on
within thirty days after adjournment (Iowa Const. art. III, § 16). Acts of a
regular session usually take effect on 1 July following passage (Iowa Code
§ 3.7).

## How elections work in Iowa

Safe on any Iowa page, at the tier named.

### Election days

**County and township offices** are filled at the general election, the first
Tuesday after the first Monday in November of each **even-numbered** year. So are
the county-level nonpartisan boards: county public hospital trustees, soil and
water conservation district commissioners and county agricultural extension
council members.

*Iowa Code §§ 39.1, 39.3(7), 39.21 · statutory · read in statute*

**Cities** hold their regular election on the first Tuesday after the first
Monday in November of each **odd-numbered** year. State law fixes this for every
city, and no charter or ordinance can move it.

*Iowa Code § 376.1(1) · statutory · read in statute*

**School boards and community college boards** are elected at the regular school
election, on the same day as the city election: the first Tuesday after the
first Monday in November of each odd-numbered year. Directors serve four-year
terms.

*Iowa Code §§ 277.1, 39.24, 274.7 · statutory · read in statute*

A school board may be elected at large, from director districts, or a mix,
depending on the plan the district adopted. A community college (merged area)
board is elected one member per director district.

*Iowa Code §§ 275.12(2), 260C.11(1) · varies by place · read in statute*

**Watch out:** area education agency (AEA) boards are not elected by voters.
School board members elect five directors and school superintendents appoint the
other four. Never describe an AEA board as a race a resident can run in or vote
in.

*Iowa Code § 273.8 · statutory · read in statute*

So a county page and a city page in the same county are almost never about the
same election year. Avoid putting county and city offices on one ballot, but let
our race data decide.

### Primary type

**Partisan offices only.** The party primary is held on the first Tuesday after
the first Monday in June of each even-numbered year. Supervisors and the elected
county officers are nominated there.

*Iowa Code §§ 43.6, 43.7 · statutory · read in statute*

A primary voter receives only the ballot of the party they are registered with.
But any registered voter may change or declare a party at the polls on primary
day, and the change is entered on their registration record, so it stays after
the election.

*Iowa Code §§ 43.38, 43.42 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not call Iowa a simple open primary. A voter who switches at
the polls stays switched. "Pick a party at the polls" is closer.

A county nominee needs at least 35 percent of their party's primary vote. If no
one reaches it, the nomination goes to a party convention.

*Iowa Code § 43.52(1)(b) · statutory · read in statute*

**City primaries are a different thing.** A city primary is nonpartisan and is
held only when more than twice as many candidates file for an office as there are
seats. It falls four weeks before the city election, and the top vote-getters, up
to twice the number of seats, go on to November.

*Iowa Code §§ 376.6(1), 376.7 · usual case · read in statute*

The Iowa caucuses elect party delegates and committee members, not local
officeholders.

*Iowa Code § 43.4(1) · statutory · read in statute*

### Partisanship

**County: partisan.** Supervisors, auditor, sheriff, treasurer, recorder and
county attorney are nominated in the June party primary and carry a party label
in November. A county that adopts an alternative form of government must still
elect its officers on a partisan basis.

*Iowa Code §§ 43.6, 39.17, 49.31(1)(a), 331.238(3) · statutory · read in statute*

**County-level boards: nonpartisan.** County public hospital trustees, soil and
water conservation district commissioners and county agricultural extension
council members are elected at the general election on a nonpartisan basis.

*Iowa Code §§ 39.21, 347.25, 161A.5 · statutory · read in statute*

**Township: nonpartisan.** Township trustees and the township clerk are elected
on ballots that do not show a nominee's party.

*Iowa Code § 39.22(2)(a) · statutory · read in statute*

**City: nonpartisan, with one narrow exception in law.** City candidates file by
petition, the published notice carries no party designations, and city and school
ballots list candidates by lot with no party heading.

*Iowa Code §§ 376.4, 376.5, 49.31(2)(d) · usual case · read in statute*

The exception: a city that held a special charter in 1973 and has more than
50,000 people holds **partisan** city elections unless its voters chose
nonpartisan elections, and it may switch back the same way. State law gives
such cities a mayor and **aldermen**.

*Iowa Code §§ 376.3, 45.1(10) · statutory · read in statute*

Davenport is the large city this most likely concerns: it elects a mayor and
aldermen, and its city ballot lists them with no party labels.

*Scott County sample ballot, City of Davenport, 2017 · varies by place · local government publication*

**Watch out:** no primary source listing Iowa's special charter cities was read
for this doc, and the Davenport ballot read is from 2017. On Davenport pages,
avoid saying its races are partisan or nonpartisan unless race data shows which.
Elsewhere, say Iowa city races carry no party label.

**School and community college boards: nonpartisan.** Every school board
candidate is nominated by petition and listed by lot.

*Iowa Code §§ 277.4(2)(a), 49.31(2)(d) · statutory · read in statute*

### Winning an election

**Plurality** is the default: the candidates with the most votes are declared
elected. That holds for county and township races, the soil and water boards,
and city races in cities that use a primary.

*Iowa Code §§ 50.45, 39.22(2)(a), 161A.5(3)(d), 376.8(1) · usual case · read in statute*

**Some cities use runoffs instead.** A city council may choose by ordinance to
replace the city primary with a runoff. In those cities a candidate needs a
majority to win in November, and a runoff is held four weeks later for any seat
nobody won outright.

*Iowa Code §§ 376.6(1)(a), 376.8(2), 376.9 · varies by place · read in statute*

**Ranked choice voting is banned.** Since 1 January 2026, no election in Iowa
may use ranked choice or instant runoff voting.

*Iowa Code § 49.93(2) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not write "no runoff" or "majority needed" on an Iowa city page.
It depends on the city's ordinance. Avoid the topic unless race data shows a
runoff.

### Running as an independent

**County offices.** A candidate who is not a party nominee gets on the general
election ballot by a nomination petition signed by eligible electors who live in
the county (or the supervisor district). A person already nominated another way,
such as in the party primary, cannot be added by petition for the same office.

*Iowa Code §§ 45.1(6), (7), 45.2 · statutory · read in statute*

Signers must live in the ward, city, county or district the candidate runs in. No
signer is barred for having signed another candidate's petition: a voter may sign
for more than one candidate for the same office.

*Iowa Code § 45.6(1), (3) · statutory · read in statute*

**City, school and township offices.** Every candidate files by petition, so
there is no separate independent path. For a ward seat, signers must live in the
ward.

*Iowa Code §§ 376.4(1), 277.4(2), 39.22(2)(a) · statutory · read in statute*

## County government

Iowa has **99 counties**, all with a county government; our data has a page for
each. There are no independent cities or unorganized areas.

*Our page data (sitemap shard 16, 4 November 2026)*

### Board of supervisors

Every Iowa county is governed by a **board of supervisors** with three members,
or five if county voters have approved the increase. Supervisors serve staggered
four-year terms, so some seats come up at each general election. Counties also
elect an **auditor**, **sheriff**, **treasurer**, **recorder** and **county
attorney**, each for four years.

*Iowa Code §§ 331.201, 331.203, 39.17, 39.18 · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not state a supervisor count for a named county. Say the county
is run by a board of supervisors and let race data supply the seats.

A county may also combine the duties of elected offices by petition and election.
Marshall County and Woodbury County have a combined **county auditor/recorder**
in our data. Take county offices from race data.

*Iowa Code § 331.323(1) · varies by place · read in statute*

### How supervisors are elected

Each county uses one of three plans: at large with no residence rule (plan one),
at large with each member living in a district (plan two), or single-member
districts (plan three). Which plan a county uses is not in our data.

*Iowa Code § 331.206(1) · varies by place · read in statute*

A county with the main campus of a state university must use single-member
districts. That is **Johnson County** (University of Iowa, Iowa City), **Story
County** (Iowa State University, Ames) and **Black Hawk County** (University of
Northern Iowa, Cedar Falls). The legislature set this rule in 2025, with a
transition at the 2026 general election.

*Iowa Code § 331.206(2)(a); Board of Regents institutions page · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** outside those three counties, never say whether supervisors are
elected at large or by district unless race data says so.

### Home rule and charters

The constitution gives counties home rule only where it is **not inconsistent
with state law**, so elections stay under state law at the county tier. State law
offers alternative forms (board-elected executive, board-manager, charter,
city-county consolidation, multicounty consolidation and community commonwealth),
but every alternative form must keep partisan elections for its officers. Our
data shows a board of supervisors in all 99 counties.

*Iowa Const. art. III, § 39A; Iowa Code §§ 331.231, 331.238 · statutory · read in statute*

### Townships

Iowa townships are a separate government with their own pages in our data (1,152
pages, slugs ending in `-township`). Each elects a board of **three township
trustees** (five in townships with a very high property valuation) and one
**township clerk**, all for four-year terms, at the even-year general election,
nonpartisan, by plurality. In a township that includes a city, only voters living
outside the city vote for township officers.

*Iowa Code §§ 39.22(2), 359.17(1) · statutory · read in statute*

Township voters may also switch the offices to appointment by the board of
supervisors, so a township may have no elected officers. Let race data say.

*Iowa Code § 39.22(1) · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** a township and a city often share a name and a county
(`ia/winneshiek-county/calmar` and `ia/winneshiek-county/calmar-township`). They
are different governments that vote in different years. Townships are not cities
under Iowa law, so never describe a township as a city, and keep the word
"Township" in its name.

*Iowa Code § 362.2(4) · statutory · read in statute*

## City government

"City" is the only class of Iowa municipality. Size does not change a city's
legal class, and our data has 939 city pages, from a few dozen residents to Des
Moines.

*Iowa Code § 362.2(4) · statutory · read in statute*

Every city has a **mayor** and a **council**. The council **appoints the city
clerk**, so the clerk is not on the ballot.

*Iowa Code § 372.13(3) · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** special charter cities keep their own rules on whether the clerk
and treasurer are elected, and a commission form city may elect its treasurer.
Four small cities in our data carry a city treasurer race. So say the clerk is
appointed, but do not say no city office besides mayor and council is ever
elected.

*Iowa Code §§ 420.41(1)(d), 372.5(5) · varies by place · read in statute*

### Forms of government

State law offers eight forms: mayor-council (with or without an appointed
manager), commission, council-manager-at-large, council-manager-ward, home rule
charter, special charter, city-county consolidated and community commonwealth. A
new city starts as mayor-council and keeps that form until its voters adopt
another. Which form each city uses is not in our data.

*Iowa Code §§ 372.1, 372.2 · statutory · read in statute*

What each form elects by default:

- **Mayor-council:** a mayor and five council members at large. A city of up to
  5,000 people may cut the council to three.
- **Commission:** a mayor and four council members at large.
- **Council-manager-at-large:** five council members on staggered four-year
  terms, who choose one of themselves as mayor. The city may switch to a mayor
  elected by the voters.
- **Council-manager-ward:** a mayor and six council members, by ward or a mix of
  ward and at large, on staggered four-year terms.
- **Home rule charter:** an odd-numbered council of at least five, a mayor, and
  two-year or staggered four-year terms, as the charter sets.

*Iowa Code §§ 372.4, 372.5, 372.6, 372.7, 372.10 · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** do not name an Iowa city's form of government, and do not write
that a city elects its mayor. In our data 923 of 939 city pages carry a mayor
race. The 16 that do not include Iowa City and Burlington. Say the city has a
mayor and a council, and let race data say whether the mayor is on the ballot.

### Terms and council size

City terms are **two years** unless state law or the charter says otherwise. The
council-manager forms set four-year terms, and voters in any city may change a
term to two or four years by petition and election. Voters may also change
whether council members are elected at large, by ward, or a mix.

*Iowa Code §§ 376.2(2), 372.13(11) · usual case · read in statute*

Do not state an Iowa city's term length or council size. Take them from race
data.

### Home rule

The constitution gives cities home rule only where it is **not inconsistent with
state law**. Election timing, nomination and ballot rules are set in state law
for every city, so a home rule charter cannot change them. A charter can set
council size (odd, at least five), terms and the representation plan.

*Iowa Const. art. III, § 38A; Iowa Code §§ 376.1, 376.3, 372.10 · statutory · read in statute*

## Metro and major-place exceptions

Iowa has fewer of these than most states, because there is one municipal class
and every county has the same kind of board. The exceptions are about form and
about size.

### Davenport

`ia/scott-county/davenport`

The large city most likely covered by the special charter rule above, the only
case where state law still allows partisan city elections. Its 2017 city ballot
shows a mayor and **aldermen**, elected by ward and at large, with no party
labels.

*Iowa Code § 376.3; Scott County sample ballot, City of Davenport, 2017 · varies by place · local government publication*

Avoid calling Davenport's races partisan or nonpartisan unless race data shows
which. In copy, "alderman" is the local word for a council member.

### Johnson, Story and Black Hawk counties

`ia/johnson-county`, `ia/story-county`, `ia/black-hawk-county`

The three state university counties. Supervisors must be elected from
single-member districts here. Safe to say on these pages.

*Iowa Code § 331.206(2)(a) · statutory · read in statute*

### Iowa City and other large cities

`ia/johnson-county/iowa-city`, `ia/polk-county/des-moines`,
`ia/linn-county/cedar-rapids`, `ia/woodbury-county/sioux-city`

Large cities are more likely to use a council-manager form, so the mayor may be
chosen by the council, terms may be four years, and seats may mix wards and at
large. Iowa City has no mayor race in our data. Lean hardest on race data on
these pages.

## Writing rules

These are the ways Iowa copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never infer a county from a place name.** 17 Iowa city pages are named
   after a county they are not in, for example Des Moines (Polk County),
   Jefferson (Greene County), Greene (Butler County), Keokuk (Lee County) and
   Webster (Keokuk County). Always take the county from the page data.
2. **Use the right year for the tier.** Cities, school boards and community
   college boards: odd years. County offices, townships and the county-level
   boards: even years.
3. **County races are partisan. City, township and school races are not.** The
   one hedge is Davenport.
4. **Keep townships and cities apart.** Iowa law says a township is not a city.
   Keep "Township" in the name and never merge a city with the township of the
   same name.
5. **Do not name a city's form of government** and do not say a city elects its
   mayor. Say it has a mayor and a council.
6. Do not state a city term length, council size or supervisor count.
7. Do not put a city clerk on an Iowa ballot. The council appoints one.
8. Do not mention runoffs or majority rules for a city unless race data shows a
   runoff. Never mention ranked choice voting as an option: it is banned.
9. Do not call an area education agency board elected.
10. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
11. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
    contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures.

### What search results commonly get wrong

- Iowa is often called an "open primary" state. A voter can switch parties at
  the polls, but the switch is recorded and stays.
- Summaries often mention the Iowa caucuses. They choose party delegates and
  committee members, not local officeholders. Local nominations come from the
  June primary or by petition.
- Older material describes ranked choice voting as an option for Iowa cities. It
  has been banned since 1 January 2026.
- Townships are sometimes left out of Iowa local government entirely. They still
  elect trustees and a clerk in most of the state.
