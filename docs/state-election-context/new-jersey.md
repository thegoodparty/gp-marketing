# New Jersey: state election context

Starting point for the location page editorial generator. This gives you the
structure that holds across New Jersey so you do not have to research the same
ground on every page. It is not an authority: per-page specifics, like which
offices are actually on this ballot and how many seats, come from our own race
data, which always beats anything inferred from here.

Source lines read: `citation · how universal · how verified`. See the
[README](./README.md) for what those mean. Every claim below has a verbatim quote
in [`research/new-jersey.json`](./research/new-jersey.json).

**The one thing to know first:** in New Jersey the name of a municipality
(township, borough, city, town, village) does not tell you how it votes. Most
municipalities elect their governing body in November, with party labels and
June party primaries. But a large group has adopted a charter or the commission
form and elects **without party labels**, usually on the second Tuesday in
**May**, sometimes in November. School boards are always nonpartisan and vote in
April or November. Copy that says "New Jersey local elections are partisan" or
"are held in November" is wrong for hundreds of places.

## Legislative calendar

The Legislature meets year-round. Each Legislature lasts two years, starting at
noon on the second Tuesday in January of an even-numbered year, and each
legislative year starts on the second Tuesday in January. The current
Legislature's second year begins on 12 January 2027, and bills pending at the end
of its first year carry over. The next new Legislature begins on 11 January 2028.

*N.J. Const. art. IV, § 1, ¶ 3 · statutory · read in statute*

The Governor has until noon of the 45th day after a bill is presented to act on
it. A bill passed in the last 10 days of a two-year Legislature becomes law only
if the Governor signs it by noon of the seventh day after the Legislature
expires, so bills from the January 2028 "lame duck" weeks are settled by about
the third week of January 2028.

*N.J. Const. art. V, § 1, ¶ 14(c) · statutory · read in statute*

Each new law states its own effective date. We found no general default, so read
the effective-date section of any new election or local-government law.

Re-check this doc: January 2027 and July 2027 (full-time Legislature), then
February 2028, after the two-year Legislature ends and its last bills are
signed. Last researched: October 2026.

## How elections work in New Jersey

Safe on any New Jersey page, at the tier named.

### Election days

**November.** The general election is the Tuesday after the first Monday in
November, every year. County offices are filled here, and so are the governing
bodies of most townships, boroughs, cities and towns.

*N.J.S.A. 19:2-3; 40A:60-2, 40A:61-2, 40A:62-2, 40A:63-2 · statutory · read in statute*

**June.** The primary for the general election is the Tuesday after the first
Monday in June, for all parties. Every office on the November ballot is filled by
a party nominee from this primary or by a candidate nominated by petition.

*N.J.S.A. 19:23-40, 19:13-1 · statutory · read in statute*

**May.** Municipalities that elect without party labels (Faulkner Act charters,
the commission form and similar) hold their regular municipal election on the
second Tuesday in May, unless they have moved it to November (see below).

*N.J.S.A. 40:45-6, 40:45-7, 40:69A-150 · usual case · read in statute*

**April.** Most elected school boards vote on the third Tuesday in April unless
the district has moved its election to November (see school districts below).

*N.J.S.A. 19:60-1 · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** the Secretary of State can move a May municipal election, and the
Commissioner of Education can move an April school election, when it falls on a
religious observance. Publish the rule ("the second Tuesday in May"), never a
calendar date.

*N.J.S.A. 40:45-7, 19:60-1 · statutory · read in statute*

### Can a place move its own election date?

Yes, in specific ways set by state law, each with a lock:

- A municipality with May nonpartisan elections may move them to the November
  general election by ordinance. It cannot move back to May until at least 10
  years have passed. The election stays nonpartisan.
- A Faulkner Act municipality can amend its charter by referendum to switch
  between May municipal elections and November general elections, which also
  switches it between nonpartisan and partisan elections.
- A school district can move its April election to November by resolution of the
  school board or the municipal governing body, with no public vote. It can move
  back to April only after at least four November school elections.
- A fire district can move its election from February to November by resolution,
  and then cannot move it back.

*N.J.S.A. 40:45-7.1, 40:69A-25.1, 40:69A-150, 19:60-1.1, 40A:14-72.1 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** because of these switches, never say which month a specific
town or school district votes unless race data shows it.

### School and special districts

**School districts** are Type I or Type II. Districts in cities start out as
Type I, where the mayor appoints the school board to five-year terms, though
voters can switch to an elected board by referendum. Elsewhere, and in every
regional district, the district is Type II and voters elect the board.

*N.J.S.A. 18A:9-1, 18A:9-2, 18A:9-3, 18A:12-17, 18A:12-18 · usual case · read in statute*

An elected board has nine members by default (or three, five or seven) serving
three-year terms. School board ballots carry **no party label**. A candidate may
print a slogan of up to three words, but it cannot use a party's name.

*N.J.S.A. 18A:12-11, 19:60-1(b) · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** an April school election may also carry a vote on extra school
funding. Do not describe the April election as only a school board race.

**Fire districts** elect a five-member board of fire commissioners to three-year
terms. The annual election is the third Saturday in February, or the November
general election if the district has moved it. Candidates file nominating
petitions, not party primaries.

*N.J.S.A. 40A:14-70, 40A:14-71, 40A:14-72 · statutory · read in statute*

### Primary type

New Jersey's party primaries are **for party members**, with an opening for
unaffiliated voters:

- A voter who votes in a party's primary, or names a party when registering,
  becomes a member of that party until they file a change.
- A party member who wants to vote in a different party's primary must switch at
  least 55 days before the primary.
- New voters, and voters who have never voted in a primary, are exempt from the
  55-day rule. This is how an unaffiliated voter can pick a party at the polls.
- At the polls, each voter names the party primary they want to vote in.
- A voter can declare themselves unaffiliated at any time.

*N.J.S.A. 19:23-45, 19:23-46 · statutory · read in statute*

What it commits the voter to: voting in a party's primary **enrolls them in
that party**. They stay a member, and are locked out of other parties' primaries
for at least 55 days after any switch, until they file a change.

What it commits a candidate to: a candidate who runs in a party primary for a
municipal office cannot be another party's candidate for that office in the
November that follows, and a petition cannot nominate someone who has accepted a
primary nomination for the same office.

*N.J.S.A. 19:13-14.1, 19:13-4 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** search results call New Jersey's primary "closed" or
"semi-closed". Avoid both labels. Say that primaries are for party members, that
unaffiliated voters can join a party at the polls, and that doing so makes them a
member of that party.

### Partisanship by type of government

| Township, borough, city, town under their own general laws | Yes, November ballot after June primary | N.J.S.A. 40A:60-2, 40A:61-2, 40A:62-2, 40A:63-2, 19:13-1 · usual case |

*All read in statute.*

In nonpartisan municipal races, candidates may print a slogan of up to six words
next to their names, and it may not show a party affiliation. Candidates running
as a group can share one slogan, so a ballot may look like a slate.

*N.J.S.A. 40:45-10 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** never write "New Jersey municipal elections are partisan", or
"nonpartisan". Both are wrong for hundreds of places. A township can be
nonpartisan (if it has a charter) and a city can be partisan. Let race data
decide: a May race, or a race with no party label, is a nonpartisan
municipality.

### Plurality or majority

Plurality is the default: whoever gets the most votes wins, with no runoff. This
holds for party primaries, the general election, township committees and default
nonpartisan elections.

*N.J.S.A. 19:3-4, 19:13-14, 40A:63-2, 40:45-17 · statutory · read in statute*

The exception: a nonpartisan municipality can adopt runoffs by referendum (and
abandon them the same way). There, a mayor or ward council candidate needs a
majority, and a runoff follows on the fourth Tuesday after a May election, or in
December after a November one.

*N.J.S.A. 40:45-18, 40:45-19, 40:45-21 · varies by place · read in statute*

### Independent candidates

**November races** (county offices, and municipal races in partisan towns): an
independent files a direct nominating petition with the county clerk. The
petition may carry a designation of up to three words, which cannot use the name
of a party that holds primaries.

*N.J.S.A. 19:13-3, 19:13-4 · statutory · read in statute*

Who is barred from signing: each signer certifies they have not signed any other
nominating petition, for the primary or the general election, for that office. A
voter may sign only one petition per office. In practice, someone who signed a
party candidate's primary petition for an office cannot sign an independent's
petition for the same office.

*N.J.S.A. 19:13-4, 19:13-6 · statutory · read in statute*

**Nonpartisan municipal races** have no independent route because every
candidate files the same way: a nominating petition with the municipal clerk. A
voter may not sign more petitions for an office than there are seats to fill.

*N.J.S.A. 40:45-8 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** New Jersey calls voters without a party "unaffiliated". Use
"independent" for the candidate, not for a party or a voter registration status.

## County government

21 counties (our page count). Every county is governed by a **board of county
commissioners**, which holds the county's executive and legislative powers.

*N.J.S.A. 40:20-1 · statutory · read in statute*

**Watch out:** until 2021 these officials were called **freeholders**, and the
board was the "board of chosen freeholders". A 2020 law (P.L.2020, c.67) renamed
them county commissioners. Many statutes and old web pages still say
"freeholder". Never use it in copy except to explain the old name.

*N.J.S.A. 40:41A-1(b), 40:20-1 · statutory · read in statute*

The board has three, five, seven or nine members, as county voters decide by
referendum. Members serve three-year terms unless voters have switched to
four-year staggered terms. They are elected at the November general election with
party labels.

*N.J.S.A. 40:20-20, 40:20-20.4, 19:14-10 · usual case · read in statute*

Every county elects a **county clerk** and a **surrogate** (five-year terms) and a
**sheriff** (three-year term) at the general election. Some counties also elect a
**register of deeds and mortgages** (our data has this race in Essex and Hudson).

*N.J. Const. art. VII, § 2, ¶ 2; N.J.S.A. 19:14-10 · statutory · read in statute*

### County charters

Counties may adopt an optional charter, starting with a voter-approved charter
study. Under the **county executive plan**, voters also elect a county executive
to a four-year term. Our data has a county executive race in five counties:
Atlantic, Bergen, Essex, Hudson and Mercer.

*N.J.S.A. 40:41A-1, 40:41A-31, 40:41A-33 · statutory · read in statute*

A charter does **not** reach elections: charter counties stay bound by general
law on elections. Their races are still partisan, on the November ballot.

*N.J.S.A. 40:41A-26 · statutory · read in statute*

### No townships inside counties as a separate layer

A New Jersey township is one of the five kinds of **municipality**, on the same
footing as a city or a borough, with its own township committee. Do not describe
it as a layer of government above other towns, the way townships work in
Pennsylvania, Illinois or Michigan.

*N.J.S.A. 40:55D-5, 40A:63-2 · statutory · read in statute*

## Municipal government

New Jersey has five kinds of municipality: **city, borough, town, township and
village**. Each type has a default form of government in Title 40A. Many
municipalities have since adopted a different form (see Faulkner Act and
commission form below), and the type name stays the same when they do.

*N.J.S.A. 40:55D-5; 40A:60-2, 40A:61-2, 40A:62-2, 40A:63-2, 40A:63-8 · statutory · read in statute*

### Borough (default form)

Elects a **mayor** at large for four years and a **council of six** at large for
three-year terms, two seats a year. Elections are every November.

*N.J.S.A. 40A:60-2 · usual case · read in statute*

### Township (default form)

Elects a **township committee** of three or five members at large for three-year
terms, every November. The committee chooses one of its own members each year as
**mayor**, who chairs the committee. Voters do not elect the mayor directly
under this form.

*N.J.S.A. 40A:63-2, 40A:63-3 · usual case · read in statute*

**Watch out:** in a township under the default form, do not write "the mayor's
race". There is none. The committee seats are the race.

### City (default form)

Elects a **mayor** for four years and a **council of seven**: six from two wards
for staggered three-year terms and one at large. Elections are every November.
Cities whose council or mayoral term differed before 1987 keep their old
structure until voters adopt the new one.

*N.J.S.A. 40A:61-2 · varies by place · read in statute*

### Town (default form)

Elects a **mayor** for four years, who is also called the councilman-at-large,
and a **council of eight**, two from each of four wards, for four-year terms.
Elections are in November.

*N.J.S.A. 40A:62-2 · usual case · read in statute*

### Village

A village runs under the township law, with a **board of trustees** of five
members elected at large for three-year terms and a **president of the board**
in place of a mayor.

*N.J.S.A. 40A:63-8 · usual case · read in statute*

### Faulkner Act charters

Any municipality, of any type, can adopt one of the Optional Municipal Charter
Law (Faulkner Act) plans by referendum:

- **Mayor-council**: a directly elected mayor (four years) and a council of five
  unless the charter says otherwise (four-year terms).
- **Council-manager**: an elected council and an appointed manager. The charter
  says whether the council or the voters choose the mayor.
- **Small municipality**: only for places under 12,000 people. A mayor and a
  council elected at large for three-year terms.
- **Mayor-council-administrator**: an elected mayor and council and an appointed
  administrator. This plan's elections are at the November general election, so
  they are partisan.

*N.J.S.A. 40:69A-31, 40:69A-33, 40:69A-34, 40:69A-81, 40:69A-82, 40:69A-86, 40:69A-115, 40:69A-117, 40:69A-149.1, 40:69A-149.2, 40:69A-149.3 · varies by place · read in statute*

Within its plan, a Faulkner Act municipality can change by referendum when it
votes (May or November), whether council members are elected at large or by ward,
whether terms are staggered, how the mayor is chosen, and council size (three,
five, seven or nine). After adopting a plan, voters cannot vote on another form of
government for 10 years.

*N.J.S.A. 40:69A-25.1, 40:69A-23 · varies by place · read in statute*

### Commission form (Walsh Act)

Elects **three commissioners** in places under 12,000 people and **five** at
12,000 or more. The commissioners choose one of themselves to preside, with the
title of mayor. Elections are nonpartisan, in May unless moved to November.

*N.J.S.A. 40:72-1, 40:72-10, 40:45-6 · usual case · read in statute*

### Class does not follow population

Cities and counties fall into population classes "for legislative purposes", and
a city that grows into another class comes under that class's laws. But that
class is about which laws apply, not what the place is. We found nothing in
state law that turns a township or borough into a city as it grows. Changing the
form of government takes a majority vote of the municipality's voters. Never
work out a place's type from its population. Take it from our data.

*N.J.S.A. 40A:6-1, 40A:6-4, 40A:6-7, 40:69A-22 · usual case · read in statute*

### Home rule

Home rule in New Jersey does not come from a population threshold or from
adopting a home rule charter. It rests on the Constitution, which tells courts to
read laws on counties and municipalities liberally in their favor, and on the
Faulkner Act is meant to give "the greatest power of local self-government" the
Constitution allows. A municipality or county can also ask the Legislature for a
special law of its own, which takes effect only if adopted locally.

*N.J. Const. art. IV, § 7, ¶¶ 10-11; N.J.S.A. 40:69A-30 · statutory · read in statute*

Home rule does **not** free a place to set its own election rules. The
Uniform Nonpartisan Elections Law governs the time, manner and method of
nonpartisan municipal elections, and the choices it allows (May or November,
runoffs or none) are the ones listed above.

*N.J.S.A. 40:45-6, 40:45-7.1, 40:45-21 · statutory · read in statute*

### Changing terms and council size

All by local referendum, within limits set by state law:

- County voters can set the board of county commissioners at three, five, seven
  or nine, and move to four-year terms.
- Township voters can set the committee at three or five.
- Faulkner Act voters can change council size, wards, staggering and how the
  mayor is chosen.

*N.J.S.A. 40:20-20, 40:20-20.4, 40A:63-3, 40:69A-25.1 · varies by place · read in statute*

So do not state a term length or seat count as fixed for a specific place.

## Metro and major-place exceptions

New Jersey has 1,158 location pages: 21 county pages, 509 school district pages
and 627 municipal pages (our 27 October 2026 snapshot). Seven municipal pages
share a name with a county, and all seven sit in that county (Camden, Passaic,
Salem, Sussex, Middlesex, Burlington, Cape May). Still take the county from the
page data, never from the name.

### Large cities

`nj/essex-county/newark`, `nj/hudson-county/jersey-city`,
`nj/passaic-county/paterson`, `nj/union-county/elizabeth`,
`nj/mercer-county/trenton`, `nj/camden-county/camden`

Do not assume the default city form (a seven-member council from two wards) for
any large city. A large city may have adopted a Faulkner Act charter or another
form, and may vote in May or November, with or without party labels. Describe
what race data shows. School boards in these cities may be appointed (Type I) or
elected: check race data before mentioning a school board race.

*N.J.S.A. 40A:61-2, 40:69A-150, 18A:9-2, 18A:12-17 · varies by place · read in statute*

### Big townships

`nj/ocean-county/lakewood-township`, `nj/middlesex-county/edison-township`,
`nj/middlesex-county/woodbridge-township`, `nj/ocean-county/toms-river-township`,
`nj/mercer-county/hamilton-township`

Several of the state's largest municipalities are townships. Never call them a
village or a rural government, and never assume the township committee form:
where race data shows a directly elected mayor, the township has adopted another

### Charter counties

`nj/atlantic-county`, `nj/bergen-county`, `nj/essex-county`,
`nj/hudson-county`, `nj/mercer-county`

These have a county executive race in our data, in addition to the board of
county commissioners. The executive serves four years.

*N.J.S.A. 40:41A-33 · statutory · read in statute*

## Data issues

Note these, do not write copy around them:

- **Most of the 87 same-name groups are one government, not two.** In a typical
  pair, the plain page holds the council and the `-borough` or `-city` page holds
  only the school board of the same municipality, for example
  `nj/passaic-county/ringwood` (council) and `nj/passaic-county/ringwood-borough`
  (school board only). 70 municipal pages hold nothing but a school board.
- **Some pairs are real.** Two municipalities of the same name sit in the same
  county in places such as Chatham, Chester, Rockaway, Mendham, Clinton, Boonton,
  Lebanon, Freehold, Shrewsbury, Pemberton, Bordentown, Berlin and Washington
  (Warren County). The `-township` page is the township.
- **Suspected duplicates or misfiled pages.** These plain pages carry a city-style
  mayor and council beside a `-township` page, in counties that we believe have
  only the township: `nj/gloucester-county/washington`,
  `nj/somerset-county/franklin`, `nj/sussex-county/hampton`,
  `nj/cumberland-county/hopewell`, `nj/monmouth-county/millstone`.
  `nj/hunterdon-county/raritan` may be Raritan borough, which we believe is in
  Somerset County. These need checking against the source data before any copy
  describes them.
- `nj/burlington-county/burlington` lists an "island manager" office.
- `nj/bergen-county/edgewater-borough` holds only a fire board.
- `nj/essex-county/south-orange-village-township` is the only slug containing
  "village". It is a township with "Village" in its name.

### Our labels

Borough, city and town councils carry `city-legislature` and
`city-executive-mayor` labels. Township committees carry
`township-trustee-township-council`, and the committee-chosen mayor carries
`township-mayor`. New Jersey has no township trustees. Use the office names the
law uses: council, township committee, board of county commissioners.

## Writing rules

These are the ways New Jersey copy goes visibly wrong.

1. **Never call county commissioners "freeholders"**, except to explain the old
   name.
2. **Never say New Jersey local elections are partisan, or nonpartisan, as a
   rule.** County races are partisan, school boards are nonpartisan, and
   municipalities vary with their form of government, not their type.
3. **Never put every municipal race in November.** Nonpartisan municipalities
   usually vote in May, and school boards in April or November.
4. **Never work out a place's form of government from its name or size.** A
   township can have a directly elected mayor and May elections. A city can
   have a township-style ballot. Take structure from race data.
5. **Never describe a township as a layer above other towns.** In New Jersey it
   is a municipality like a borough.
6. **Decide what a page is from its offices, not its slug.** A `-borough` page
   next to a plain-named page is usually the same borough's school board.
7. **Do not write "the mayor's race" for a township committee town.** The
   committee picks the mayor.
8. Do not call the primary "closed" or "open". Say primaries are for party
   members and unaffiliated voters can join a party at the polls.
9. Do not state a term length or seat count as fixed for a place.
10. Never name an officeholder or a candidate.
11. Avoid anything that changes within a year: who has filed, whether a race is
    contested, filing deadlines, current ballot measures, which month a district
    has chosen.

### What search results commonly get wrong

- "Freeholders" is still everywhere online. The title changed in 2021.
- Many sources call every New Jersey local election partisan, which misses the
  May nonpartisan towns.
- Sources from other states describe townships as rural sub-county units. Not
  here.
- "Independent" is often used for unaffiliated voters. New Jersey's word is
  "unaffiliated".
